Your First Share Purchase

How to decide which NEPSE shares to buy, how much, and when you would sell — in eight slow steps, with a worked example and a printable checklist.

How to use this guide

This guide takes you, one step at a time, from "I have some savings" to a written decision on which NEPSE shares to buy, how much, and when you would sell. It follows the method of The Investor's Canon and its Canon Score. It is written for a first-time investor in Nepal. Every technical word is explained the first time it appears, and again in Words to know near the end.

The big picture. Buying a share is like buying a buffalo at a haat bazaar. A careful farmer does not buy the first healthy-looking animal. First he checks that he has fodder at home. Then he checks the season. Then he looks at the animal, asks about its owner, checks its milk, agrees a fair price, and decides how many he can feed. The eight steps below follow the same order.

Two kinds of rule. This guide mixes two things, and you should always know which is which. A fact is a rule or figure from a regulator or the exchange (NRB, SEBON, the Nepal Insurance Authority, NEPSE, CDSC), given with its date. A Canon rule is this book's own method, set in Chapter 64 (rubric version 1.6.0) and Chapter 66: a reasoned choice, not a law, and not yet proven to give better returns. The exact numbers in it (15 points for governance, 55 to qualify for new money and so on) are design choices, not thresholds anyone has proven to be the best. Canon rules are marked (Canon rule) where it matters.

How to read it. Go in order the first time. Each step uses ideas from the steps before it. Each step ends with a short check box: do not move on until you can tick it.

Four possible answers. This guide is not here to make you buy. Your first purchase is not a test of courage; it is a test of whether you can decide from evidence rather than excitement. Every company you study ends in one of four answers:

Answer When What you do next
Buy Every gate is passed, including the three purchase checks in Step 8: the price is at least 35% below your own value estimate, your order passes the liquidity rule, and the season allows buying Buy the amount from Step 8, and no more
Wait The business is acceptable, but the price or the season is not Write down the price or event that would change your answer
Reject It fails a gate that nothing else can make up for Remove it; look again only if the problem is cleared
Not enough evidence You cannot find or check the figures you need: more than 20 points unverified (grade D), a gate you cannot test, or no value estimate you can defend Do not buy. Missing data is not proof of a bad company, but it is not permission to invest either. (Chapter 66 would let an experienced investor hold a grade D score at a ceiling one band lower; for a first purchase this guide is stricter.)

Deciding not to buy is a valid investment decision. A farmer who leaves a field fallow for a season has not failed.

Gates, not averages. Some checks are gates: failing one ends the study, however good the rest looks. Strong profits cannot make up for a governance hard rule, and a cheap price cannot make up for a share you cannot sell. The gates are Step 1 (are you ready?), Step 3 (enough turnover, no circuit trap, no red flag), Steps 4 and 5 (the hard rules that cap the score at 54) and Step 7 (a total of 55 or more, after any cap). Step 8 adds three purchase checks from Chapter 66 that the score cannot replace: a price check (failing it means Wait), your own liquidity check, and the season check. Only a company that passes them all is bought, and Step 8 decides how much. A failed gate means Reject; a gate you cannot test means Not enough evidence. They are different answers.

Our example investor. We follow Ramesh, a teacher in Pokhara, through every step. He has saved Rs 2 lakh from his salary and his brother's remittance. Ramesh is a made-up teaching character. The companies he studies (Bank A, Hydro D and so on) are also made up, so nothing here is a tip on a real share.

Step 1 — Am I ready to buy shares at all?

You are ready only when the money going into shares is money you can leave invested for at least five years, and preferably longer. Five years is a planning minimum, not a promise: shares can stay below what you paid for longer than that. This step comes before any company, because the wrong money in the market hurts you even when you pick a good share.

Why it matters. NEPSE can fall hard. It fell about 43% from 3,198.60 (18 August 2021) to 1,815.13 (25 September 2022). If you needed your money during a fall like that, you would have to sell at the worst price. A farmer never eats his seed rice, even in a hungry month. Your share money is your seed rice.

The readiness checklist

  1. Emergency money set aside. Keep about six months of household costs in a bank account or fixed deposit that you do not touch. Six months is a starting point: keep more if your income is irregular (a business, remittance-dependent, seasonal work) or if many people depend on you. It covers a hospital bill, a lost job or a family emergency, so you never have to sell shares in a hurry. If you keep it in a fixed deposit, check the penalty for breaking it early, and keep a month or two of it in a savings account you can reach the same day.
  2. Costly loans paid off first. Paying off a loan earns you its interest rate, guaranteed. This guide's line is simple: clear any loan costing about 12% a year or more before you buy shares. At 15–18% (a credit card, a moneylender, a costly personal loan) the choice is obvious. At 12–14% it is still usually wiser, because a sure 12% saving beats an uncertain share return; no share can promise a fixed return. A cheaper loan, such as a home loan well below 12%, need not stop you, as long as your income covers its instalments safely. (This 12% line is this guide's suggestion, not a Chapter 64 rule.)
  3. Five-year money only. Money for next year's wedding, a child's admission fees, a land payment or a trip abroad is not share money.
  4. No borrowed money as a beginner. Do not buy shares with a margin loan (a loan from a bank or broker secured on your shares). In 2021–22, margin loans forced many Nepali investors to sell at the lowest prices, because the lender sells your shares when they fall too far.

If any of these four is "no", stop and fix it first. The market will still be there.

Open three accounts. They link together like three rooms of one house:

Account What it does Where you open it
Bank account Money comes in and goes out here Any bank
Demat account Keeps your shares electronically; you see it in MeroShare A depository participant (most banks and brokers act as one)
Broker account (TMS) Where you place buy and sell orders online A licensed NEPSE broker

After you buy, shares and money change hands two working days later: CDSC's settlement procedure pays out securities and funds on T+2 (trade day plus two). You will normally see the shares in MeroShare after that settlement; ask your broker if they have not appeared, and do not plan to sell shares before they are credited to you.

Split your share money into two parts. This idea comes from the book's Lesson 59.7.

  • The steady part. A fixed amount every month into a diversified mutual fund, through a monthly SIP (systematic investment plan). It never stops, whatever the market does. Think of it as the kitchen garden behind the house: you plant it every season, rain or no rain.
  • The active part. Individual shares you choose yourself, using Steps 2–8. These are the rented fields: how many you rent depends on the season.

While you are still learning, keep at least half of your share money in the steady part (Canon rule, Lesson 59.7). That rule assumes a suitable fund exists: one spread across several sectors, with fees you understand and a clear way out. If you cannot find one, keep the steady part in fixed deposits or government savings bonds until you do, rather than in a fund that holds the same few banks you would buy yourself (this guide's suggestion). As your skill and record grow over several years, you can move some toward the active part, but never let the steady part disappear.

Choosing the fund. A monthly SIP is a way of paying in, not a guarantee of safety. Before you choose a fund, look at what it holds (how much in banks, how much in hydropower), its yearly fees, how much it overlaps with shares you will buy yourself, and how you get your money out: an open-ended fund buys units back from you, while a closed-end fund must be sold on NEPSE, often below its NAV (Chapter 16).

Step 2 — What season is the market in?

Step 2 tells you how much of your active part to use right now, not which share to buy. A farmer in Chitwan plants her kitchen garden every year, but decides how many extra fields to rent only after she sees how the monsoon is shaping up.

Why the season matters in Nepal. NEPSE moves strongly with bank money. When banks have spare money and lend easily, share prices tend to rise. When money gets tight and interest rates climb, they tend to fall. The link is strong but neither automatic nor instant: remittances, government spending, company profits, new share issues and politics matter too.

The four seasons (phases). Chapter 0.3 names four phases of Nepal's money cycle. Lesson 59.7 turns each into a setting for your active part (Canon rule). Your steady part (the monthly mutual fund) keeps buying in every phase.

Phase What it feels like Active part New share buying Margin loans
1. Expansion Banks lend freely; money is easy Up to 100%, never more Yes, at normal sizes (Step 8) Never raised in a rally
2. Compression Banks run short of money; rates rise Cut to 50%, selling the weakest scores first None Repay them
3. Repair Rates high, market beaten down Rebuild from 50% toward 75% Only scores of 70+ at low prices None
4. Recovery Money starts flowing again Rebuild toward 100% in half-steps Yes, at normal sizes Only after Phase 1 is confirmed, and never as a beginner (Step 1)

Money taken out of the active part in Phase 2 goes into fixed deposits or treasury bills, not into land and not into a relative's business. Compression phases are often when fixed deposits pay more, but not always: compare the actual rates, tax and lock-in on offer.

How to read the season

  1. Once a quarter, open Nepal Rastra Bank's monthly report on the economy (the "current macroeconomic and financial situation").
  2. Apply Chapter 0.3's fixed decision rule (section 0.3.7, "The Decision Rule"): three main signals, checked in a set order, about bank lending, the spare money in banks, and interest rates, with the other figures used as background, never as votes. That lesson gives the exact NRB figures to use and what to do when signals disagree; this guide only summarises it.
  3. Change your setting only when two quarterly checks in a row agree on a new phase. One reading can mislead. One rainy day in Baisakh does not mean the monsoon has come. The price of this caution is delay: by the time two checks agree, prices may already have moved. That is accepted on purpose. Acting a quarter late on a true signal usually costs less than acting at once on a false one.
  4. Write the phase and your reason in your notebook before you place any order.

A real example of why this works. In late 2021, banks' lending pressed against NRB's limit and interbank rates ran hot: Phase 2 signals. An investor following this table would have stopped buying individual shares, cut the active part toward half and repaid any margin loan, while the monthly mutual fund kept buying cheaper units through the fall.

Ramesh's Step 2. He reads NRB's latest report with the Chapter 0.3 rule. If his last two checks both say Phase 1, he may use his full Rs 1 lakh active part over time. If they say Phase 2, he buys no new shares at all, and the money stays in a fixed deposit. In this example his last two checks both say Phase 1.

STEP 2 CHECK You can write: "The phase is ___ (two checks agree). I may put up to Rs ___ into individual shares right now."

Step 3 — Make a shortlist

From about 300 listed companies, cut the list down to 8–12 worth studying properly. At this stage you mostly remove: like skipping the stalls with rotten vegetables before you compare prices.

Filter 1 — Stay with businesses you understand. You should be able to say how the company earns money in one simple sentence.

Sector One-sentence test
Commercial bank It takes deposits at a low rate and lends at a higher rate.
Hydropower It sells electricity to NEA at a fixed price under a long contract.
Microfinance It gives small loans to rural borrowers, often women in groups.
Life insurer It collects premiums now and pays claims years later.

If you cannot say the sentence, leave that sector for later. It is not banned, just not for now.

Filter 2 — Can you sell it when you need to? This is the most important filter in Nepal. A share that is easy to buy in a rally can be very hard to sell in a fall, because buyers disappear. It is like a road that is busy on festival days and empty when you need a ride.

Check the average daily turnover: the rupee value of the share traded on a normal day, over the last six months (from NEPSE's website or a market data site). Chapter 64 grades it like this (Canon rule; the rupee lines suit a small investor and will need reviewing as the market grows):

Average daily turnover (6 months) Meaning Score (of 5)
Rs 50 lakh or more Easy to trade 5
Rs 10 lakh to under 50 lakh Fair 3
Rs 2 lakh to under 10 lakh Thin; be careful 1
Under Rs 2 lakh Very thin 0 — remove from the shortlist

How to measure it (Canon rule, Chapter 64):

  • Add up the value traded in the ordinary market over six months and divide by the number of NEPSE trading days, counting the days the share did not trade at all.
  • Leave out negotiated and block deals where you can identify them: an ordinary investor could not have traded at those sizes and prices.
  • Thin-trading rule: if the share traded on fewer than half of those days, score turnover at no more than 1, whatever the average says.
  • Block-trade check: if the average is more than twice the median day, a few big days are doing the work. Score from the median instead and write "block-trade adjusted". Think of a village shop that sells a whole year's rice to one wedding party: one big day does not make it a busy shop.

The six-month figure is for the score, which tells you whether a company can be traded at all. Whether your own order can get out is a separate question: use the shorter and harsher numbers below, the 20-day figure, the median day and a stressed week.

How much you could hold. Chapter 56's rule: never hold more of a share than you could sell in 5 trading days while being only 10% of each day's trading (using the 20-day average). In simple maths, your maximum is about half of one day's average turnover. A share trading Rs 20 lakh a day allows about Rs 10 lakh; a share trading Rs 1 lakh a day allows only about Rs 50,000.

Then test a bad week. Average turnover is what happens on normal days. In a falling market, buyers thin out. A simple stress test: exit days = your position ÷ (stressed daily turnover × 10%). Try several stresses (illustrations, not book rules; Chapter 58 models this properly). For a Rs 2 lakh position in a share that normally trades Rs 20 lakh a day:

Situation Daily turnover Your 10% share of it Days to sell Rs 2 lakh
Normal day Rs 20 lakh Rs 2 lakh 1
Turnover halves Rs 10 lakh Rs 1 lakh 2
Turnover falls by three-quarters Rs 5 lakh Rs 50,000 4
Lower circuit, almost no buyers Close to zero Close to zero Cannot be counted: you wait until buyers return, at whatever price they offer

If the stressed answer is longer than you could bear to wait in a falling market, hold less. No turnover formula guarantees an exit. On lower-circuit days, sell orders queue at the limit price and many are never filled; several such days in a row can trap a position completely (Chapter 58). A good average is a screen, not a promise. Two more habits help: look at the median day as well as the average (a few frantic days can inflate an average), and look at how the share traded on the market's worst days. On the day you trade, also look at the spread (the gap between the best buying and selling prices) and how many shares are waiting in the order book: a wide gap or a thin queue means your own sell order will push the price down.

Filter 3 — Skip circuit traps. Since 20 April 2026, an ordinary share can move at most 15% from the previous day's close. That is a limit on each share. Separately, NEPSE has market-wide circuit breakers tied to the index: a 5% move in the first two hours pauses all trading for 15 minutes, and an 8% move stops trading for the rest of the day (Chapters 8 and 10). Both can stop you selling when you most want to. A circuit trap is a share that keeps hitting that limit while very few shares change hands. A fast rise on its own is not a trap: prices can jump for real reasons, such as good results. The warning is the combination: limit hits day after day on tiny turnover, a small free float, and a rise driven by a rumour or a social-media "tip" rather than results. When it turns, it can hit the lower limit day after day and you cannot sell at all.

Filter 4 — Remove obvious red flags. A quick look at company notices and news is enough:

  • more than half of the promoters' shares are pledged as loan collateral, or a lender is already selling pledged shares (smaller pledges are scored in Step 4, not removed here);
  • the auditor refused to give an opinion, or there are fraud or "problematic institution" notices;
  • a bank or insurer has fallen below its regulator's capital requirement;
  • the company is not yet listed (you cannot buy it on NEPSE; an IPO is a separate decision, covered in Chapter 77).

Filter 5 — Spread across sectors. Keep 3–4 sectors on the shortlist, not ten banks. In Nepal, companies in one sector often rise and fall together: from 2021 to 2026 many microfinance companies' bad loans rose at the same time, and the Rasuwa flood of 26 August 2026 hit several hydropower plants in a single day.

For a beginner with about Rs 1 lakh to invest actively, four or five shares in the end is plenty. Very small positions lose more to fixed costs: each time you sell a company's shares you pay a fixed fee of about Rs 25, plus broker commission.

Step 4 — Check the people

Before you trust a company's numbers, check the people who publish them. If a buffalo seller once sold a sick animal and lied about it, his healthy-looking buffalo is not a bargain at any price. In Nepal this matters even more than in big markets, because most listed companies are controlled by a few promoter families, business houses or the government, and small shareholders have little power.

A promoter is a founder or controlling shareholder who started the company and usually sits on its board.

Where to look (all public)

Source What you find there
Annual report (sent before the AGM, often on the company website) Board members, the auditor's opinion, the related-party note, the promoter list
Quarterly reports (NEPSE and company website) Results, and whether they came out on time
NEPSE and SEBON notices Promoter share sales, AGM dates, penalties
NRB notices (banks, microfinance, finance companies) Fines, directives, "problematic" status
Financial news sites Disputes, court cases, ownership changes

The three checks. Chapter 64 gives governance 15 points, split into three checks.

Check 1 — Promoter holding and pledging (6 points). Are the owners staying, and are their shares free?

  • Who are the promoters, and how have their other companies treated small shareholders?
  • Are they selling? Count a fall in promoter holding only if it has no stated reason. A merger, a sale the law requires or a new public issue can lower it innocently. Unexplained selling, especially soon after a lock-in period ends, is the warning. In Chapter 83, Nabil Bank's foreign promoter tried to sell its stake twice in three years; both sales were stopped or withdrawn, but it still cost a point.
  • Are their shares pledged, meaning given as collateral for a loan? If the loan goes bad, the lender can sell those shares suddenly and the price falls on you. It is like a family house mortgaged to a moneylender: the family lives there, but someone else can take it.

Chapter 64 scores two parts, each out of 6, and the check takes the lower of the two (Canon rule):

Promoter holding, last 3 years Points Share of promoter shares pledged Points
Stable (moved under 1 point) or rising 6 Under 10% 6
Fell 1 to under 3 points 4 10% to under 25% 4
Fell 3 to 5 points 2 25% to 50% 2
Fell over 5 points, or repeated insider selling 0 Over 50% 0

Measure pledging as pledged promoter shares ÷ all promoter shares. If you cannot find pledge data, do not assume there is none: score this check at no more than 4 and write "pledging not verified".

Check 2 — Related parties and the auditor (5 points). Is money quietly moving to the owners' other businesses?

  • Related-party dealings are deals between the company and people or firms connected to its owners. Look in the annual report's related-party note. A bank lending heavily to its own directors' companies; a hydropower plant built, without open bidding, by a contractor its promoters own; a manufacturer buying raw material from the promoters' own firms at unclear prices: these are the classic leaks.
  • The auditor's opinion is printed at the start of the accounts. "Clean" (unqualified) is good, though it is reasonable assurance, not a guarantee against fraud. "Qualified" means the auditor disagrees on something: read what. Under Chapter 64 a qualification on loans, provisions or related-party dealings, a limit on the auditor's work in an important area, a going-concern doubt, an "adverse" opinion, a "disclaimer" or undisclosed related-party dealings all score 1 or less, which triggers the cap below. The full table (Canon rule, Chapter 64):
Audit opinion and related-party dealings Points (of 5)
Clean opinion; related-party dealings small and fully disclosed 5
Clean opinion with an emphasis-of-matter note (a paragraph drawing attention to something, not about going concern) 4
Accounts restated for an earlier year, or related-party dealings large though disclosed 3

  • Big revisions. Compare the first, unaudited yearly figure with the later audited one. One revision is normal; the same direction year after year is a pattern. Nabil's first-reported profit of Rs 7.13 arba for FY 2081/82 became Rs 5.92 arba after audit, and the year before showed the same pattern (Chapter 83).

Check 3 — Disclosure and the board (4 points). Do they tell you things on time, and is anyone on the board watching for you?

  • Are quarterly results published on time? Late reports often hide bad news.
  • Is the AGM held on time? A company that delays its AGM for years is not treating shareholders as owners.
  • Is there at least one independent director, a board member with no link to the promoters? Independence on paper is a start. Look also at whether they attend meetings, sit on the audit committee, and stay rather than resign quickly.
Disclosure and board Points (of 4)
Every quarterly report and the AGM on time for 3 years; more independent directors than the law's minimum, who attend at least three-quarters of meetings, with one on the audit committee 4
On time for 3 years, but only the legal minimum of independent directors, or the attendance or audit-committee test not met or not checked 3
One late quarterly report or one late AGM in 3 years, with no penalty 2
Two or more late filings in 3 years, or fewer independent directors than the law requires 1

Hard rules: these cap the whole Canon Score at 54 (below the 55 needed for new money), however good the profits look (Canon rule):

  • Check 1 scores 0 (over half of promoter shares pledged, an unexplained sharp fall, or repeated insider selling);
  • Check 2 scores 1 or less (the audit problems listed above);
  • the governance total is under 5 of 15.

Chapter 64 adds four more caps that are not about governance: NRB has declared the company "problematic"; a lender's capital is below its minimum in any layer (for a commercial bank, the Canon counts NRB's 2.5-point conservation buffer as part of that minimum; this is a Canon choice, explained in Step 5); an insurer's solvency ratio is under 100%; or there is fraud. However many rules fire, the result is one cap: the total cannot exceed 54. Add up the seven dimensions first, then apply the cap. It lasts only while the problem is current: when a later report clears it, the cap lifts.

Step 5 — Read the numbers

Now check whether the business itself is healthy, the way you would check a buffalo's milk: how much it gives, whether it is steady every season, and whether the animal is strong enough to survive a bad winter. This is Chapter 64's Financial Strength & Profitability, worth 20 points in three parts.

Part A — How much does it earn on the owners' money? (8 points)

The measure is ROE (return on equity): yearly profit divided by the shareholders' own money in the company. If you put Rs 10 lakh into a tea shop and it earns Rs 1.5 lakh in a year, your ROE is 15%. To be exact: profit for ordinary shareholders ÷ the average of the opening and closing equity for that year. Use the average of the last three years (three yearly ROEs from four year-end balance sheets), not one lucky year. A high ROE from heavy borrowing, or from provisions set too low, is not quality.

Three-year average ROE Points (of 8)
15% or more, with no year below 10% 8
10% to under 15%; or 15% or more with a year below 10% (no loss year either way) 6
7% to under 10%, no loss year 4
Under 7%, or a loss in any of the three years 1

A common-sense check: compare ROE with what owners need. The right yardstick is the cost of equity: the yearly return shareholders need to be paid for the risk of owning that share. Chapter 46 puts it in the low-to-mid teens for Nepal (its worked examples use 12% for banks and 14.5% for a hydropower company). A bank earning a 9% ROE is earning less than its owners need, even though 9% is three times the 3.07% average deposit rate (mid-October 2026). The deposit rate is only background: it tells you what safe money earns, not what risky money should earn. A tea shop that earns less than the risk of running it deserves is not a good shop, even if it beats a savings account.

Part B — Can it survive a bad year? (7 points)

This is the cushion test, and each sector uses a different ruler. Using the wrong ruler is a common beginner mistake.

Sector What to check The line to know
Commercial bank CAR (capital adequacy ratio): the bank's own money as a cushion against its loans See the explanation below the table. NRB's figure for all 20 commercial banks together: 12.61% (mid-July 2026) and 12.19% (mid-October)
Other lenders CAR, in every layer that applies to their class See the class table below the bank explanation
Insurer Solvency ratio: money available divided by the capital it must hold for its risks See the Nepal Insurance Authority ladder below. Under 100% is a hard limit
Hydropower, manufacturer, hotel Debt to equity and interest cover (operating profit divided by interest) Two small tables below: 3 points + 4 points

Think of CAR or solvency as a family's emergency fund: not the monthly income, but what saves you in a bad year. A minimum is a floor, not a comfortable cushion.

Banks: the minimum and the buffer are two different things. This is a fact from NRB's Capital Adequacy Framework: a commercial bank must hold at least 8.5% total capital, 6% Tier 1 (core) capital and 4.5% common equity (the shareholders' own money). On top sits a 2.5-point conservation buffer, held in common equity. Together that makes 11% / 8.5% / 7%. The buffer is like the last few metres of water in a reservoir: you may draw on it in a drought, but while you do, NRB limits what you can hand out. A bank inside the buffer but above the base minimum mainly faces limits on dividends and bonuses; it has not failed. A loss is worse than a fall, so one loss year scores lower than two falling years.

Real. Then take off 1 point if the profit check for the company's type fails:

  • Non-financial company: operating cash flow over the last three years, added up, is less than half of net profit over the same three years.
  • Bank or other lender: in any of the last three years, more than a quarter of profit came from writing back old loan-loss provisions or from one-off gains such as selling property.
  • Insurer: in any of the last three years, more than a quarter of profit came from realised gains on selling investments or from releasing old reserves.

Watch for three warning signs as you read:

  • Profit up, cash down. For a non-financial company (not a bank or insurer, whose cash flows work differently), check the cash flow statement (Chapter 27). Profit that never turns into cash is like a shop selling on credit that never gets paid.
  • One-off gains. A big profit from selling land or investments, not from the business.
  • The bonus-share illusion. After a bonus issue, EPS (earnings per share) falls because the same profit is split across more shares. Compare total profit, not just EPS (Chapter 85 shows this with Chhimek).

  • Bank: NPL (non-performing loans, bad loans as a share of all loans) against its class. Step 7 — Is today's price sensible?

A good company is not automatically a good buy. In 2021 many good Nepali companies traded at prices so high that buyers waited years just to get back to even. This step scores Valuation (15 points), which completes Share Attractiveness and gives the full Canon Score. Two identical flats in Bhaktapur, one at Rs 80 lakh and one at Rs 1.4 crore, have the same walls and roof, but they are very different deals.

Two price measures

  • P/E (price ÷ earnings per share): how many years of today's profit you are paying for. A share at Rs 600 that earns Rs 40 a year has a P/E of 15.
  • P/B (price ÷ book value per share): how much you pay for each rupee the company owns after paying its debts. A P/B of 2 means you pay Rs 2 for every Rs 1 of net assets.

The key rule: compare with the company's own sector. A fixed rule such as "P/E below 15 is cheap" fails in Nepal, because banks, hydropower companies and insurers always trade at very different levels. Compare each share with its sector's median: the middle company when all are lined up, so one extreme company cannot drag the comparison. To build a fair median:

  1. Use only similar business models, not just the same NEPSE label: operating hydropower plants apart from projects still being built; life insurers apart from non-life insurers; commercial banks apart from development banks and finance companies; microfinance institutions on their own.
  2. Use the same price date and the latest four quarters of profit for every company.
  3. Build the two medians separately, because they need different peers. For the P/E median, leave out companies with a loss over the last four quarters (a negative P/E means nothing). For the P/B median, keep loss-makers as long as their book value is positive; leave out only zero or negative book value. Use the same accounting basis and the latest published quarter for every company.
  4. Each median needs at least five companies of its own. With fewer, mark that part "unverified" and score it in the middle ("in line") row. So a sector can have a verified P/E median and an unverified P/B median.
  5. Use the unrounded ratio when you pick the row. A ratio of 1.504 belongs in "over 1.5", even if you write it as 1.50.

For insurers, earnings move with investment gains and actuarial assumptions, so read P/E with care; for life insurers, Chapter 74 explains why embedded value and reserve trends matter more. A company with a loss scores 0 on P/E. Sector methods such as cash-flow models for hydropower are in Chapters 43–49.

A discount must be earned. A cheap share gets the top row only if its ROE is at or above the sector median and, for a bank, its bad loans are at or below the system average. Otherwise it scores in the "in line" row. Cheap is often cheap for a reason, like a cheap plot of land that floods every monsoon.

Before you call a share cheap, ask four questions. Are today's profits likely to last? Is the ROE above the cost of equity? What growth does today's price already assume? What could permanently reduce what this business earns? A low P/E can simply mean profits are about to fall; a low P/B can mean the market doubts the loans.

Two cross-checks (write them down; they do not change the points, but they should change your decision in Step 8):

  • Plain numbers. A share at 0.8× a sector that itself trades at 40× profit is only cheap by comparison. The cheapest house on an expensive street is not a cheap house.
  • For banks: what P/B does the ROE justify? A simple formula links them: justified P/B = (ROE − g) ÷ (required return − g), where g is long-run growth. For a bank earning a 13% ROE, with 5% growth and a 12% required return: (13 − 5) ÷ (12 − 5) ≈ 1.1×. If the market charges 2.4× book, you are paying for a lot of hope. The formula holds only if that ROE and growth can last, and growth stays below the required return. The justified P/B is also a simple intrinsic-value estimate for a bank: value per share ≈ justified P/B × book value per share. Step 8's price check uses it.

A third check: what is the business actually worth? (not scored)

P/E and P/B tell you whether a share is cheap compared with its neighbours. They cannot tell you what the business is worth on its own. A share's price is what people pay for it today. Its intrinsic value is what the cash it will hand its owners in future is worth today. The two can be far apart, in both directions.

Think of buying a mango orchard in Sarlahi. Comparing prices with the orchard next door is relative valuation. Counting how many mangoes your trees will give over the coming years, and remembering that a mango ten years away is worth less to you than one this season, is intrinsic valuation. That second method is the DCF (discounted cash flow).

DCF in four plain steps

  1. Forecast the cash the business will leave for its owners each year, usually for five years. This is FCFE (free cash flow to equity). Chapter 46 builds it from the accounts: FCFE = net profit + depreciation and amortisation (and other non-cash charges) − capital spending − increase in working capital + net borrowing, where net borrowing = new loans taken − loan principal repaid. Depreciation and amortisation are added back because they are costs on paper, not cash leaving this year; interest is already taken out inside net profit. Working capital here means all the day-to-day operating balances: stock, money owed by customers and money owed to suppliers. This build-up is for ordinary companies: a bank's deposits are its raw material, not borrowing, so banks need the methods in the table below. Example: net profit Rs 10 crore, depreciation and amortisation Rs 4 crore, new equipment Rs 3 crore, Rs 1 crore more tied up in stock and unpaid bills, new loans Rs 2 crore, repayments Rs 5 crore (so net borrowing is −Rs 3 crore). FCFE = 10 + 4 − 3 − 1 + (2 − 5) = Rs 7 crore.
  2. Choose a required return (the discount rate): the yearly return you need to justify the risk. A rupee received in five years is worth less than a rupee today, and the higher the risk, the less it is worth.
  3. Add a terminal value for all the years after year five, using a cautious long-run growth rate. For a business with a limited life, do not assume cash forever. A hydropower plant's licence and PPA end on known dates; Chapter 46 says to value the remaining licensed life, plus a reasoned view of what happens at expiry. It is like valuing a shop on a 30-year lease: you count the years you can trade, not forever.
  4. Discount everything back to today and divide by the number of shares.

The reverse DCF: what does today's price assume? Turn the question round. Suppose HHPL trades at Rs 85. Keeping the same five-year forecast and the 14.5% required return, Rs 85 only makes sense if the cash then grows by about 8.7% a year, forever. A single plant with a fixed tariff, on a river that does not grow, cannot do that on its own. You would need evidence of something more: new capacity being built, tariff increases written into the PPA, or other new sources of cash. Without that evidence, at Rs 85 you would be paying for a story, not for the business. This check needs no forecasting skill: only the question "is the growth this price needs believable?"

Margin of safety. Because a DCF is a range, not a fact, you want to pay well below your estimate of value, not just a little below it. Margin of safety = (estimated value − price) ÷ estimated value. If HHPL's base value is Rs 54 and you pay Rs 40, your margin is (54 − 40) ÷ 54 ≈ 26%. Like a bridge built to carry three times its expected load, the gap protects you when your forecast turns out too hopeful. The Canon's rule is 35% (Canon rule, Chapters 66 and 67): buy only when the price is at least 35% below your own intrinsic-value estimate. In other words, the most you pay is 65% of your value: for HHPL's base value of Rs 54, that is about Rs 35. At Rs 40 the margin is only 26%, so the answer is Wait. The 35% line is this book's guideline for NEPSE, a design choice like the score's thresholds, chosen because forecasts in a small, sentiment-driven market are often wrong.

Step 8 — How much to buy, and when you will sell

The last step turns scores into rupee amounts, and writes down, before you buy, what would make you sell. A farmer may own excellent land, but he does not plant all of it with one crop, and he decides when to harvest before the season starts so the first hailstorm does not panic him.

First: three checks before any purchase (Canon rule, Chapter 66). A company that passes Steps 1–7 must still pass three checks before you place an order. None of them is part of the score, and none can be skipped.

  1. The price check. The Valuation dimension compares a company only with its own sector, so in a sector-wide bubble the cheapest of the expensive still scores well. So add an outside anchor:
    • Buy only when the price is at least 35% below your own intrinsic-value estimate (Step 7). Most you pay = 65% of your value. If it fails, the answer is Wait.
    • Earnings yield test: EPS ÷ price × 100, compared with the current one-year fixed-deposit rate. If the share earns less than a bank deposit pays, write down why you accept that (a hydropower company valued on its project cash flows is a legitimate reason; "everyone is buying" is not).
    • Overheated sector: if the sector's median P/E is more than 1.5 times its own five-year median, no new position larger than a small side position (the Adequate size, 1.5–3%), whatever the score.
  2. Your own liquidity check. The score's liquidity points only screen out shares nobody can trade. Run the Chapter 56 rule (Limit 2 below) on your actual order size, including the stressed week from Step 3.
  3. The season check. Step 2 decides whether the active part is buying at all this quarter. In Phase 2 it is not, however good the score.

Then: how much? Take the smallest of three limits.

Limit 1: the score's ceiling (Chapter 66). These are percentages of your whole share money (steady and active parts together) (Canon rule).

Canon Score Band Most you may hold in one share
85–100 Exceptional 6–8% (8% is the absolute maximum for any one share), built over 2–3 purchases
70–84 Strong 4–6%, built in parts
55–69 Adequate 1.5–3%, a side position re-checked every quarter
45–54 Watch list No new money
Below 45 Avoid No new money; a candidate to sell

These are ceilings, not targets. Two rules from Chapter 64 can lower a ceiling by one band: a Strong or Exceptional total whose Business Quality is under 38 of 75, or whose Share Attractiveness is under 13 of 25; and a score marked provisional.

Limit 2: can you sell it in time? (Chapter 56). Hold no more than about half of one day's average turnover (Step 3). Big investors hit this limit often; small ones rarely do. Check it anyway.

Limit 3: sector ceiling (Chapters 60, 61 and 66). Keep any one sector to about 25–30% of your share money. Your best five companies may all be banks, and if banks have a bad year, all five fall together. Count what your mutual fund holds too: if the fund keeps 40% in banks and you also own two banks directly, your real bank exposure is the two added together. Count overlap company by company too: if your fund holds Bank A and you also buy Bank A directly, your real Bank A exposure is your own shares plus your share of the fund's Bank A holding.

Permitted is not the same as purchased. The smallest of the three limits is the most you may hold. You can, and often should, buy less: when the price is only fair, when the company is new to you, or when you would struggle to sell it in a bad week. Ramesh may hold up to Rs 12,000 of Bank A, but its price fails the price check, so for now he buys none. Position size is a way of controlling risk, not a reward for finding a company you like.

Same size, different risk. Two 3% positions are not equally risky. A highly indebted hydropower plant can fall much further than a well-capitalised bank. When two shares get the same ceiling, give less to the one with more debt, thinner trading or more overlap with what you already own.

Two buying habits

  • Buy in parts. Split a position into two or three purchases over a few weeks, so you are not betting everything on one day's price. This spreads timing risk; it does not add return. Decide in advance what lets you buy the later parts: a price closer to fair value, or new results that confirm your reason. For very small amounts, one purchase is fine: on a few thousand rupees the timing gain from splitting is tiny. Split when the amount is large enough that a few weeks of price movement matter more than the extra effort.
  • Use a limit order, where you set the highest price you will pay. Avoid buying on days a share is rushing toward its 15% upper limit.

Placing the order, step by step

  1. In TMS, check the company's symbol carefully (similar names exist).
  2. Check that the share is trading normally today.
  3. Enter the quantity and your limit price, and read the total value and charges before you press buy.
  4. Check that the order was actually filled, not just placed.
  5. Pay on time, and two working days later (T+2) confirm the shares in MeroShare.

Know the full cost. Buying and selling cost more than the price: the broker's commission, a small SEBON fee, a fixed charge of about Rs 25 each time you sell a company's shares, and capital gains tax on any profit (rates in Chapter 35). Small trades lose a bigger share to these.

A worked example (rates as of November 2026; check your broker's current schedule). Buying Rs 6,000 of shares: broker commission 0.36% = Rs 21.60 (the minimum is Rs 10), SEBON fee 0.015% = Rs 0.90, so about Rs 22.50. Selling the same Rs 6,000 later costs the same again plus the Rs 25 DP charge: about Rs 47.50. The round trip is about Rs 70, or 1.2%, before any capital gains tax.

Part B — When will you sell? Write it down before you buy.

This is the step most Nepali investors skip, and it costs them the most. When a share falls 25%, fear takes over and decisions get worse. Decide now, while you are calm. For each share, write three things:

  1. Why I am buying: one or two lines.
  2. What would prove me wrong: specific signs you can check.
  3. What I will do then: sell, reduce, or hold without adding.

Seven reasons to sell or reduce (this guide's summary of Chapters 61, 66, 81 and 97):

Reason How you would know Usual action
Your reason for buying is proven wrong The sign you wrote down has happened Sell or reduce
Governance gets worse A hard rule fires: heavy pledging, an audit qualification, repeated insider selling Re-score at once; no new money while capped; sell if the problem is serious
The business weakens The score falls to 45–54, or below 45 Stop adding and review; below 45, a candidate to sell (Chapter 81)
The price runs far ahead of value The price now needs heroic growth (reverse DCF, or far above the justified P/B), and the valuation score has fallen Consider trimming: quality alone does not justify any price
The share becomes hard to sell Turnover falls so your holding is more than about half a day's trading, or a circuit-trap pattern appears Reduce toward the liquidity limit while you still can
One share or sector grows too big Above 8% in one share, or above 25–30% in one sector (counting your funds) Trim back to the ceiling
Your own life changes You will need the money within five years Move that money out calmly, over time, not in a panic

A review, not a reflex. A falling score starts a structured review: re-score from the new figures, decide whether the business, the price or your portfolio changed, then act. It is never an automatic sell order. A broken hard rule or a broken reason for buying needs the fastest review.

Two things that are not enough on their own. A fall of 10–15% is not proof that your reason was wrong: re-score first. A rise of 30% is not a reason to sell: check the price against value and your ceilings first.

Rules that apply to everyone

  • Re-score every quarter, when new results come out.
  • Do not wait for the quarter if something serious happens. A fraud allegation, an NRB or SEBON action, an audit qualification, a lender selling pledged promoter shares, a disaster at a plant, or a large rights issue: re-score at once.
  • A falling price alone is not a reason to sell. Ask whether the business got worse (re-score) or only the price moved (check your portfolio loss rules in Chapter 97). Never add more to a company whose score is falling just because it is cheaper than when you bought it.
  • Score falls to 45–54: stop adding and open a review.
  • Score falls below 45: a candidate to sell under Chapter 81's exit rules.
  • The season turns to Phase 2 (two quarterly checks agree): cut the active part to about half, selling the weakest scores first, and repay any loan.
  • Portfolio-level loss rules are in Chapter 97.
  • Only a documented company event can justify going against these rules, never a mood or a rumour (Chapter 96).

नेपालीमा सार

सेयर किन्नु भनेको हाटमा भैंसी किन्नुजस्तै हो: पहिले घरमा दाना छ कि छैन, अनि मौसम, अनि भैंसी, अनि बेच्ने मान्छे, अनि दूध, अनि मूल्य, र अन्त्यमा कतिवटा पाल्न सकिन्छ।

किन्नु मात्र उत्तर होइन। हरेक कम्पनीको अन्त्यमा चारमध्ये एउटा उत्तर आउँछ: किन्ने, पर्खने, अस्वीकार गर्ने, वा प्रमाण अपुग। नकिन्ने निर्णय पनि सही लगानी निर्णय हो।

  1. तयारी: करिब ६ महिनाको खर्च छुट्याउनुहोस्, महँगो ऋण तिर्नुहोस्, ५ वर्ष नचाहिने पैसा मात्र लगाउनुहोस्, मार्जिन कर्जा नलिनुहोस्। कम्तीमा आधा पैसा मासिक म्युचुअल फन्डमा राख्नुहोस्।
  2. मौसम: NRB को तथ्याङ्क हेरेर बजारको चरण थाहा पाउनुहोस्। यसले कुन सेयर होइन, कति पैसा लगाउने भन्छ। लगातार दुई जाँच मिलेपछि मात्र योजना बदल्नुहोस्।
  3. छोटो सूची: बुझ्ने क्षेत्र मात्र, दैनिक कारोबार रु २ लाखभन्दा माथि, सर्किट ट्र्याप र स्पष्ट खतराका संकेत भएका कम्पनी हटाउनुहोस्।
  4. मानिस: प्रवर्द्धक, धितो राखिएका सेयर, सम्बन्धित पक्षसँगको कारोबार, लेखापरीक्षकको राय र समयमै जानकारी जाँच्नुहोस्। सुशासन ५ भन्दा कम भए हटाउनुहोस्।
  5. हिसाबकिताब: ROE, पुँजी कुसन (बैंकमा CAR, बीमामा solvency) र स्थिर, साँचो नाफा हेर्नुहोस्। ROE लाई निक्षेपको ब्याजसँग होइन, सेयरधनीले चाहने प्रतिफल (cost of equity, नेपालमा करिब १२–१५%) सँग तुलना गर्नुहोस्। NRB का अनुसार २० वाणिज्य बैंकको समग्र CAR १२.१९% र NPL ५.७१% (असोज २०८३) छ।
  6. व्यवसायको गुणस्तर (७५): टिकाउपन, वृद्धि र नगद लाभांश थपेर कम्पनी राम्रो छ कि छैन हेर्नुहोस्। लाइसेन्स मात्रै moat होइन: पूरा ८ अङ्कका लागि प्रमाण चाहिन्छ। हरेक अङ्क कुन तालिकाको कुन पङ्क्तिबाट आयो, लेख्नुहोस्। जलविद्युत् र बीमालाई आफ्नै क्षेत्रसँग मात्र तुलना गर्नुहोस्।
  7. मूल्य: P/E र P/B आफ्नै क्षेत्रको बीचको कम्पनीसँग तुलना गर्नुहोस्। सेयरको बजार मूल्य र व्यवसायको वास्तविक मूल्य (DCF) फरक कुरा हुन्; आजको मूल्यले असम्भव वृद्धि मागेको छ भने नकिन्नुहोस्। सस्तोपनले अङ्क तब मात्र पाउँछ जब कम्पनी राम्रो हुन्छ। किन्नुअघि आफ्नो वास्तविक मूल्य (intrinsic value) अनुमान गर्नुहोस् र बजार मूल्य त्योभन्दा कम्तीमा ३५% कम भए मात्र किन्नुहोस्। ५५ वा बढी अङ्क "किन्न योग्य" हो, "किन्नैपर्छ" होइन।
  8. कति र कहिले बेच्ने: सीमाले बढीमा कति राख्न पाइन्छ भन्छ; त्योभन्दा कम किन्न सकिन्छ। बेच्ने सात कारण (कारण गलत साबित, सुशासन बिग्रिनु, व्यवसाय कमजोर, मूल्य धेरै महँगो, बेच्न गाह्रो, एउटै सेयर वा क्षेत्र धेरै ठूलो, आफ्नो जीवनको आवश्यकता) पहिल्यै लेख्नुहोस्। तीन सीमामध्ये सबैभन्दा सानो मान्नुहोस्, रकम ठूलो भए किस्तामा किन्नुहोस्, कमिसन र DP शुल्कसहित पूरा खर्च हिसाब गर्नुहोस्, र किन्नुअघि नै बेच्ने नियम लेख्नुहोस्।

सबैभन्दा महत्त्वपूर्ण तीन कुरा: राम्रो कम्पनी हुनु र राम्रो किनाइ हुनु फरक हो; नेपालमा "बेच्न सकिन्छ कि सकिँदैन" उत्तिकै महत्त्वपूर्ण छ; र किन्नुअघि नै बेच्ने नियम लेख्नुहोस्। यो शिक्षाका लागि हो, लगानी सल्लाह होइन।
