Case Study 1 — A Commercial Bank

Case Study 1 — A Commercial Bank

Sushmita Karki had read fifteen Parts of this book before she opened her first spreadsheet to actually use any of it. That gap — between understanding a framework and running it, cold, on a real company with real numbers that refuse to line up neatly — is the gap this entire Part exists to close. Parts XIII through XV gave you the machinery: the Canon Score itself, its seven dimensions, the sector adjustments that keep a bank from being graded like a trekking company, the backtesting mindset, the calibration habit, the exit-rule discipline, and the drift-management routine that keeps all of it honest as the market changes underneath you. Part XVI is where that machinery meets eight real companies, one per chapter, worked start to finish the way you would actually do it at your own desk on a Saturday morning with tea going cold beside your laptop.

Sushmita chose to start with a commercial bank for a reason that will sound almost too simple: banks are the most "textbook" sector on the Nepal Stock Exchange (NEPSE). Every commercial bank in the country reports to the same regulator, Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB), on the same quarterly schedule, using largely the same handful of ratios. A hydropower company's value depends on rainfall and river flow. A hotel's value depends on tourist arrivals nobody can fully predict. A bank's value depends on numbers that show up, every quarter, in a disclosure format NRB itself prescribes. If you are going to practice the Canon Score for the first time on a real company, a bank is the fairest test — the data exists, it is comparable across the whole peer set, and there is nowhere to hide behind a story.

The bank Sushmita picked was Nabil Bank Limited, traded on NEPSE under the ticker NABIL. This chapter follows her through all six lessons of the case study, using Nabil's real, publicly reported figures as of mid-2025 and the first half of fiscal year 2082/83. Some of those figures move a little from one disclosure to the next — audited versus unaudited, one quarter versus the next — and rather than smoothing that mess away, this chapter treats it as part of the lesson. Real companies do not hand you a single clean number. They hand you a range, and your job is to read the range honestly.

Lesson 83.1 — Setting Up the Case: A Bank Called Nabil

Nabil Bank has a claim to being the single most historically important private bank on NEPSE. It was incorporated in 1984 as Nepal Arab Bank Limited, the country's first joint-venture commercial bank — meaning a domestic bank formed in partnership with a foreign banking group, in this case Emirates-linked capital that was later replaced by Bangladesh's IFIC Bank as the principal foreign promoter. It was later renamed Nabil Bank Limited, and it has traded on NEPSE for decades under the scrip code NABIL, long enough that several generations of Nepali retail investors have owned it at one point or another, often as a first bank stock.

In July 2022, Nabil completed a merger with Nepal Bangladesh Bank Limited (NB Bank), swapping 43 Nabil shares for every 100 NB Bank shares held. Mergers of this size reshape a bank's numbers for years afterward — loan books get blended, deposit bases get combined, and ratios that looked clean before the merger often look messier for several reporting cycles afterward as the two institutions' loan quality, systems, and staff get folded into one. Keep that merger date in mind; it explains almost every awkward number in this chapter.

As of its most recent disclosures, Nabil operates 268 branches and around 316 ATMs across Nepal, with a paid-up capital of roughly Rs 32.06 arba (Rs 32.06 billion) — about four times NRB's old minimum capital mandate, a detail Lesson 83.4 will return to in detail. Its shares outstanding sit at about 27.06 crore (270.6 million), promoter shareholders hold roughly 58.44 percent of the company, and the general public holds the remaining 41.56 percent. Market capitalization has recently sat around Rs 146 arba (Rs 146 billion), making Nabil one of the larger banks on the exchange by market value, though no longer automatically the largest — a decade of mergers among its peers has produced several banks of comparable or greater size.

Sushmita's first move, before touching a single Canon Score dimension, was to pull up Chapter 71's banking-sector playbook and re-read the six numbers it insists any competent bank analysis must include. She wrote them on an index card and taped it above her desk, because — as she put it in her notes — "if I can't explain these six things to my mother in one sentence each, I don't actually understand the bank yet."

KEY CONCEPT A bank's capital adequacy ratio works like a household's emergency fund relative to its total borrowing — it is not how much money is coming in this month, it is how much cushion exists to absorb a bad month (or a bad year) without the household defaulting on anyone. NRB sets a floor under that cushion for every commercial bank; falling below the floor is a regulatory emergency, not just a bad quarter.

Sushmita's working assumption going in — one she wrote at the top of her notebook page — was that because Nabil is old, large, and closely watched, its Canon Score would probably land somewhere comfortably good, and the exercise would mostly be about confirming that instinct with numbers. By the end of Lesson 83.5, that assumption would need real revision. That is, in fact, exactly what a first full case study is supposed to do to you.

Lesson 83.2 — Hemisphere 1: Liquidity, Governance, and Business Model Durability

The Canon Score splits into two broad hemispheres. Hemisphere 1 covers the qualitative dimensions — is this stock actually tradeable, who controls the company, and do the people running it have a track record you can trust. Hemisphere 2 covers the quantitative dimensions built from the financial statements themselves. Sushmita worked Hemisphere 1 first, on the theory that if a stock fails the basic "can I actually buy and sell this at a real price" test, the fundamentals barely matter.

Liquidity and market structure. Average daily volume (ADV) — the average number of shares that actually change hands each trading day — is the practical measure of whether a stock can absorb a meaningful order without moving the price against you. Nabil's ADV has recently run around 46,000 shares over a one-week window and about 52,000 shares over a one-month window. At a share price near Rs 540, a single day's typical volume represents roughly Rs 2.5 to 2.8 crore of turnover — not deep enough to move six-figure-dollar institutional size without noticeable slippage, but comfortably enough for the retail position sizes this book has discussed throughout Parts I through XII. Free float — the portion of shares actually available to trade, as opposed to locked up with promoters — sits at roughly 41.56 percent of the company, since promoters hold the remaining 58.44 percent. That is a normal, unremarkable split for a NEPSE commercial bank; it is neither an unusually closed company nor an unusually widely held one.

Governance. Nabil's promoter group has historically centered on IFIC Bank of Bangladesh as the principal foreign joint-venture partner, alongside Nepali business interests including the Chaudhary Group. In 2025, news emerged of a planned transfer of roughly 17.7 million promoter shares from IFIC Bank to the Chaudhary Group — a transaction that, at the time Sushmita did her research, was sitting under a court stay order rather than proceeding cleanly. That is worth flagging honestly rather than glossing over: a contested promoter-share transfer under judicial stay is exactly the kind of governance texture the Canon Score's Governance & Promoter Behavior dimension is designed to catch, even when it does not amount to a scandal. It signals an ownership structure in transition, with a legal dispute attached, and Sushmita marked it as a fact to re-check at the next reporting cycle rather than a reason to panic.

CASE IN POINT Nabil's own quarterly disclosures illustrate why "Governance & Promoter Behavior" is a real, gradable dimension and not a box-ticking formality. Its Q4 FY2081/82 unaudited results reported net profit of roughly Rs 7.13 arba, a 15.01 percent jump — but the subsequently audited figure came in at roughly Rs 5.92 arba, a downward revision of about 17 percent. Reporting patterns like this, where unaudited quarterly figures run consistently ahead of the audited restatement, have shown up more than once in Nabil's recent history. None of this means fraud; unaudited-to-audited revisions happen across the sector. But a pattern of revisions in one direction, quarter after quarter, is exactly the kind of soft signal that belongs in a Governance & Promoter Behavior score rather than being waved away as a rounding difference.

Governance & Promoter Behavior, more broadly. On the positive side of the ledger, Nabil is the oldest continuously operating private commercial bank in Nepal, has survived four decades of political and economic cycles, completed a complex merger without a depositor-facing crisis, and in July 2025 commissioned a fresh issuer rating from CARE Ratings Nepal — the kind of voluntary external scrutiny that a bank hiding something usually avoids inviting. Sushmita's research turned up no major publicized NRB enforcement action against Nabil in recent years. Balanced against that is the audited-versus-unaudited revision pattern above, and the unresolved promoter-share dispute. Her honest note in her worksheet read: "Old and mostly clean, but not spotless, and not finished changing hands at the top." That sentence, more than any single number, is what the Governance & Promoter Behavior dimension is trying to capture.

WARNING Do not let a bank's age and reputation substitute for actually checking the current governance picture. A bank that was impeccably run for thirty years can still be mid-transition in its ownership or mid-recovery from a merger today. Score the bank you can verify right now, not the bank's reputation.

Sector & Business Model Durability. Nabil's moat, such as it is for a commercial bank, rests less on any single product and more on scale, history, and brand: it is the oldest continuously operating private bank in Nepal, the country's original joint-venture bank, and it carries that brand across 268 branches and around 316 ATMs nationwide — a distribution network few peers can match branch for branch. It has also pushed into digital banking through its nBank neo-banking initiative, a real if still early-stage attempt to defend that franchise against digital-only competitors. On the concentration side, Nabil's loan book is a diversified, broad-based commercial banking portfolio spanning retail, SME, and corporate lending rather than a bet concentrated in one sector or a handful of large borrowers — the kind of durability a bank's size and age are supposed to buy it, and mostly deliver here.

Lesson 83.3 — Hemisphere 2: What the Numbers Actually Say

With Hemisphere 1 assessed, Sushmita turned to the financial statements themselves — Hemisphere 2, the quantitative half of the Canon Score, covering Financial Strength & Profitability, Growth Trajectory, Dividend & Capital Return Discipline, and Valuation Reasonableness.

Financial Strength & Profitability. For fiscal year 2081/82 (roughly mid-2024 to mid-2025), Nabil reported customer deposits of about Rs 5.24 kharba (Rs 524 billion), up 13.53 percent year-on-year, and loans and advances of about Rs 4.12 kharba (Rs 412 billion), up 10.50 percent. Its CASA ratio — the share of deposits sitting in low-cost current and savings accounts rather than more expensive fixed deposits — stood at about 44.35 percent, recovering from a post-merger trough near 35 percent. Its cost of funds — what it pays, on average, to raise deposits and other funding — fell from about 5.86 percent to 4.41 percent over the same stretch, and continued falling toward roughly 3.25 percent in the following quarters. Earnings per share (EPS) rose from about Rs 22.90 to about Rs 26.34 for the fiscal year, with some data providers showing an annualized figure closer to Rs 27.74 depending on which quarter's trailing window they use. Reported net profit for the year, using the unaudited figure, was about Rs 7.13 arba; the audited figure, as already noted, came in lower at about Rs 5.92 arba — an earnings-quality wrinkle that belongs squarely inside this dimension, not off to the side of it.

Capital adequacy and asset quality — the CAR/leverage half of Financial Strength & Profitability. This is where a bank's numbers earn their keep, and where NRB's regulatory floors matter most directly.

REGULATORY DETAIL Nepal Rastra Bank requires Class 'A' commercial banks to maintain a minimum total capital fund (capital adequacy ratio) of 11 percent of risk-weighted assets. NRB's separate credit-to-deposit ceiling — the rule that a bank cannot lend out more than 90 percent of the deposits and qualifying funds it holds — has been the sector's headline liquidity constraint for years, though NRB's monetary policy for 2025/26 signals a shift toward assessing bank liquidity through Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) and Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR) standards instead, with the older CD-ratio framework due to be revised once that transition is complete.

Against that 11 percent floor, Nabil's capital adequacy ratio has recently sat in a range from about 11.8 percent to about 12.5 percent, depending on the quarter measured — dipping slightly in the immediate aftermath of the NB Bank merger's integration and recovering somewhat since. That is comfortably above the regulatory minimum, but not by a wide margin; a bank running at 11.9 percent against an 11 percent floor has roughly 90 basis points (0.9 percentage points) of headroom, not the several-percentage-point cushion that some peers carry.

Nabil's non-performing loan ratio has followed a similar arc: from a pristine 0.84 percent in fiscal year 2078/79, up to a post-merger peak near 4.78 percent in 2080/81 as NB Bank's loan book was absorbed and reclassified under Nabil's own underwriting standards, and back down to a range of roughly 4.3 to 4.5 percent in the most recent quarters. That is a large move from the bank's historic norm, and it has not yet fully round-tripped back to pre-merger levels. On the liquidity side, Nabil's loan-to-deposit ratio has recently sat around 82.5 percent, comfortably under the 90 percent regulatory ceiling, implying roughly Rs 39 arba of additional lending capacity before that ceiling would bind.

Growth Trajectory. Nabil's deposit and loan growth for fiscal year 2081/82 — deposits up 13.53 percent, loans and advances up 10.50 percent — is solid, broad-based expansion for a bank of Nabil's size, and its CASA ratio's recovery from a post-merger trough near 35 percent back to about 44.35 percent shows the merged deposit base settling into a healthier, lower-cost mix rather than staying diluted. Earnings-per-share growth over the same period, from about Rs 22.90 to about Rs 26.34 (or roughly Rs 27.74 on an annualized basis, depending on the data provider's trailing window), looks equally encouraging on its face. The complication, and the reason Sushmita marked this dimension's consistency down rather than up, is that a meaningful share of that EPS growth is arithmetic recovery from the 2022 merger's own dilution and integration drag rather than fresh organic growth on top of a stable base — and it sits alongside the same unaudited-to-audited profit revision pattern already flagged under governance. Growth that is real but partly a rebound off a self-inflicted dip is worth less, dimension for dimension, than the same headline percentage generated by a business that was never disrupted in the first place.

Dividend & Capital Return Discipline. Nabil has paid a dividend in every one of the last fifteen-plus fiscal years, averaging close to 37 percent historically — a genuinely long, unbroken record. But the shape of that record has changed sharply and recently. Fiscal years 2075/76 through 2077/78 paid large, bonus-share-heavy totals — 34 percent, 35.26 percent, and 38 percent of paid-up capital respectively, most of it in bonus shares rather than cash. Starting in fiscal year 2079/80, that pattern reversed: Nabil moved to cash-only dividends, and the totals fell hard, to 11 percent, then 10 percent, then 12.5 percent most recently for fiscal year 2081/82 — roughly a third of the pre-merger norm, with no bonus shares at all for three straight years running. That decline lines up neatly with the capital-adequacy picture Lesson 83.4 examines next: a bank sitting below its sector's average CAR has a straightforward reason to retain more capital rather than distribute it, and Nabil's shift to smaller, cash-only payouts reads less like distress and more like a deliberate, if shareholder-unfriendly in the short run, capital-preservation choice during merger digestion.

Valuation Reasonableness. At a recent share price near Rs 541 to Rs 548, against a book value per share of roughly Rs 235 to Rs 247, Nabil trades at a price-to-book (P/B) ratio of about 2.19 times — a premium valuation by NEPSE banking-sector standards, where several peers trade closer to 1.0 to 1.5 times book. Its price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio sits around 19.5 to 20.5 times trailing earnings, also on the richer side of the sector range. Return on equity (ROE) — the number that a premium valuation ultimately has to justify — has come in at roughly 10 to 11 percent for fiscal year 2081/82 on the most commonly cited basis, a sharp fall from a pre-merger run rate closer to 19 to 20 percent. (Different data providers compute this differently — some use average shareholders' equity, some use period-end equity, some annualize a single strong quarter — which is why you will see figures for Nabil's ROE ranging from under 10 percent to over 18 percent depending on the source. Sushmita's practical fix was to compute it herself, net profit divided by average equity for the full fiscal year, and treat any other figure as a cross-check rather than a substitute.)

PRACTICAL TOOL When a metric shows meaningfully different values across data sources — as ROE did here — do not average the sources or pick whichever number flatters your thesis. Recompute it yourself from the audited annual report using one consistent formula, note which formula you used, and use that number every time you revisit the position. Consistency in your own method matters more than chasing the "correct" published figure.

Put together, Nabil's Hemisphere 2 picture is a bank still working through the tail end of a large merger: deposit and loan growth are healthy and funding costs are falling, but return on equity has compressed sharply, asset quality has not yet fully normalized, the dividend has shrunk to a third of its pre-merger norm as capital gets retained rather than distributed — and the stock's valuation multiples have not obviously priced any of that in.

Lesson 83.4 — Applying the Drift Adjustment: Capital Adequacy, Relatively Judged

Chapter 82 flagged a specific, well-documented problem with the original Canon Score design: the capital-adequacy sub-score for banks had been built years earlier around NRB's landmark paid-up capital mandate — the rule, dating back roughly a decade, that forced every commercial bank to hold at least Rs 8 billion in paid-up capital or merge with one that did. That single policy triggered the wave of bank mergers (including, indirectly, the one that created today's larger Nabil) that consolidated dozens of small banks into the current field of survivors. At the time, "does this bank clear Rs 8 billion in paid-up capital" was a genuinely useful, discriminating question — plenty of banks did not clear it, and it correctly separated the institutions that would survive from those that would be absorbed.

A decade later, that question has stopped discriminating between anything. Nabil's paid-up capital sits at roughly Rs 32 arba — four times the old mandate — and so does essentially every other surviving commercial bank on NEPSE, because the ones that could not get there were merged out of existence years ago. A scoring rule that once separated strong banks from weak ones now gives every single remaining bank full marks. That is model drift in its purest form: the rule is still technically true, and still completely useless for telling Nabil apart from its peers.

WARNING A scoring rule that once discriminated well between good and bad companies can quietly become a rule that every survivor passes, for reasons that have nothing to do with quality — because the weak candidates were already removed from the population by the very event the rule was designed to detect. When every company in your peer set clears a threshold, that threshold has stopped being useful evidence about any one of them.

Chapter 82's fix was to replace the old binary Rs 8 billion checkpoint with a relative, peer-comparison approach for the capital-adequacy component: instead of asking "does this bank clear the old absolute floor," ask "how does this bank's actual capital adequacy ratio compare to its current NEPSE peer group." That is exactly what Sushmita did next, using a mid-2025 sector snapshot of commercial bank CAR figures.

Full marks, case closed. Under the relative approach Chapter 82 recommends, the picture changes meaningfully: Nabil's 11.94 percent CAR sits below the sector average of 13.08 percent, ranks ninth out of the twelve major banks in this snapshot, and carries a thinner capital buffer above the regulatory floor than eight of its listed peers. That does not make Nabil unsafe — it remains comfortably compliant — but it does mean Nabil is one of the less well-capitalized large banks on the exchange relative to its own peer set, which is a meaningfully different, and more useful, piece of information than "it clears an old absolute rule that nobody fails anymore."

REGULATORY DETAIL NRB's 2025/26 monetary policy adds two more reasons the capital-adequacy picture is worth watching rather than treating as settled: it directs NRB to develop a Domestic Systemically Important Bank (DSIB) framework, which would impose extra capital and supervisory requirements on the handful of banks judged too large or interconnected to fail — a group Nabil, given its size and history, would very plausibly sit inside — and it schedules a long-delayed asset quality review of commercial banks' loan books, previously stalled by litigation against the central bank. Either development could move Nabil's effective capital requirement, and its measured asset quality, independent of anything the bank itself does differently.

Sushmita's note here was blunt: "The old rule said Nabil's capital position was basically perfect. The relative rule says it's fine, but it's the ninth-best-capitalized bank out of twelve where nobody's actually bad — and there's a new systemic-bank rule and a stalled asset-quality review both headed this way." That is precisely the kind of adjustment the drift-management discipline of Chapter 82 is meant to produce: not a dramatic reversal, but a materially more honest, more discriminating number than the one the un-updated model would have handed her.

Summed, Nabil Bank's Canon Score comes to 58 out of 100 — squarely in the book's Adequate band (55 to 69), and well clear of the governance-override threshold, since Governance & Promoter Behavior's 10 out of 15 sits comfortably above the 5-point floor that would otherwise cap the whole score in Weak/Avoid regardless of the total.

Sushmita was honest with herself about which dimensions were hardest to judge, and a fair-minded analyst working the same facts could reasonably land a few points higher or lower on any of them. Three stood out.

Financial Strength & Profitability was the hardest single call. Nabil is unambiguously compliant with every NRB capital and liquidity requirement in force today. The question is how much to penalize a fully compliant bank for sitting in the lower half of its peer group on capital adequacy, especially with a systemic-bank framework and an asset-quality review both pending, and how heavily to weigh the audited-vs-unaudited profit gap against an otherwise-recovering deposit and loan franchise. Score it too harshly and you punish a bank for a relative ranking rather than an actual regulatory breach; score it too gently and you repeat exactly the drift error Chapter 82 was written to fix. Sushmita's 10 out of 20 reflects a deliberate middle path, and she flagged it in her notes as the number most likely to move at the next data refresh.

Governance & Promoter Behavior was close behind. A recurring pattern of unaudited results running ahead of the audited restatement is a real signal, but it is also common enough across the sector that treating it as disqualifying would be disproportionate, and the unresolved IFIC-to-Chaudhary Group share transfer is a live dispute rather than a settled scandal. Reasonable analysts could argue for anywhere between 8 and 12 out of 15 here depending on how much weight they put on disclosure discipline and the pending ownership question versus the bank's much longer clean operating history.

Valuation Reasonableness was the third genuine judgment call. A 2.19x book multiple on a bank earning roughly 10 to 11 percent on that book is, by the plain arithmetic of return on equity versus valuation, not obviously cheap. But Nabil's ROE compression is plausibly temporary — a direct byproduct of digesting a large merger rather than a structural decline in the underlying franchise — and a market willing to pay up for eventual recovery is not automatically an irrational market. Sushmita's 5 out of 15 assumes the compression is mostly temporary but not fully resolved; an analyst more confident in a swift recovery could reasonably score several points higher, and one who suspects the compression reflects a permanently larger, slower-growing, harder-to-manage post-merger institution could reasonably score several points lower.

CAUTION A Canon Score is a snapshot of one analyst's honest judgment on one day, built from disclosures that themselves get revised. Two careful people working from the same facts can reasonably land five or six points apart on a 100-point scale without either one being wrong. Treat the number as a structured argument you can defend, not as a verdict handed down from the framework itself.

Lesson 83.6 — The Decision, and What Would Change It

A score of 58 out of 100 sits in the book's Adequate band (55 to 69) — well clear of the danger zone that would flag an active Avoid, but well short of the conviction level that would justify calling Nabil a fresh Buy at its current price. Sushmita's conclusion, working purely through the process and holding no existing position, was this: Nabil Bank is a fundamentally sound, well-run institution still absorbing the tail end of a large merger, priced by the market as though that absorption were mostly complete, when the numbers say it is not quite there yet. That combination argues for watching rather than buying at current levels — and, for an existing holder, for holding rather than selling, since nothing in the case study points to actual deterioration, only to a valuation that has run a little ahead of the underlying recovery.

CAUTION This case study is a demonstration of a process, not a personal recommendation to buy, hold, or avoid Nabil Bank or any other security. It shows how the Canon Score's machinery, honestly applied to real, verifiable numbers, arrives at a conclusion — not what any individual reader should do with their own capital, which depends on their own goals, time horizon, and the rest of their portfolio.

The more durable value of the exercise is the watch list it produces, because a Canon Score is never a one-time verdict — it is the current reading on an instrument you keep checking, exactly as Chapter 82 insists. Four specific developments would move Nabil's score, and Sushmita wrote all four into a recurring calendar reminder rather than trusting herself to remember them unprompted.

First, a sustained recovery in return on equity back toward the mid-teens, sourced from Nabil's own audited annual report rather than a single flattering quarter, would lift both the Financial Strength & Profitability and Valuation Reasonableness dimensions meaningfully — it would be the clearest sign that the merger's integration costs are genuinely behind the bank rather than still working through the numbers. Second, a capital adequacy ratio that climbs back above the sector average, rather than sitting in the bottom third of the peer table — alongside the outcomes of NRB's planned asset quality review and its forthcoming Domestic Systemically Important Bank framework, both explicitly flagged in the 2025/26 monetary policy — would lift the Financial Strength & Profitability score further under the same relative logic Lesson 83.4 applied, and conversely, a further slide on any of these would be a real warning sign, not a rounding error. Third, resolution of the IFIC-to-Chaudhary Group promoter-share transfer, whichever way the pending court matter concludes, would remove a live piece of governance uncertainty from the Governance & Promoter Behavior dimension. Fourth, the next AGM's dividend decision — whether the cash-only payout holds near 10-12.5 percent, climbs back toward the pre-merger norm, or falls further — would directly move Dividend & Capital Return Discipline and say something real about whether capital retention is a temporary, merger-driven choice or a longer-term shift in how the bank treats its shareholders.

PRACTICAL TOOL Turn every "what would change this score" item into a specific, checkable trigger with a source and a rough date, not a vague intention to "keep an eye on it." Sushmita's list: (1) next audited annual report — ROE trend, (2) next quarterly NRB capital-adequacy sector data plus the pending asset-quality review and DSIB framework — CAR versus peer average, (3) court docket or company disclosure — promoter share transfer outcome, (4) next AGM disclosure — dividend payout trend. A watch list without dates and sources quietly turns into a watch list nobody ever rechecks.

Sushmita closed her notebook on this exercise with a line that captures the whole point of a first case study better than any score could: "I went in assuming the answer was obviously yes. The process didn't say no — it said not yet, and here's exactly what would change that." That is what a disciplined framework is supposed to do to a beginner's instinct — not override it with false precision, but slow it down long enough to be checked.

Chapter recap

This chapter took the entire Canon Score apparatus built across Parts XIII through XV and ran it, in full, against one real, currently listed NEPSE commercial bank: Nabil Bank Limited, NEPSE ticker NABIL. Working alongside a first-time practitioner, Sushmita Karki, the chapter moved through Nabil's basic profile — its 1984 origin as Nepal's first joint-venture bank, its 2022 merger with Nepal Bangladesh Bank, its 268 branches and roughly Rs 32 arba paid-up capital — and the six banking-sector numbers from Chapter 71 that any competent bank analysis has to incorporate: net interest margin, credit-to-deposit ratio, capital adequacy ratio, non-performing loan ratio and provisioning, cost-to-income ratio, and return on equity.

From there, the case study worked both hemispheres of the Canon Score in turn. Hemisphere 1 examined Nabil's Liquidity & Tradability (a real but moderate average daily volume, a normal promoter-to-public split), its Governance & Promoter Behavior (a long clean operating history complicated by a recurring unaudited-to-audited profit revision pattern and an unresolved, court-stayed promoter-share transfer), and its Sector & Business Model Durability (the oldest private bank on NEPSE, a large branch network, and an early digital push, spread across a diversified loan book). Hemisphere 2 examined the hard financial-statement numbers — Financial Strength & Profitability's healthy deposit and loan growth and falling funding costs set against a compressed return on equity and a below-average capital adequacy ratio, a Growth Trajectory partly inflated by post-merger recovery arithmetic, a Dividend & Capital Return Discipline that has shrunk from a bonus-heavy 30-plus percent to a cash-only 10-12.5 percent as capital gets retained, and a Valuation Reasonableness that has not obviously priced any of that compression in.

The chapter then applied Chapter 82's drift-adjustment discipline directly and concretely: rather than scoring capital adequacy against the now-saturated Rs 8 billion paid-up capital mandate that every surviving bank clears by a wide margin, it scored Nabil's actual capital adequacy ratio against its current NEPSE peer set — finding Nabil compliant with NRB's 11 percent floor but sitting below the 13.08 percent sector average, ninth of twelve major banks. That single substitution, from an absolute rule everyone passes to a relative comparison that actually discriminates, is the clearest illustration in this book so far of why model drift management is not an abstract concern but a routine adjustment with a visible effect on a real score.

Tallied across all seven of Chapter 64's real dimensions, Nabil's full Canon Score came to 58 out of 100 — squarely in the book's Adequate band, rather than a clear Buy or Avoid, with Financial Strength & Profitability, Governance & Promoter Behavior, and Valuation Reasonableness singled out honestly as the three dimensions where a reasonable analyst working the same facts could land several points differently. The governance override built into Chapter 64's framework did not trigger here, since Governance & Promoter Behavior's 10 out of 15 sits well above the 5-point floor that would otherwise have capped the whole score in Weak/Avoid. The chapter closed by turning that score into a decision framed with appropriate humility — a demonstration of process, not personalized advice — and into a concrete, dated watch list tied to Chapter 82's ongoing-monitoring discipline: Nabil's next audited ROE figure, its next quarterly capital-adequacy standing against peers (alongside NRB's pending asset quality review and Domestic Systemically Important Bank framework), the resolution of its promoter-share dispute, and its next AGM's dividend decision.

Chapter 84 turns to Case Study 2 — A Hydropower Plant, applying this same complete framework to a company whose economics could hardly be more different from a bank's. Where a commercial bank's numbers arrive on a predictable quarterly schedule under a single regulator's uniform template, a hydropower company's fortunes turn on monsoon rainfall, river flow, power purchase agreement terms, and a construction and commissioning history that, unlike a bank's loan book, cannot be smoothed by refinancing. The next chapter will show how the same seven Canon Score dimensions, the same qualitative-versus-quantitative hemisphere split, and the same drift-awareness discipline apply to a business built on rivers instead of deposits — and where the sector-specific adjustments of Chapter 65 diverge most sharply from the banking playbook this chapter just finished applying end to end.
