Nepal’s External Sector and NEPSE Implications

A Nepali investor who studies only price-earnings ratios and quarterly results is reading half the ledger. NEPSE does not sit in a closed domestic system. It floats on a sea of foreign currency that Nepal does not print, cannot fully control, and must earn, borrow or receive as remittances from its citizens working abroad. When that sea runs low, Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB) cannot stand aside. It tightens credit — and credit is the oxygen of the share market, as Chapters 0.1 and 0.2 showed. This chapter builds the external half of your toolkit: the balance of payments, remittances, foreign exchange reserves, and import cover, the measure that ties them together. It then uses real data from Nepal's 2022 reserve crisis and its recovery through 2025/26 to show exactly how a balance-of-payments squeeze becomes a NEPSE fall. If you can read NRB's monthly economic bulletin as easily as a balance sheet, you will have an edge most investors never bother to build — and it lasts, because it takes patience with dull data rather than a hot tip.

Lesson 4.1 — The Architecture of Nepal's Balance of Payments

The balance of payments (BOP) is the double-entry record of every transaction between Nepal and the rest of the world over a period — a fiscal year, or the monthly slices NRB publishes in its "Current Macroeconomic and Financial Situation" reports. Think of it as the country's household account book with the outside world: what comes in, what goes out. It has three main parts. You will never need to calculate one yourself, but you do need to understand all three, because the press, NRB statements and brokers' commentary mention them constantly, often loosely — and loose use is where investment mistakes creep in.

The current account records trade in goods; trade in services (tourism, transport, IT exports); primary income (interest and profit flows); and — crucially for Nepal — secondary income, which is where workers' remittances are recorded. Nepal runs a large, permanent deficit on goods trade: it imports far more than it exports. That comes from a narrow manufacturing base, dependence on imported petroleum, and growing demand for imported vehicles, electronics and building materials. What stops the current account from collapsing is remittance income. In many periods it is big enough to turn a huge goods deficit into an overall current account surplus. In fiscal year 2025/26, NRB's annual figures show goods imports of about Rs 2,096 billion against exports of only about Rs 315 billion — a trade deficit of about Rs 1,781 billion, with exports covering only 15 percent of imports. Yet remittances over the same year were about Rs 2,363 billion — enough on their own to swing the current account to a surplus of about Rs 924 billion. That single fact — remittances regularly exceeding the whole goods trade gap — is the beam that holds up Nepal's external accounts. Lesson 4.2 explores it fully.

The capital and financial account records investment across borders: foreign direct investment (FDI) into Nepali companies; portfolio investment (foreigners buying Nepali securities, which is tiny because foreign access to NEPSE is restricted); loans from abroad to the government and private sector; and grant aid. Nepal's financial account is thin compared with its neighbours. FDI is small and swings from year to year. Foreign borrowing is mostly cheap loans from the World Bank and the Asian Development Bank, not commercial markets. Grants rise and fall with donor politics. This thinness matters. Nepal cannot easily cover a current account shortfall by attracting short-term foreign money, as some emerging markets do. When the current account weakens, there is no big capital cushion waiting — the shock lands straight on reserves.

The overall balance of payments is the current account plus the capital and financial account. Its mirror image is the change in NRB's foreign exchange reserves. A BOP surplus means reserves are growing; a deficit means reserves are being used to settle Nepal's net bills abroad. This is the master variable of the chapter. Reserves — and especially how many months of imports they can pay for — decide whether NRB tightens or eases policy. And NRB policy is what moves NEPSE, through credit.

KEY CONCEPT In simple terms, for Nepal: current account balance + capital and financial account balance = change in foreign exchange reserves. Remittances are counted inside the current account. So a remittance boom directly strengthens the balance of payments and swells reserves, and a remittance slowdown directly weakens both — like a family whose savings rise and fall with the money a son sends home from Qatar.

One more structural point before moving on. Nepal has a pegged exchange rate: the Nepali rupee is fixed to the Indian rupee at 1.60 NPR per 1 INR, and it moves against other currencies only as the Indian rupee does. This peg is why the BOP and reserves matter so much more in Nepal than in a country with a floating currency. Under a float, a current account deficit tends to fix itself. The currency falls, imports get dearer, exports get cheaper abroad, and the deficit shrinks — with reserves largely untouched. Under Nepal's peg, that automatic valve is switched off. NRB is committed to holding the peg. So whenever demand for foreign currency exceeds supply, NRB must sell foreign currency — effectively dollars — at the fixed rate, and it pays for that from reserves. When reserves run low, NRB cannot simply let the exchange rate adjust. It has to act on the amount of credit and imports instead. That is the mechanical reason credit becomes NRB's main lever whenever the external accounts are under stress — and why Nepali monetary policy often moves more abruptly than in countries with floating currencies that business news sometimes loosely compares it with.

Lesson 4.2 — Remittances: The Load-Bearing Wall of the External Sector

No single data series matters more to the Nepali economy, and so to NEPSE, than workers' remittances. Relative to the size of its economy, Nepal is among the most remittance-dependent countries in the world. For over a decade, remittances have equalled somewhere between a quarter and a third of GDP, depending on the year and the exchange rate. In FY 2025/26 they reached about 36 percent: roughly Rs 2,363 billion against a GDP the National Statistics Office projected at about Rs 6,600 billion. Only a few countries, mostly small Pacific and Central Asian states, depend on remittances this much. This is not a curiosity. It means the spending power of a large share of Nepali households, the deposits in the banking system and, in the end, the money available to buy NEPSE shares all depend on jobs abroad — in the Gulf states, Malaysia and, increasingly, South Korea, Japan and parts of Europe — rather than on what Nepal itself produces.

Remittances reach NEPSE through the banks. A dollar earned in Doha or Kuala Lumpur is converted to rupees and paid into a Nepali bank account, by the worker or a family member. That deposit becomes part of the base banks lend against. Some is spent on daily needs, some on land, some on education — and a portion, historically meaningful and watched by NRB, goes into shares, bought with cash or margin loans. So remittance strength feeds NEPSE through two channels at once: it enlarges the deposits that let banks lend (including margin loans against shares), and a slice of it is invested directly. Both channels shrink when remittance growth stalls, and both grow when it speeds up — exactly what happened, first in reverse and then in recovery, across the 2022 crisis and the 2025/26 rebound examined later in this chapter.

Recent trends have been unusually good. In the first eight months of fiscal year 2025/26 (to mid-March 2026), remittances reached about Rs 1,449.65 billion — up 37.7 percent in rupees and about 31 percent in dollars on the year before, an exceptional pace for Nepal. By the eleven-month mark, the total was about Rs 2,120.80 billion, up about 38.2 percent. The full year closed at about Rs 2,363 billion, up 37.1 percent in rupees and 28.1 percent in dollars (to about USD 16.2 billion). In the first two months of FY 2026/27 growth slowed, to 23.9 percent in rupees and 13.5 percent in dollars — still strong, but a slowdown worth watching, as Lesson 4.5 explains. Several things explain the jump. There is a large and still growing number of Nepalis working abroad, especially in the Gulf. Gulf job markets and construction tied to regional infrastructure have stayed strong. The gap between the official exchange rate and the informal hundi rate has narrowed, pushing more money through banks instead of hundi — helped by digital remittance apps and NRB's drive to bring transfers into the formal system. And a relatively weaker rupee means each dollar earned abroad turns into more rupees at home.

WATCH FOR A slowdown in remittance growth toward single digits, or an outright fall, is one of the earliest warnings a NEPSE investor can get of a coming cash squeeze. NRB publishes the figure every month, and the market is usually slow to connect it with share-market money until credit has already tightened. (Chapter 0.1's test found that deposit growth, which remittances feed, tended to move about one quarter ahead of NEPSE, and the interbank rate about two quarters ahead.)

This dependence carries real risk, and a disciplined investor should treat it as a risk, not a permanent tailwind. Remittances depend on foreign demand for labour, which Nepal does not control. Several things could slow them over a few years: a Gulf building slowdown caused by lower oil prices; a change in a host country's immigration rules; Nepali workers being replaced by cheaper labour from other countries; or fewer young Nepalis choosing to work abroad than in the past. Because remittances are the biggest offset to Nepal's permanent trade deficit, any lasting slowdown feeds straight into a weaker current account, pressure on reserves and — following the chain in Lesson 4.4 — tighter credit that eventually hits NEPSE.

WARNING Do not treat today's very fast remittance growth as permanent. Part of it comes from money switching from hundi to banks, and part from a weaker rupee turning each dollar into more rupees. Neither can go on for ever. Only the rest comes from more workers abroad earning more. Before you use remittance growth to forecast NEPSE liquidity, ask how much of it is the lasting part.

Lesson 4.3 — Foreign Exchange Reserves and the Import Cover Discipline

Foreign exchange reserves are the stock of foreign currency — mostly US dollars, with smaller amounts of other major currencies and gold — that NRB holds to pay Nepal's bills abroad: imports, foreign debt and the defence of the rupee peg described in Lesson 4.1. The most important number derived from them, which every NEPSE investor should track as routinely as the NEPSE index, is import cover: how many months of goods and services imports today's reserves could pay for if no more foreign currency came in. Think of it as a household's emergency fund measured in months of expenses.

Import cover (months) = Gross Foreign Exchange Reserves ÷ Average Monthly Merchandise and Services Import Bill

NRB's own monetary policy treats seven months of import cover as the minimum safe buffer — well above the IMF's traditional rule of thumb of three months for an import-dependent country without deep capital markets or a floating currency. Below that line, a country is seen as externally vulnerable: a shock to exports, remittances or world commodity prices could leave it unable to pay for essential imports — fuel and food above all, for Nepal — without emergency action. NRB tracks this ratio openly and publishes it in every economic bulletin, and in practice it works almost like a policy trigger. When import cover falls well below seven months, NRB has historically reached for the tools in Lesson 4.4 — credit tightening, import curbs and rate rises — whatever the state of domestic growth or the share market.

REGULATORY DETAIL NRB treats import cover below seven months as the zone that calls for corrective action, in line with international guidelines on how much reserve a country needs. This is not just a textbook line. NRB's toughest credit squeezes, including the 2022 episode in Lesson 4.4, came when cover fell to or below it.

By mid-June 2022, eleven months into FY 2021/22, Nepal's reserves had fallen to about Rs 1,176 billion — only about 6.6 months of goods-and-services import cover, the lowest in well over a decade and below NRB's own seven-month line. (The fiscal year closed slightly better, at about Rs 1,216 billion and 6.9 months.) Pressure came from both sides. On the outflow side, the trade deficit jumped about 25 percent on the year. World commodity prices spiked — petroleum costs alone rose an estimated 89 percent compared with the year before, as the Russia-Ukraine war hit energy markets — and a stronger US dollar made every import dearer in rupees. On the inflow side, remittance growth stalled at under 4 percent for the first eleven months (the full year closed at 4.8 percent, with remittances of Rs 1,007 billion), far below the double-digit growth Nepal was used to. Foreign grants fell sharply, from about Rs 26.8 billion to about Rs 14.1 billion, and FDI barely grew. A widening deficit paid for by weakening inflows is the pattern that drains reserves fastest. Learn it as the classic stress pattern. It will come back in some future cycle, even if the trigger next time is not a war-driven energy shock.

By contrast, by the end of fiscal year 2025/26 (mid-July 2026), reserves had rebuilt to about Rs 3,898 billion (USD 25.3 billion) — 19.6 months of goods-and-services import cover, or 23 months counting goods imports alone. That is nearly three times the safe minimum, and among the strongest positions Nepal has recorded. How it happened is telling. The recovery was driven almost entirely by the remittance surge described in Lesson 4.2, not by any big change in exports or a smaller trade deficit. In fact, the trade deficit widened further, by about 16.6 percent, to about Rs 1,781 billion for the year. That is a key point for any analyst. A comfortable import cover can sit alongside — and can be produced entirely by — an ever-wider trade deficit, as long as remittances grow faster. The headline "reserves at record high" is reassuring for now, but it does not mean Nepal's ability to compete in trade has improved. It has not. The reserve buffer depends on wages earned abroad, not on what Nepal produces and sells.

CAUTION Strong reserves built on remittances are not the same as strong reserves built on exports or careful government spending. The first kind can reverse quickly if jobs abroad dry up; the second kind tends to last. Before deciding that today's reserves are safe for the long run, look at where the surplus comes from, not just whether there is one.

Lesson 4.4 — The Transmission Mechanism: From BOP Stress to Credit Crunch to NEPSE Correction

This lesson is the hinge of the chapter, and it links straight back to the credit-cycle ideas in Chapters 0.1 and 0.2. The key claim is this. Because Nepal defends a fixed exchange rate and cannot use a falling currency as its main safety valve, whenever the balance of payments weakens and import cover drops toward or below seven months, NRB is forced to squeeze domestic credit and import demand directly through monetary policy. That squeeze reaches NEPSE through the same credit mechanics described earlier: higher policy rates, tighter liquidity, less room for margin loans and, in severe cases, direct limits on the loans that fund share buying.

The 2022 episode is the clearest illustrated case in Nepal's recent history, and it is worth walking through step by step because the sequence will very likely repeat, in some variant, in a future cycle.

Step one: an outside shock, and slowing inflows. As Lesson 4.3 showed, the trade deficit widened sharply in FY 2021/22 because of a global commodity price shock (petroleum costs up an estimated 89 percent on the year), just as remittance growth stalled below 4 percent and grants collapsed. Reserves fell to about 6.6 months of import cover.

Step two: administrative and monetary response. Policy moved on two fronts at once. On the administrative front, the Ministry of Finance and NRB banned imports of a list of non-essential and luxury goods (vehicles, some electronics and other discretionary items) from late April 2022 — at first until the end of the fiscal year, then extended again and again. The last categories were freed only on 6 December 2022, about seven months later, partly to meet IMF programme conditions. The aim was simply to save foreign currency. On the monetary front, NRB's FY 2022/23 policy raised the rate corridor sharply: the repo rate from 5.5 to 7 percent and the bank rate from 7 to 8.5 percent — a full 1.5 percentage points. It also raised the cash reserve ratio from 3 to 4 percent, mechanically shrinking the deposits banks could lend. And it cut the private-sector credit growth target from about 19 percent to 12.6 percent, a deliberate choice to choke off the domestic demand — including demand for import loans — that was draining reserves.

Step three: the squeeze reaches the banks and NEPSE. A higher CRR and higher rates tightened interbank liquidity at once and pushed up deposit and loan rates across the system. Banks were hard up against the credit-to-deposit (CD) ratio ceiling — 90 percent since it replaced the older credit-to-core-capital-cum-deposit (CCD) ratio in 2021–22 — and had less to lend. They cut back on every kind of discretionary lending, margin loans against shares among the first. Investors who had borrowed to buy NEPSE shares in the easy-money years faced margin calls they could not easily refinance, and were forced to sell into a falling market. At the same time, higher deposit and bond yields made fixed deposits and debentures more attractive than shares for the risk, pulling money away from NEPSE even from investors under no margin pressure. Get the order right. NEPSE's fall had already begun a year earlier, in August 2021, as banks hit their lending ceiling and NRB capped margin loans; by mid-July 2022 the index was already near 2,000, down more than a third. The reserve crisis and the July 2022 tightening then deepened the fall, to about 1,815 on 25 September 2022 — roughly 43 percent below the 2021 peak. Commentators then and since have blamed the crash mainly on credit and NRB's tightening, not on any collapse in listed companies' profits. Company fundamentals had not fallen anywhere near as much. What collapsed was the money and credit available to hold and finance shares — exactly the credit-cycle mechanism that Chapters 0.1 and 0.2 identify as the main driver of NEPSE cycles.

CASE IN POINT Nepal's 2022 reserve crisis is a textbook example of the chain from balance of payments to NEPSE. Import cover fell to 6.6 months. NRB answered with a 1.5-percentage-point rate rise, a CRR increase from 3 to 4 percent, and a cut in its credit growth target from 19 to 12.6 percent. NEPSE — already falling since its August 2021 peak because of the credit squeeze — slid further, to a trough about 43 percent below that peak in September 2022. The fall came overwhelmingly from credit and cash conditions, not from any similar collapse in company profits.

A careful investor should note one detail: not every part of NRB's 2022 package was bad for shares. The same statement that raised rates and the CRR also relaxed the margin lending caps. The "4/12" rule — Rs 40 million per institution and Rs 120 million across all institutions — was eased by dropping the per-institution cap, leaving a single Rs 120 million combined ceiling. That technically allowed bigger margin loans from one bank, even as overall liquidity tightened. The detail matters. NRB's tools do not always point the same way for NEPSE. Defending reserves (less credit overall) and other goals (wider access to margin loans, supporting brokers and banks) can pull in different directions within one statement. Reading any one tool on its own, without checking the overall effect on liquidity, can mislead you.

REGULATORY DETAIL NRB revises its margin-lending rules from time to time. The FY 2021/22 "4/12" rule allowed Rs 4 crore per institution and Rs 12 crore across all of them. It was later simplified to a single combined ceiling, raised in steps (Rs 12 crore, Rs 15 crore, then Rs 25 crore) and removed altogether in October 2025. These rules are a separate lever from the CRR and the rate corridor, and the two can point in opposite directions in the same policy statement. Check both before deciding whether credit for share investors is getting tighter or looser.

The recovery followed the same logic in reverse. As remittances surged from 2024 into 2025 and 2026, and reserves rebuilt past 15, then 18, then 22-plus months of import cover, the emergency that had justified tight policy faded. Later monetary policies relaxed credit growth limits, policy rates were cut over the following years (the cash reserve ratio stayed at 4 percent), and liquidity — seen in interbank rates and the CD ratio — loosened a great deal. NEPSE's recovery from its 2022–23 lows followed this loosening with a delay, just as the credit-cycle pattern elsewhere in this book predicts. Easing shows up in bank lending capacity and interbank rates within a quarter or two. It takes longer — often two to four quarters — to show up as steady NEPSE trading and rising prices, because investors' appetite for risk and their margin capacity rebuild more slowly than raw liquidity.

Lesson 4.5 — Reading the Signals: A Practical Framework for Investors

The last three lessons gave the theory and the history. This one turns them into a checklist. A disciplined NEPSE investor should get into the habit of checking five external indicators. NRB publishes all of them monthly or periodically, free, and they usually move well before the same signal shows up in NEPSE prices or brokers' commentary.

Import cover and reserve trend. Pull NRB's "Current Macroeconomic and Financial Situation" report — issued roughly monthly, cumulatively through the fiscal year — and track the reported months of import cover against the seven-month threshold discussed in Lesson 4.3. Falling cover approaching single digits from a comfortable level, even before it crosses seven months outright, is the earliest actionable signal in this entire framework, because NRB has historically begun signalling policy intent well before the ratio becomes acute.

Remittance growth, in both rupees and dollars. Watch the rate of change, not the level — the level will almost always be positive, given how many Nepalis work abroad. A slowdown in year-on-year growth is the early warning of reserve stress about two to three quarters ahead, because slower remittances take time to build into a clearly weaker BOP.

Trade deficit growth relative to remittance growth. The two series should be read against each other, not in isolation. A widening trade deficit is tolerable, even benign for reserve purposes, so long as remittance growth is outpacing it, as has been the case through 2025/26. The moment trade deficit growth begins to outpace remittance growth — which is exactly the pattern that preceded the 2022 crisis — is the moment the external accounts flip from strengthening to weakening.

Policy rate corridor and CRR settings, published with every quarterly monetary policy review and its periodic updates. A tightening bias here — rate hikes, CRR increases, or credit growth ceiling reductions — is the direct mechanical transmission point into bank lending capacity and, with a lag, into NEPSE liquidity.

The interbank lending rate and the banking system's overall CD ratio. These are the fastest live gauges of liquidity. They update far more often than the quarterly policy statements, and they usually move before the policy rate itself changes, because NRB's market operations and standing facilities respond to liquidity all the time, not just on review dates.

PRACTICAL TOOL Keep a simple watchlist and update it each month from NRB's bulletin: (1) months of import cover; (2) remittance growth, in rupees and dollars; (3) trade deficit growth compared with remittance growth; (4) the rate corridor and CRR; (5) the interbank rate and the CD ratio. Three or more of these worsening together is a much stronger signal than any one alone. In 2021–22, several of them worsened together before the deepest part of the NEPSE fall.

One important point when using this framework: the signal is lopsided in urgency. A weakening external position, once it crosses NRB's comfort lines, tends to bring quick action. Rate rises and CRR increases can be announced and take effect in a single policy statement, and import bans can be imposed by government order within days. A strengthening position tends to be eased into policy slowly and cautiously, because NRB does not want to loosen credit fast and reignite the imbalances that caused the last tightening. So if you are positioning for a NEPSE recovery on an improving BOP, expect easing to arrive more slowly and grudgingly than the tightening did on the way down. Build that into your position sizes and timing, rather than expecting a mirror-image, equally fast recovery.

WATCH FOR NRB moves unevenly: it tightens fast and hard when reserves are in trouble, but eases slowly and carefully as they rebuild, for fear of restarting the same import and credit pressures. So do not expect a NEPSE recovery built on better external accounts to be as fast as the fall that came before it.

Lesson 4.6 — Current Position and Forward Risks

On NRB's full-year figures for FY 2025/26, Nepal's external position is unusually strong. Foreign exchange reserves reached about Rs 3,898 billion (USD 25.3 billion) by mid-July 2026 — 19.6 months of goods-and-services import cover, or 23 months of goods imports. Remittances grew 37.1 percent in rupees. The current account posted a surplus of about Rs 924 billion, even though the trade deficit widened to about Rs 1,781 billion. Inflation averaged about 3.1 percent over the year. Together, these have given NRB plenty of room to keep credit loose compared with the sharp tightening of 2022, and NEPSE's multi-year recovery since its 2022–23 lows has broadly followed that easier backdrop, as this chapter and Chapters 0.1 and 0.2 would predict.

The first data for FY 2026/27, though, show some of the early signals in Lesson 4.5 starting to turn. None is alarming yet, but together they deserve attention:

• Remittance growth slowed to 23.9 percent in rupees, and 13.5 percent in dollars, in the first two months (to mid-September 2026), from 37.1 percent the year before.

• Import cover eased to 17.8 months of goods and services, from 19.6 months in mid-July. Reserves still rose, to about Rs 4,066 billion; the dip came because goods imports jumped 31.6 percent. Fast-rising imports with slowing remittances is the combination Lesson 4.5 warns about.

• Year-on-year inflation rose to 5.1 percent by mid-July and 6.3 percent by mid-September 2026, from about 2 percent a year earlier.

• In October 2026, the Reserve Bank of India raised its main rate from 5.25 to 5.50 percent. Because of the rupee peg, that adds pressure on NRB to stop easing (Chapter 1 explains why).

Reserves are still nearly three times NRB's safe line, so there is no sign of a 2022-style squeeze. But the direction of these signals has changed, and the five-indicator watchlist exists for exactly this moment.

Your job, though, is not to assume today's comfortable position will last forever. It is to identify the specific pressure points that could reverse it — 2022 showed how fast a stable-looking position can crumble once its supports change. Four risks deserve tracking.

First, remittances come from too few places. Nepal's migrant workers are concentrated in a few Gulf countries and Malaysia, whose demand for labour depends on oil prices, regional building cycles and immigration decisions made entirely outside Nepal. A slowdown in Gulf construction, a shift toward machines or toward cheaper workers from other countries, or a cut in foreign-labour quotas in any of Nepal's top two or three destinations would show up first in remittance growth. Under the framework in Lesson 4.5, that would be an early warning worth acting on well before it shows up in NEPSE prices.

Second, Nepal's exports are weak. The trade deficit keeps growing even while the current account shows a surplus, so the whole external cushion depends on remittances growing faster than an ever-bigger import bill — not on Nepali exports becoming more competitive. That is a fragile foundation. Remittance growth has to keep speeding up just to keep pace, let alone to strengthen reserves further. Even a plateau in remittance growth, without any actual fall, would shrink the current account surplus and eventually turn it into a deficit, given where the trade deficit is heading.

Third, world commodity prices, especially oil. Nepal imports most of its petroleum, and 2022 showed how a global energy shock (petroleum costs up an estimated 89 percent in a year then) can widen the trade deficit fast enough to swamp even healthy remittances. Any new shock to world energy markets reaches Nepal's reserves quickly and directly — faster than most domestic news an investor might be watching.

Fourth, the politics of the response. How fast and how hard NRB tightened in 2022 depended on how severe the crisis was, and on pressure from international lenders and rating concerns. Future episodes may play out differently, depending on the political climate, the government's finances and NRB's leadership. Do not assume the exact 2022 toolkit and timing — the CRR rise, the rate corridor hike, the import bans — will be repeated in a future crisis. The direction of the response (tightening) is reliable. Its size, speed and specific tools may differ.

CAUTION Today's comfortable reserves (about 18 to 23 months of import cover) are real, and they support cash conditions for NEPSE in the near term. But they rest on an unusually strong, and possibly temporary, burst of remittance growth on top of a trade deficit that keeps widening. They do not mean Nepal's external weakness has been solved for good. Keep checking the five indicators in Lesson 4.5 instead of treating today's position as permanent.

For this book's purpose — analysing NEPSE — the lesson is not that you should forecast Gulf job markets or oil prices yourself. That is well beyond share analysis. It is that the external indicators in Lesson 4.5 are an early-warning system — public, free and widely ignored — for the credit conditions that, as this book argues, drive NEPSE's turning points. A retail investor who checks NRB's monthly bulletin as regularly as the NEPSE index has a real, lasting information advantage over most investors, who react only after tightening is already showing up as falling prices and margin calls.

Chapter recap

Nepal's balance of payments depends on remittances to offset a large and growing goods trade deficit, and this dependence — not the fundamentals of listed companies — is the biggest outside force shaping NEPSE's liquidity cycles. Because the rupee is pegged to the Indian rupee instead of floating to absorb shocks, any weakening in the balance of payments falls straight on foreign exchange reserves and forces NRB to respond by tightening credit rather than letting the currency adjust. That makes credit the main path from external stress to share-market stress. Seven months of import cover works as NRB's practical policy trigger. In 2022, when cover fell to 6.6 months, the result was a textbook sequence: a 1.5-point policy rate rise, a higher CRR, lower credit growth limits and import bans, which fed through the banks into margin calls and forced selling and deepened a NEPSE fall that had begun with the 2021 credit squeeze, to roughly 43 percent below the August 2021 peak by September 2022. The later rebuild of reserves, driven mainly by remittance growth of about 37 percent in FY 2025/26, pushed import cover to 23 months of goods imports (19.6 months counting goods and services) and let NRB ease gradually; NEPSE's recovery has followed with the usual delay. In the first months of FY 2026/27, remittance growth has slowed, import cover has eased and inflation has risen — early signals worth tracking. Keep a five-indicator watchlist — import cover, remittance growth momentum, trade deficit growth versus remittance growth, the policy rate and CRR, and the interbank rate and CD ratio — and check it as routinely as company results. And do not mistake today's comfortable reserves for a problem solved for good: they rest on remittance growth exposed to Gulf and Malaysian job markets, on top of a trade deficit that keeps widening whatever the headline current account surplus says.
