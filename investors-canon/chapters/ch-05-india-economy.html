How India’s Economy Affects NEPSE

Every trading morning, before a single share changes hands on NEPSE, a quieter process has already set the terms of the day. Nepal Rastra Bank has checked its books against the Reserve Bank of India (RBI). The money changers on New Road and in Birgunj have posted their rates from a fixed formula. And the price of almost everything — diesel, steel rods, cooking oil — has been partly decided in Mumbai and New Delhi before it reaches a Kathmandu invoice. A Nepali investor who studies only NEPSE's own charts is reading half a book. The other half is written in India: in the RBI's interest rate decisions, in how strong or weak the Indian rupee is against the dollar, in the harvests of Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, and in the flow of Indian money and goods across an open, 1,751-kilometre border. This chapter teaches you to read that other half. It explains how the peg tying the Nepali rupee to the Indian rupee works; how Indian monetary policy and Indian economic conditions reach Nepal's banks, trade, remittances and share market; and which Indian indicators to watch as early signals for NEPSE.

Lesson 5.1 — The Peg: What It Is and Why It Exists

The Nepali rupee (NPR) has been fixed to the Indian rupee (INR) at NPR 1.60 per INR 1 since February 1993. That rate grew out of an earlier peg from the 1960s, when both currencies were valued against gold and, later, the US dollar. It is not a market price in any real sense. It is an administrative anchor set by Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB), and it has not moved in more than three decades, even though Nepal's economy, India's economy and every other currency have changed enormously. When the Indian rupee falls against the US dollar, the Nepali rupee falls against the dollar by exactly the same proportion, because the NPR/INR rate is fixed. And when the RBI steps in to defend the Indian rupee, it is — whether anyone in Mumbai thinks about it or not — defending the Nepali rupee too.

This is a fixed exchange rate regime in the strictest sense: one-sided, unilateral, and asymmetric. Nepal pegs to India; India does not peg to Nepal, and Indian monetary authorities set policy with zero reference to conditions in Kathmandu. The relationship is entirely one of dependency. Understanding why Nepal accepts this arrangement — and why, periodically, prominent economists and even NRB officials openly debate whether it should continue — is the necessary starting point for understanding how India's economy reaches into the Nepali stock market.

KEY CONCEPT A currency peg is a promise: the central bank will buy or sell its own currency at a fixed rate against another currency, as much as people want. NRB's promise covers the Indian rupee only, at NPR 1.60 per INR 1. Against the US dollar, the euro or the Chinese yuan, the Nepali rupee moves — but exactly in step with the Indian rupee. Like a small boat tied to a big one, it goes wherever the big boat goes. Nepal has handed its exchange rate policy to India by design, not by accident.

The reasons are practical, not sentimental. Nepal is landlocked. Almost all its sea-borne trade — most of its fuel, machinery, consumer goods and raw materials — passes through Indian ports at Kolkata and Visakhapatnam before reaching Nepal by road or rail. A very large share of Nepal's trade is with India itself. Labour migration deepens the tie: hundreds of thousands of Nepalis work in India under the open-border arrangement of the 1950 Treaty of Peace and Friendship, sending money home in Indian rupees. A fixed rate with India removes currency risk from this dense web of trade and remittances, which is exactly how NRB and successive governments have defended it since 1960.

Keeping the peg working depends on how NRB manages its foreign exchange reserves. NRB keeps a large share of Nepal's reserves — often estimated at about a fifth; NRB's reports give the exact figure from time to time — in Indian rupees, separate from its "convertible currency" reserves (dollars, euros and similar) used for trade with the rest of the world. It tops up its Indian rupee reserves by selling convertible currency to the RBI for Indian rupees, under an agreement between the two central banks. This is not a market trade; it works more like a standing swap arrangement that exists to keep the peg defensible. When Nepal's Indian rupee reserves run low compared with demand for Indian goods, NRB must sell dollars to buy rupees — using up the convertible reserves that pay for everything Nepal imports from outside India.

REGULATORY DETAIL NRB measures whether its reserves are big enough in months of import cover. A fall is a sign of strain on the peg. When reserves have fallen too far, NRB has tightened import rules, restricted some goods, or made importers put up more cash for letters of credit, to save foreign currency. Each of these squeezes the profits of trading companies and spending on non-essentials, which shows up in NEPSE-listed companies' results within a quarter or two.

What this means for an investor is that Nepal has, in effect, handed its exchange rate policy, and a good part of its monetary policy, to India. NRB still sets its own policy rate, its own rules on bank capital and liquidity, and its own safety rules for the financial system. But it does so within limits largely set by what the RBI does, because money can move across an open border, and if Nepali rates drift too far from India's, NRB is eventually forced to act on rates or reserves. Lesson 5.2 covers this.

Lesson 5.2 — Monetary Policy Without Full Independence

In principle, Nepal Rastra Bank is a sovereign central bank with its own mandate: price stability, external sector stability, and financial sector stability, pursued through its own policy rate corridor, its own reserve requirements, and its own open market operations. In practice, the fixed peg to the Indian rupee means NRB's room to run an independent interest rate policy is narrower than a textbook reading of central bank independence would suggest. This is the classic "impossible trinity" of international macroeconomics: a country cannot simultaneously maintain a fixed exchange rate, free capital mobility, and independent monetary policy. Nepal has chosen the fixed exchange rate and has an open border with India that makes capital controls difficult to enforce in practice, even where they exist on paper. The residual — independent monetary policy — is the trinity's casualty.

In practice, when the RBI raises or cuts its policy repo rate, NRB comes under pressure to move Nepali rates in roughly the same direction, or risk money flowing across the border. If Indian rates rise well above Nepali rates, rupee deposits and investments in India look more attractive. Across an open border with a shared culture and language, money can drift toward India — draining the very Indian rupee reserves that hold up the peg. If NRB keeps rates too low for too long while the RBI tightens, the pressure shows up first as falling reserves, then as import restrictions, and, if ignored, as a real balance-of-payments crisis. That is what happened in 2022, when a post-pandemic surge in imports combined with a strong dollar (and so a weaker rupee against the dollar, because of the peg) to squeeze reserves to levels that triggered emergency import bans and a sharp round of NRB rate rises.

CASE IN POINT Through late 2021 and the first half of 2022, Nepal's foreign exchange reserves fell to 6.6 months of import cover by mid-June 2022 — below the seven-month line NRB treats as a warning. NRB responded with a battery of measures: bans on importing vehicles and other non-essential goods, more cash up front for letters of credit, and sharp rises in its policy rate. NEPSE had peaked in August 2021 and was already falling, because banks had hit their lending ceiling and margin loans had been capped. The reserve squeeze and the rate rises then deepened the fall, to about 43 percent below the peak by September 2022, as loan rates jumped, margin lending shrank, and rate-sensitive banks and hydropower companies were re-priced lower. It is the clearest recent example of an external shock under the peg feeding into share prices within months.

So RBI policy reaches NEPSE mainly through two in-between steps: the reserve constraint just described, and the direct comparison between Nepali and Indian interest rates, which affects whether money moves across the border. When the RBI cuts rates to support growth, Nepal usually gains room to ease too, because pressure on reserves and the pull of money toward India both fade. When the RBI raises rates to fight inflation, NRB usually feels it must follow after a delay, even if Nepal's own conditions would not call for it. An investor who watches only NRB's press releases is watching the symptom, not the cause. The RBI's Monetary Policy Committee decisions, roughly every two months, are the better early signal.

WATCH FOR The RBI's Monetary Policy Committee meets about every two months on a published schedule. If you hold rate-sensitive shares — commercial banks, development banks, finance companies or heavily indebted hydropower developers — treat each RBI decision as a dated event that can shape NRB's next move. NRB announces its yearly policy in mid-July and reviews it during the year, with the first review around Mangsir (November–December) and the mid-term review around Falgun (February–March).

Do not overstate the link. NRB does not move in automatic lockstep with the RBI; Nepal's own inflation, credit growth and government finances also shape its decisions. But the peg makes NRB's independence conditional and limited, not absolute. NRB's own policy documents confirm this again and again: they regularly list the state of foreign exchange reserves and the gap between Indian and Nepali interest rates as explicit factors in setting the policy rate.

The latest cycle shows the link at work. Through 2025 the RBI cut its repo rate four times, by 1.25 percentage points in all, from 6.50 to 5.25 percent by December 2025. Over the same period NRB cut its policy rate from 5.00 to 4.25 percent. Both central banks were easing together, as the peg would lead you to expect. Then, in October 2026, the RBI reversed course: it raised its rate to 5.50 percent and said further cuts were off the table for now, because Indian inflation was no longer as low as before. Nepal's own inflation had already risen to 6.3 percent by mid-September 2026. This is exactly the situation this lesson describes, and NRB's first-quarter review of FY 2026/27, due around Mangsir, will show how it responds.

Interest rate pass-through in Nepal

NRB's research department has studied interest rate pass-through: how quickly and fully a change in the policy rate shows up in the deposit and loan rates banks actually charge. The finding that matters to a NEPSE investor is that pass-through in Nepal is slow and incomplete compared with developed systems. A change in the policy rate usually takes several quarters to show up fully in banks' average loan rates, and how much passes through depends on how tight liquidity is at the time. That changes how you should read an RBI move. An RBI rate change does not instantly change NEPSE-listed banks' interest margins the following week. The effect comes later, comes partly, and comes harder when domestic liquidity is already tight than when banks are full of deposits and can absorb a change without repricing their loans much. So if you follow the banking sub-index, pair any RBI or NRB rate move with a check of current bank liquidity — the interbank rate and NRB's reported credit-to-deposit ratio — before assuming the market has priced it in.

WATCH FOR A rate move that lands when Nepali banks are short of cash (a high credit-to-deposit ratio, a high interbank rate) passes into loan rates faster and more fully than the same move landing when banks are flush. So the same RBI decision can hit NEPSE's bank shares quite differently depending on conditions at home. Today, with the interbank rate below NRB's floor and the CD ratio around 73 percent, Nepali banks are flush — which should soften the first effect of India's October 2026 rate rise.

Lesson 5.3 — Trade Dependency and the Import Bill Channel

Beyond the money mechanics of the peg, India shapes Nepal's economy — and so NEPSE — through the sheer size of trade between them. India is by far Nepal's biggest trading partner: it supplies about 60 percent of Nepal's imports and buys more than four-fifths of its (much smaller) exports. Petroleum, imported only from India under a long-standing deal between Nepal Oil Corporation and Indian Oil Corporation, is one of the largest single items in Nepal's import bill. Because the peg fixes the Nepali rupee to the Indian rupee, Nepal's import bill is exposed to India twice. First, through the amount and price of Indian-made goods — petroleum products, steel, cement clinker, vehicles, medicines and many industrial inputs — which follow Indian inflation. Second, through world prices for dollar-priced commodities such as crude oil, which pass into Indian rupee prices before the goods ever reach Nepal.

WARNING Nepal's widening trade deficit is not just a number in NRB's bulletins. Together with remittances, it decides how much pressure builds on reserves each year. A deficit growing faster than remittances — mostly from imports from India — can lead to NRB tightening to save reserves, or to import curbs that cut straight into the sales of NEPSE-listed traders, car dealers and consumer-goods distributors. In the first two months of FY 2026/27, goods imports jumped 31.6 percent while remittances grew 23.9 percent: worth watching.

Trade dependence reaches NEPSE through several concrete business channels. Listed commercial banks earn a good share of their fee income from trade finance — letters of credit, document collections and remittance services tied to trade with India — so slower India-linked trade cuts into this income. Listed manufacturers and cement companies that depend on Indian clinker, coal or gypsum see their input costs move with Indian prices and Indian export policy. (India has at times restricted exports of sugar, wheat and non-basmati rice, each with visible knock-on effects for Nepali importers and processors.) Hydropower developers — one of NEPSE's largest and most traded sectors — buy much of their electrical and mechanical equipment, transformers and turbine parts from India. So for projects still being built, Indian-rupee costs move with the rupee's value against the dollar, even though the projects earn their revenue in Nepali rupees.

A newer and growing channel is cross-border power trade. Nepal used to only import power from India, especially in the dry season. Since cross-border transmission lines opened and Nepal joined India's real-time electricity market, it has also exported power to India during the monsoon surplus. Listed private producers still sell to the Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA) at their fixed PPA tariffs; it is NEA that exports the surplus, at prices set on India's power markets. So Indian power prices reach NEPSE-listed hydropower companies indirectly: through NEA's finances, its ability to pay producers on time, and how much monsoon spill-over energy it can absorb.

PRACTICAL TOOL If you hold or are studying hydropower shares, track two things besides Nepali data. First, monsoon-season prices on India's power exchanges (IEX and PXIL), which decide what NEA earns on exported surplus power. Second, progress on new cross-border transmission lines, which decides how much surplus can be exported at all. NEA and India's power regulators report both from time to time. Together they tell you more about export income than rainfall data alone.

Consumer goods, cement, and the India price umbrella

A less obvious but lasting channel is what you could call India's price umbrella over Nepali consumer and construction shares. Many finished goods sold in Nepal are either imported from India or made in Nepal from Indian raw materials, and moving goods across the open border is cheap. So Indian shop and wholesale prices act as a soft ceiling on what Nepali producers can charge. A listed Nepali cement, noodle, biscuit or drinks maker that prices well above the landed cost of the Indian equivalent risks losing shelf space to imports, legal or informal. Think of a vegetable seller in Birgunj: if he charges too much, buyers simply walk across the border to the market in Raxaul. This means the pricing power — and so the profit margins — of much of NEPSE's manufacturing and consumer sectors is partly capped by Indian inflation and the rupee's value, not just by Nepali demand or costs. If you are modelling higher margins for a listed consumer company, always ask whether the assumed price rise is realistic given Indian prices for the same product.

Lesson 5.4 — Remittances, the Rupee, and Bank Sector Liquidity

Workers' remittances are one of the biggest single flows into Nepal's economy — between a quarter and a third of GDP in recent years, and about 36 percent in FY 2025/26, among the highest ratios in the world. The largest flows come from the Gulf states and Malaysia, but India is a major and different source. Hundreds of thousands of Nepalis work in India under the open-border treaty, many in informal jobs. Their earnings are harder to capture in official figures than remittances arriving through banks and money transfer companies from the Gulf. So the true India-linked contribution is almost certainly understated in the headline numbers. Much of it arrives as cash carried across the border, through informal hundi networks, or as direct spending by families that never appears in NRB's tables.

For an investor, the exact size matters less than the mechanism. Remittances are the main source of deposit growth for Nepal's banks, and deposit growth is the main limit on how much banks can lend — to businesses, to households and, crucially for NEPSE, to margin borrowers and IPO applicants. When remittances are strong, deposits grow, loan-to-deposit ratios ease, banks compete harder to lend, interest rates on deposits and loans tend to soften, and liquidity improves — including the money available for margin trading and IPO applications. When remittance growth slows, or the rupee's moves against the dollar change workers' decisions about how much to send and when, bank liquidity tightens. NEPSE usually feels it within one or two quarters, through higher loan rates and less room for margin loans.

KEY CONCEPT Because the Nepali rupee is tied to the Indian rupee, and both move together against the US dollar, a weaker rupee automatically means more rupees for each dollar sent home from the Gulf. That partly offsets the damage: the same strong dollar that makes Nepal's petroleum and machinery dearer also swells the rupee value of much of the money flowing in. It is one reason remittances grew 37.1 percent in rupees but only 28.1 percent in dollars in FY 2025/26.

For the India-linked share of remittances, the effect runs the other way. Because the Nepali and Indian rupees are fixed at 1.60 to 1, a Nepali worker in India who sends money home gains or loses nothing from exchange rate moves — the peg fixes the value whatever happens to the dollar. That makes India-sourced remittances a steady, low-volatility part of Nepal's remittance base, unlike the more currency-sensitive Gulf part. This difference is rarely discussed in NEPSE commentary, but it matters when you judge how stable bank deposit growth will be in different currency conditions.

CAUTION Do not treat NRB's total remittance figure as one single number when you think about bank liquidity. Money from India behaves differently: it is steadier, unaffected by the dollar, but sensitive to Indian job markets and to any Indian crackdown on informal cross-border work. Money from the Gulf swings more, and follows oil-country budgets and the dollar.

Remittances and the IPO calendar

One more link ties remittances to a very Nepali market habit: the retail IPO subscription cycle. Nepali households — especially families receiving remittances or with members just back from abroad — have long put a meaningful share of that money into IPO applications and share trading, especially around Dashain and Tihar, when both remittances and household cash peak. Merchant bankers and issue managers know this and often schedule IPOs ahead of, or around, these high-cash periods. So when you judge how strongly an upcoming IPO or further public offering is likely to be subscribed, look not only at the company but also at the latest remittance trend in NRB's balance-of-payments updates, and at where the issue falls in the festival calendar. Strong remittance quarters have tended to come with heavier oversubscription and stronger listing days, and weak quarters with thinner demand and softer debuts — a useful tendency, though not a guarantee.

Lesson 5.5 — Capital Flows, INR Convertibility, and Financial Sector Risk

A newer and less understood channel has opened over the past few years, as the RBI has slowly allowed the Indian rupee to be used more widely abroad — for example, letting some cross-border trade be settled in rupees and allowing rupee lending to neighbouring and partner countries. For Nepal, this cuts both ways. On one side, easier use of the rupee for trade could reduce the friction and the burden on dollar reserves of Nepal's trade with India, since deals that once needed scarce convertible currency could more often settle in rupees through the existing arrangement. On the other side, easier rupee movement and freer flow of Indian money across the open border could make Nepal's already thin capital markets, including NEPSE, more exposed to the swings of Indian investor mood and Indian liquidity, with less of the protection that capital controls have provided until now.

REGULATORY DETAIL Nepal's rules allow foreign investment in NEPSE shares only through specific approved channels, and Indian retail investors cannot freely trade NEPSE shares. But the open, lightly watched border means money has always moved more freely in practice — for property, business and family needs — than the rules suggest on paper. So when the RBI widens the use of the Indian rupee abroad, watch it less for its legal effect on foreign investment rules and more for what it says about how easily money will cross the border in future.

This links straight back to the impossible trinity in Lesson 5.2: a country cannot have free movement of money, a fixed exchange rate and an independent monetary policy all at once. Any increase in how freely money actually moves — through rupee liberalisation, digital payment links, or simply weaker enforcement along the open border — further narrows NRB's room for independent policy, and makes RBI decisions reach Nepal even more strongly. So read news of RBI moves to widen the rupee's international use not as a distant technical change in Indian policy, but as a sign that Indian and Nepali financial conditions are likely to become more tightly linked over time, not less.

A related and more immediate risk runs through the links between Nepali and Indian banks. Nepali commercial banks keep correspondent accounts with Indian banks to handle trade finance and remittance payments. Any tightening of Indian banking rules on such relationships with smaller neighbouring banking systems, or any RBI directive affecting rupee accounts held by foreign banks, has in the past caused short-term delays in Nepal's trade payments. Those delays hit the working capital of NEPSE-listed trading and manufacturing companies that depend on letters of credit being processed on time.

WARNING NEPSE is small, and the share of its stock that actually trades is smaller still. So even a modest shift in informal money across the border — into Nepali land, into NEPSE through nominees, or out of Nepal toward India when Indian shares are booming — can move NEPSE far more than the same amount would move a bigger market. A bucket of water barely changes a lake, but it floods a teacup.

Lesson 5.6 — Reading Indian Indicators as Leading Signals for NEPSE

With the channels laid out — the peg, linked monetary policy, trade dependence, remittances and capital flows — the practical task is to build a short watchlist of Indian indicators that act as early or same-time signals for Nepali market conditions. They sit alongside, not instead of, the Nepali indicators covered elsewhere in this book.

First and most important: the RBI Monetary Policy Committee's repo rate decision, announced every two months on a fixed schedule. A run of rate rises in India makes an NRB tightening more likely within one or two quarters. The October 2026 rise to 5.50 percent is the first step in the opposite direction since the 2025 cuts — one rise is not yet a run, but it is the signal to start watching. That would hit NEPSE's rate-sensitive banks, finance companies and hydropower firms, through higher borrowing costs for indebted companies and less room for retail margin loans.

The second is the rupee-dollar exchange rate itself, since the Nepali rupee moves against the dollar exactly in step with the Indian rupee. A lasting fall of the rupee against the dollar makes Nepal's imports dearer in rupees — especially dollar-priced petroleum and inputs not bought from India — while boosting the rupee value of Gulf remittances. The net effect on any NEPSE sector depends on how much it imports, exports and relies on remittance-fed spending, but the direction of the rupee always matters.

The third is India's own headline and core inflation prints, published monthly by India's Ministry of Statistics and tracked closely by the RBI in its policy deliberations. Indian inflation feeds two ways: directly, through the rupee price of goods Nepal imports from India, and indirectly, as a driver of the RBI's own rate decisions described above.

Fourth: monsoon and harvest data for India's main grain- and sugar-growing states. India sometimes restricts exports of staples to control food prices at home, and these restrictions have repeatedly disrupted the supply and prices of goods Nepal imports, with visible effects on the profits of NEPSE-listed trading and consumer companies.

Fifth: the mood of India's own share market and its foreign investor (FII) flow data. There is no formal link to NEPSE, but it often reflects the wider regional appetite for risk, which also colours informal money moving into and out of Nepal.

PRACTICAL TOOL Keep a simple quarterly dashboard with five rows: the RBI's latest repo rate and its direction over the last two reviews (now: 5.50 percent, after a rise in October 2026); the rupee's level and quarterly change against the US dollar; India's latest headline inflation; NRB's reserves in months of import cover (17.8 months in mid-September 2026); and Nepal's trade deficit for the year so far. Read these five together before reading any NEPSE company's results. They tell you more about the winds facing the market than most local commentary does.

Chapter recap

The Nepali rupee is not independent the way a floating currency is. It has been fixed to the Indian rupee at NPR 1.60 per INR 1 since 1993, and that single fact shapes almost everything in this chapter. Because of the peg, NRB's monetary independence is real but limited: with a fixed rate and an open border, Nepali interest rates cannot stray far from India's without eventually straining reserves. Nepal's heavy trade dependence on India — about 60 percent of imports, over four-fifths of exports, all of its petroleum and a growing power trade — means Indian prices, Indian export rules and Indian power markets feed directly into the costs and revenues of NEPSE-listed banks, traders, manufacturers and hydropower companies. Remittances, worth about a third of GDP, drive bank deposit growth and so the money available for lending and margin trading; the India-sourced share is more stable in currency terms but more sensitive to Indian job markets than the dollar-exposed Gulf share. The RBI's gradual moves to widen the rupee's international use cut both ways: they could ease trade payments with India, but they also tie Indian financial conditions more tightly to a shallow NEPSE. The disciplined response is not to drop domestic analysis but to add a short, regularly updated watchlist of Indian indicators — the RBI's policy rate, the rupee-dollar rate, Indian inflation, Indian harvest and export-policy news, and Indian share-market mood — treating each as a possible early signal for conditions that Nepali data will only confirm later. Right now that watchlist shows India turning toward tighter money just as Nepal's own inflation rises.
