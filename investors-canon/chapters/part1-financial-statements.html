Part I · Chapter 1

Reading the Story a Company Tells

Imagine you are about to buy a cow at the haat bazaar. Before you pay, you check its teeth, look at its udder, watch how it walks. You are not buying a cow — you are buying a cow's health, and the only way to judge that health is to check it carefully first.

Buying a share of a company works the same way. When you buy even one share of a company listed on NEPSE (the Nepal Stock Exchange), you are buying a small piece of that company's future. And just like the cow, you cannot judge that future by looking at the company's building or its friendly manager. You have to check its financial statements — the documents that show whether the company is actually healthy, or just looks healthy from the outside.

This chapter introduces the three financial statements every company publishes, and what each one is really trying to tell you.

A term, defined A financial statement is simply a written report a company prepares — usually every three months and every year — showing what it owns, what it owes, how much it earned, and how much actual cash moved in and out. Think of it as the company's report card, except instead of grades in Mathematics and Science, it has numbers for money.

Three report cards, three different questions

There are three main financial statements, and each one answers a completely different question. Beginners often mix them up because all three are full of numbers, but each is really telling a different kind of story:

  • The balance sheet answers: "What does this company own, and what does it owe, right now, today?" It is a photograph, taken at a single moment.
  • The income statement answers: "How much did this company earn and spend over the last three months, or the last year?" It is the story of a whole season, not a single moment.
  • The cash flow statement answers: "How much actual cash moved in and out of this company's bank account?" It is the company's bank passbook.

We will walk through each one slowly, using a small made-up company called Himal Traders Pvt. Ltd., a fictional trading business based in Kathmandu, so the numbers stay simple and the ideas stay clear.

The balance sheet: a photograph of owning and owing

Think about your own household for a moment. You might own a house, a small plot of land, some gold jewelry passed down from your grandmother, and some cash in a savings account at a bank. These are things you own. In accounting, these are called assets.

At the same time, you might owe money — perhaps a home loan from a bank, or an amount you still owe your local sahu (shopkeeper) after buying groceries on credit (udhaaro) last month. These are things you owe. In accounting, these are called liabilities.

Now subtract what you owe from what you own. Whatever is left over is truly yours, free and clear. In accounting, this leftover amount is called equity — and for a company, it belongs to the shareholders, which is you, if you own shares in that company.

The one equation to remember Assets = Liabilities + Equity. What a company owns is always equal to what it owes to others, plus what it owes to its own shareholders. This equation must always balance — which is exactly why it's called a "balance" sheet.

Here is a simplified balance sheet for Himal Traders Pvt. Ltd., as of Ashad end (mid-July):

Assets (what it owns)NPR (in lakhs)
Cash at bank18
Inventory (goods waiting to be sold)42
Land and building90
Total assets150
Liabilities (what it owes)NPR (in lakhs)
Bank loan50
Owed to suppliers20
Total liabilities70
Equity (owners' share)80

Notice: 70 (liabilities) + 80 (equity) = 150, which matches total assets exactly. That is the balance sheet balancing, just as it must.

The income statement: the season's story of earning and spending

Now picture a paddy farmer in the Terai at the end of a growing season. During the season, the farmer sold the harvested rice for a certain amount of money. That is revenue — money earned from the main business.

But growing that rice was not free. The farmer paid for seeds, fertilizer, and labor to plant and harvest. These are expenses — the cost of running the business.

Subtract the expenses from the revenue, and whatever remains is profit (also called net income) — the actual gain the farmer walks away with after the whole season. If expenses were larger than revenue instead, the farmer would have a loss.

Here is Himal Traders' income statement for the full fiscal year:

NPR (in lakhs)
Revenue (goods sold)220
Cost of goods sold150
Gross profit70
Other expenses (rent, salaries, electricity)40
Net profit30

Two profit numbers appear here on purpose. Gross profit is what's left after only subtracting the direct cost of the goods themselves — like the farmer's seed and fertilizer cost. Net profit is what's left after subtracting everything else too — rent for the shop, salaries for staff, electricity bills. Net profit is the true bottom line.

The cash flow statement: the company's bank passbook

Here is something that surprises many new investors: a company can show a healthy profit on its income statement and still have very little actual cash in the bank. How is that possible?

Think of a small shopkeeper in your neighborhood who sells rice and lentils. If a regular customer buys groceries on udhaaro (credit) and promises to pay at the end of the month, the shopkeeper's sales go up right away — but no cash has actually entered the till yet. The shopkeeper is richer on paper, poorer in pocket, until the customer actually pays.

The cash flow statement exists to answer exactly this question: forget what was promised — how much real cash actually moved? It is split into three parts:

  • Operating cash flow — cash from the company's main, everyday business. For a Nepali household, this is like the monthly remittance a family member sends home from working abroad: steady cash from ordinary, ongoing activity.
  • Investing cash flow — cash spent on, or earned from, buying long-term things like land, buildings, or machinery. This is like a family using savings to buy a small plot of land — cash goes out, but it buys something meant to last.
  • Financing cash flow — cash from borrowing or repaying loans, or from raising money by selling shares. This is like taking a loan from a cooperative or bank, or repaying one.
Why this matters A company can look profitable and still run out of cash to pay its own staff and suppliers, if too much of its "profit" is sitting as unpaid bills from customers rather than actual money in the bank. Investors who only read the income statement, and skip the cash flow statement, can be caught by surprise.

Reading all three together

A doctor doesn't diagnose a patient from a single test. A blood test, an X-ray, and the patient's own history are read together, because each one catches something the others might miss. Financial statements work the same way. The balance sheet shows the patient's current condition, the income statement shows how they've been doing this season, and the cash flow statement confirms whether the story actually holds up in real cash. Reading only one, and ignoring the others, is how investors get surprised.

Chapter recap

  • Balance sheet — a photograph of what a company owns (assets), owes (liabilities), and what's left for owners (equity), at one moment in time.
  • Income statement — the season's story of revenue earned, expenses paid, and profit (or loss) left over.
  • Cash flow statement — the real bank passbook, showing actual cash moved through operating, investing, and financing activities.
  • The core equation to remember: Assets = Liabilities + Equity.
  • Profit on paper is not the same as cash in hand — always check both.
