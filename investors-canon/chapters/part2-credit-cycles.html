The Rhythm of Borrowing and Lending

Every farmer in Nepal knows the rhythm of the monsoon. Rain arrives, rivers fill, canals overflow, and fields that were dry and cracked a few months ago turn green almost overnight. Then the rains stop, the water recedes, and the same canals that were overflowing now run thin. Nobody is surprised by this. It is simply the season turning.

Money moves through an economy in a very similar rhythm. Sometimes credit — which simply means borrowed money — flows freely, like the monsoon canal. Banks are eager to lend, loans are easy to get, and interest rates are low. At other times, credit dries up. Banks become cautious, loans are harder to get, and interest rates climb. This recurring rhythm is called a credit cycle, and understanding it is one of the most useful things an investor can learn, because it moves almost everything else — house prices, business profits, and yes, the stock market too.

The two seasons of a credit cycle

Just like the monsoon has a wet season and a dry season, a credit cycle has two main phases.

The expansion (the easy-money season)

During an expansion, banks have plenty of deposits sitting with them, and they compete with each other to lend that money out, because a bank only earns money by lending, not by letting cash sit idle in a vault. To attract borrowers, banks lower their interest rates and relax how carefully they check a borrower's ability to repay. Businesses take advantage of the cheap loans to expand — opening new shops, buying more land, building more houses. As more people borrow and spend, prices of things like land in the Kathmandu valley or shares on NEPSE tend to rise, because more money is chasing the same amount of land, houses, and shares.

The contraction (the tight-money season)

Eventually, the easy lending catches up with itself. Banks realize they have lent out more than is safe, some borrowers start struggling to repay, and banks become far more cautious. Interest rates rise, and new loans become harder to get approved. Businesses that depended on cheap, easy loans to keep growing suddenly find themselves squeezed. Spending slows, and prices of land, houses, and shares often fall back down, because there is now less borrowed money available to chase them.

A term, defined Liquidity is simply how much easily-lendable cash is sitting in the banking system at a given moment. When liquidity is high, loans are easy to get and cheap. When liquidity is low — sometimes called a "liquidity crunch" — loans are hard to get and expensive, even for creditworthy borrowers.

Nepal Rastra Bank: the dam operator

If credit flows like water through a canal, then Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB) — Nepal's central bank — is the operator standing at the dam, deciding how much water to let through. NRB's job is to try to smooth out the extremes of the credit cycle: to stop the "wet season" from flooding the economy with too much cheap credit, and to stop the "dry season" from starving it completely.

NRB has a few main tools to do this. Two of the most important are:

  • The policy rate (interest rate) — the rate at which NRB itself is willing to lend to commercial banks. When NRB raises this rate, it becomes more expensive for banks to borrow, so banks in turn raise the interest rates they charge ordinary borrowers, cooling down lending. When NRB lowers this rate, borrowing becomes cheaper throughout the economy, encouraging more lending.
  • The cash reserve ratio (CRR) — the fraction of every deposit a bank receives that it must set aside and is not allowed to lend out at all. Think of it like a farmer's storehouse rule: before selling or lending any of this year's harvest, the farmer must first set aside a fixed portion, untouched, as insurance against a bad season ahead. If NRB raises the CRR, banks must lock away more of each deposit, leaving less available to lend — tightening credit. If NRB lowers the CRR, more becomes available to lend — loosening credit.
Worked example Suppose a bank receives NPR 100 crore in new deposits, and NRB sets the cash reserve ratio at 4%. The bank must set aside NPR 4 crore, untouched, and may lend out up to NPR 96 crore. If NRB later raises the ratio to 6%, the bank must now set aside NPR 6 crore instead — leaving only NPR 94 crore available to lend. A small change in the ratio can meaningfully tighten or loosen how much money flows into the economy.

Nepal's own twist: remittances

Every economy's credit cycle is shaped by local conditions, and Nepal has one unusually powerful force of its own: remittances — the money Nepali workers abroad, especially in the Gulf countries, Malaysia, and India, send home to their families. Remittances make up a very large share of Nepal's entire economy, and a huge share of that money eventually lands as deposits inside Nepali banks.

This gives Nepal's credit cycle its own rhythm, somewhat separate from what NRB itself is doing. When remittance inflows are strong, banks suddenly find themselves holding far more deposits than usual, even if NRB has not changed any policy at all. Flush with this extra cash, banks compete harder to lend it out, interest rates tend to fall, and credit loosens — much like an unusually good monsoon filling every canal in the valley, whether or not the dam operator planned for it.

The reverse is also true. If remittance inflows slow down — say, because of a slowdown in the economies where Nepali workers are employed — banks can find deposits shrinking, liquidity tightening, and interest rates climbing, even without NRB doing anything differently. This is one reason Nepal has, at different times, swung between periods of very easy bank lending and sudden, sharp liquidity crunches: the credit cycle here depends not only on NRB's dam, but on how much water is arriving from outside the valley altogether.

Why this matters for an investor

Credit cycles do not stay confined to banks and loans — they reach into the stock market too. When credit is easy and interest rates are low, more money looks for a home, and some of it flows into buying shares, often pushing the NEPSE index higher. When credit tightens and interest rates rise, borrowing to buy shares (margin lending) becomes more expensive and more restricted, and some of that money flows back out of the share market, often pulling the index lower.

An investor who understands which season the credit cycle is currently in — expansion or contraction — has a real advantage: they can recognize whether a rising NEPSE index is being driven by genuinely improving businesses, or simply by a temporary flood of easy, borrowed money.

Chapter recap

  • A credit cycle is the recurring swing between easy-money expansion and tight-money contraction, much like a monsoon's wet and dry seasons.
  • Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB) tries to smooth this cycle using tools like the policy interest rate and the cash reserve ratio (CRR).
  • Remittances add a uniquely Nepali force to the cycle, filling — or draining — bank deposits somewhat independently of NRB's own policy.
  • Credit cycles influence NEPSE too: easy credit tends to push share prices up, tight credit tends to pull them down, regardless of individual company performance.
