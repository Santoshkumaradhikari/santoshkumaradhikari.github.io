WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Microfinance institution (MFI) — a Class D lender making small loans, mostly to rural women in groups. Group lending — borrowers in a group or centre guarantee each other's loans, so neighbours press each other to repay. Wholesale borrowing — an MFI borrowing in bulk from commercial banks, which then count it towards their deprived-sector lending quota. Base rate — a lender's published minimum cost of funds, on which loan rates are built. Portfolio at risk (PAR) — the share of loans with any payment overdue. Over-indebtedness — one family borrowing from several MFIs at once until it cannot repay any.

Microfinance is the part of NEPSE where the balance sheet can mislead you about the business. A commercial bank's numbers, for all their complexity, fairly reflect a spread-out, collateral-backed lending business. A Class D microfinance institution's numbers may not.

Behind a reported 20 percent return on equity can sit ten thousand overlapping loans, with no collateral, made to the same few hundred rural households — households that took a second loan to pay the first. Reading an MFI's accounts without understanding group lending, wholesale funding and Nepal's multiple-borrowing crisis is like reading a hospital's revenue without knowing which patients are contagious. This chapter builds that understanding from the rules up.

Lesson 31.1 — The "D" Class Tier and the Group-Lending Model

The bottom of the capital ladder, the top of the risk ladder

Under the Bank and Financial Institutions Act, Nepal Rastra Bank licenses four classes: A commercial banks, B development banks, C finance companies and D microfinance financial institutions — locally called laghubitta bittiya sanstha. Class D needs the least capital and carries the most risk. Its purpose is to give small, mostly collateral-free loans to low-income and "deprived sector" borrowers whom commercial banks will not or cannot lend to directly.

How group lending works

What defines an MFI is its lending method, not its size. Nepali MFIs mostly use the group-lending model that began with Bangladesh's Grameen Bank:

  • Borrowers, mostly women, form centres of about twenty to forty members, split into groups of five to eight.
  • They meet weekly or every two weeks with a field officer.
  • Each loan goes to an individual, but the group carries joint liability: if one member fails to pay, the group is expected to cover it, or all lose access to further credit.

This took the place of collateral. For years it produced repayment rates that looked better on paper than secured bank lending.

Why joint liability matters for the numbers

Joint liability changes what a bad loan means. At a commercial bank, one borrower's default is an isolated event. In a true group-lending MFI, one default is an early warning for four to seven more, because it shows that the social pressure enforcing repayment has stopped working.

So when an MFI's portfolio at risk (PAR) rises, do not treat it as random, independent defaults. Treat it as linked risk that can spread through a whole centre once trust breaks — like one leaking pipe in a shared water tank: once one joint fails, the pressure on the others rises. That is exactly how a manageable pocket of stress became a sector-wide problem after 2023 (Lesson 31.5).

KEY CONCEPT Group liability is a way to enforce repayment, not a way to spread credit risk. It concentrates behavioural risk inside each centre, even though thousands of small loans look diversified.

Deposit-taking or not

A few nationally licensed MFIs — Nirdhan Utthan Laghubitta and Chhimek Laghubitta among the best known — may take deposits from the public and work at a scale closer to a small bank. Most NEPSE-listed Class D companies, however, collect savings only from their own members and fund much of their lending with money borrowed from banks. That is the most important fact to grasp before you open an MFI's balance sheet.

Lesson 31.2 — Funding Structure: Wholesale Borrowing vs. Deposits

Where the money comes from

A commercial bank's funding rests on deposits from the public — generally the cheapest money in the system. Most MFIs lack that option. Their money comes mainly from wholesale borrowing: term loans from commercial and development banks, which are themselves meeting a regulatory target.

NRB requires commercial banks to direct set shares of their lending to priority areas:

  • about 10 percent to agriculture;
  • about 20 percent combined to tourism, small businesses, energy, IT and exporters using local raw materials;
  • at least 5 percent to the deprived sector — about 35 percent of the loan book in all.

Lending directly to deprived-sector households is costly and unfamiliar for a commercial bank's credit staff. So banks meet the quota by lending in bulk to MFIs, which re-lend in small, collateral-free amounts. The bank's loan to the MFI counts towards its target, though it never meets the final borrower. It is like a wholesaler supplying a village shop: the wholesaler never sees the households who buy.

REGULATORY DETAIL Commercial banks meet their deprived-sector quota mainly by lending in bulk to MFIs, not by lending directly. That gives commercial banks a real, if indirect, exposure to microfinance distress — which surfaced in 2025–2026 as MFI loan quality fell and banks' deprived-sector books fell with it.

Three consequences

  1. Funding cost follows the banks. A retail MFI's cost of funds depends on the rate banks charge it, not on deposit rates, so it moves with the same liquidity cycle as bank base rates, plus a margin for risk. When liquidity tightens, banks pass their higher cost straight on, squeezing MFI margins even before any bad loans appear.
  2. Little diversification. A retail MFI that gets 70 to 90 percent of its liabilities from a handful of bank credit lines has no lender of last resort and no depositor base to fall back on if one bank pulls its line in a crisis. That is exactly what happened across the sector as banks grew cautious about lending to MFIs through 2025.
  3. Wholesale and retail MFIs differ. Wholesale MFIs lend mainly to smaller MFIs and cooperatives, not to individuals — Sana Kisan Bikas Laghubitta is the best-known example. Being one step further from the final borrower, and lending to institutions with their own systems, they have had much better loan quality. NRB data showing wholesale-MFI bad loans of about 2.9 percent against more than 7 percent for retail MFIs is that difference made visible.
PRACTICAL TOOL When you open an MFI's notes, find the borrowings breakdown first. Ask: (1) What share of liabilities is wholesale borrowing versus deposits? (2) How concentrated is that borrowing among a few banks? (3) What is its average cost compared with the yield on loans? An MFI with 85 percent wholesale funding from three banks is a very different risk from one with a broad deposit base.

Lesson 31.3 — The Interest Rate Cap Regime and Its Business Model Consequences

Why NRB controls MFI lending rates

MFI borrowers usually have little information and little bargaining power. So NRB has regulated MFI lending rates directly and repeatedly — far more than it regulates bank pricing. Each change squeezes or widens MFI margins, so you need the sequence, not just today's rule.

The history in brief

  • 2016: NRB capped the spread — the gap between an MFI's cost of funds and its lending rate — at 7 percentage points. Over the following years, lending rates crept into the high teens as funding costs rose and MFIs used the full allowance.
  • Until Shrawan 2082 (mid-July 2025): a flat 15 percent lending-rate ceiling applied. In November 2023 an NRB study concluded that it was unworkable for smaller, higher-cost MFIs and recommended removing it in favour of a cost-based (spread-based) system.
  • From Shrawan 1, 2082 (17 July 2025), new loans: each MFI works out its own base rate from its cost of funds, running costs and loan-loss risk, and may add at most 3 percentage points. An MFI with a base rate of 11 percent can charge at most 14 percent.
  • NRB's July 2025 guideline adds a second ceiling: a new loan's rate may not exceed the latest monthly average base rate of commercial banks plus 9 points. Loans made before Shrawan 1, 2082 stay capped at 15 percent until they mature or are renewed.
  • December 2025: NRB's first-quarter review raised the limit for collateral-backed microfinance loans from Rs 7 lakh to Rs 15 lakh.
REGULATORY DETAIL Two pricing systems now sit side by side in a typical MFI's book: old loans made before Shrawan 1, 2082, capped at a flat 15 percent; and new loans, priced at base rate plus at most 3 points, recalculated quarterly. When an MFI reports its "average yield on loans", check how much of the book is still old loans. As those higher-priced loans mature, the new system will pull the yield down.

What this means for profitability

Under the new system, an MFI's profit depends directly on how efficiently it runs, because its ceiling is built from its own costs. An MFI with too many branches, too many field staff or high provisioning will have a higher allowed base rate — but that higher rate is evidence of a weaker cost structure, not pricing power. When comparing two MFIs, a higher yield is a red flag about costs and risk, not a green flag about margin.

CAUTION Do not read a rising loan yield as improving profitability under the current system. With base-rate-plus-spread pricing, a rising yield often signals rising costs or a rising built-in risk premium — both of which weaken, rather than strengthen, the economics you care about.

Lesson 31.4 — Portfolio at Risk, Write-Offs, and MFI-Specific Provisioning

PAR: the early warning

Banks track loan quality with the non-performing loan (NPL) ratio, counting loans overdue past a fixed point. Microfinance analysts also track portfolio at risk, usually PAR30: the share of loans with any payment overdue 30 days or more. In group lending, late payment spreads through the network faster than it becomes formal default. PAR30 is the early warning; the NPL ratio in NRB data confirms it later.

NRB's rules for MFIs

NRB's Unified Directives (the 2081 framework, with microfinance provisioning under Directive 1.081) set loan classes for Class D that broadly mirror the bank rules — pass, watchlist, substandard, doubtful and loss, by days overdue, with rising provisions and full or near-full provision for loss loans — but calibrated differently. MFIs must also keep minimum Tier 1 and total capital ratios. These are lower than banks' in absolute terms but set against a much less diversified, collateral-free book, which is why regulators watch MFI capital so closely in stress periods.

Write-offs: the number hiding in the footnotes

About 85 percent of outstanding microfinance loans carry no physical collateral at all (NRB data). When such a loan fails, there is no land to seize and nothing to auction. That makes an MFI's write-off policy — how quickly and fully it removes dead loans from its books — one of the most important and most easily managed disclosures.

An MFI that delays write-offs keeps a dead loan "on the books", provided for but not removed, understating a loss it has really already suffered. Provisions go through profit as an expense; a write-off is a balance-sheet step against provisions already made. So an MFI under pressure has a reason to under-provide — and the two levers together can flatter profit in a bad period.

WARNING Never take an MFI's NPL ratio at face value without checking its write-off policy. Two MFIs with the same loan quality can report quite different NPL ratios because one writes off dead loans quickly — which makes its NPL ratio look lower — while the other leaves them sitting, provided for but not written off.
PRACTICAL TOOL Work out a rough true loss rate by adding the period's write-offs back: (NPLs + write-offs in the period) ÷ (average loans + write-offs in the period). This "loss-adjusted NPL ratio" compares MFIs with different write-off habits far more fairly than the reported ratio.

How bad it has become

The direction through 2025 and into 2026 has been clear and severe:

  • Retail MFIs: NPL ratio up from 6.17 percent at Asar-end 2081 to 7.25 percent at Asar-end 2082 (about 17.5 percent higher). Total loan-loss provisions about Rs 26.24 arba, of which about Rs 15.71 arba was against non-performing loans — up nearly 18 percent in a year.
  • Wholesale MFIs: a much lower but rising NPL ratio of about 2.93 percent (up 13 percent), with provisions of about Rs 2.19 arba.
  • By mid-April 2026: the sector-wide NPL ratio reached 11.32 percent, up from about 7 percent nine months earlier. Bad loans totalled about Rs 54.08 arba — 74 percent more than a year before — on about Rs 500 arba of loans outstanding.

CASE IN POINT A jump from about 7 percent to 11.32 percent in roughly nine months is not a slow credit-cycle drift. It is the signature of a joint-liability system coming apart. Once enough borrowers in enough centres stopped paying, the social pressure that had kept repayment high for years lost its force across the network, and late payment contained within single groups became a portfolio-wide event.

Lesson 31.5 — The Over-Indebtedness Crisis: Multiple Borrowing and Systemic Risk

One household, many lenders

The biggest story in Nepali microfinance in recent years is not one company's mismanagement. It is sector-wide over-indebtedness driven by multiple borrowing: the same low-income household borrowing from several MFIs at once, often to pay what it owes the others.

How it happened is, in hindsight, predictable:

  • The number of MFIs multiplied in the 2010s, and competition for borrowers grew.
  • Field officers, pushed by boards and management to grow loans and hit profit targets, lent to people who were already clients of other MFIs, without properly checking their total debts.
  • One MFI chief executive has openly admitted the incentive problem in the Nepali press: boards push for higher profits, and that pressure turns into looser lending at the point of disbursement.

Reporting documented one borrower whose debts to five MFIs — including Forward Community Microfinance, Mero Microfinance, Mithila Laghubitta, Deprosc Laghubitta and Sana Kisan Bikas Laghubitta — grew from a manageable amount to about Rs 45 lakh in four years, through compounding interest and new loans taken to keep each lender satisfied. Estimates put about 100,000 borrowers in acute repayment distress, out of about 33.1 lakh microfinance clients. That is a minority by headcount, but concentrated and connected enough through shared centres and informal credit to move the whole sector's NPL ratio.

A business-model failure, not just a borrower failure

It is tempting to blame borrowers who took on more debt than they could manage. For an investor that view is incomplete and misleading. Multiple borrowing was made possible — even invited — by two things during the sector's fastest growth:

  • no effective shared credit-bureau discipline across MFIs;
  • incentives — growth targets, disbursement-linked staff bonuses, board pressure for profit — that rewarded loan growth over careful lending.

Every MFI that lent to an already-indebted borrower without checking was making an underwriting decision, and each one shows up later in the PAR and NPL data. Read the crisis as a governance and lending-discipline failure across a whole sector at once — which is why it moved so fast from "company problem" to "regulator's top concern".

WARNING When several MFIs you follow show the same pattern of worsening PAR and NPLs at the same time, do not treat it as company-specific risk that a basket of MFI shares can diversify away. Nepal's over-indebtedness crisis is shared and sector-wide, rooted in the same borrower networks and the same industry incentives. A basket of MFI shares does not spread this risk; it concentrates your exposure to the same borrowers through different company names.

The crossholding directive

NRB also worried about concentrated ownership, as commercial and development banks bought stakes in MFIs — partly to meet their deprived-sector quotas more easily (Lesson 31.2). In January 2022 it ordered consolidation wherever a bank or financial institution held more than a 10 percent minority stake, or 51 percent or more control, in a microfinance company, with an initial deadline of Asar-end 2079 (mid-2022) to submit merger plans.

The directive has since been eased at the edges — longer timelines, and permission for wholesale MFIs formed by merger to keep lending for up to five years afterwards — but the direction is firm: NRB wants fewer, better-capitalised, better-governed MFIs, not many thinly capitalised ones chasing the same borrowers. The Class D universe today is smaller and more consolidated than five years ago, and further mergers, sometimes forced, remain possible for individual holdings.

CASE IN POINT The 2022 crossholding directive explains why some MFI mergers on NEPSE were not strategic choices at all but compliance exercises. When an MFI with a large commercial-bank promoter stake announces a merger, check whether it is fixing a crossholding breach before believing a story about synergy.

Lesson 31.6 — Reading MFI Financial Statements: Red Flags and Valuation Adjustments

Here is the checklist to run before valuing any NEPSE microfinance company.

  1. Classify the funding. Read the borrowings and deposits notes separately. Find the share of wholesale borrowing, how concentrated it is by lender, and its average cost. Reliance on three or four bank lines carries a risk of credit lines being pulled that a broad deposit base does not. Check whether the company is one of the few nationally licensed deposit-taking MFIs, which have a very different funding risk.
  2. Find the true loan quality. Work out the loss-adjusted NPL ratio from Lesson 31.4. If an MFI reports much better quality than the sector's 11.32 percent, check its write-off timing before accepting that as better lending.
  3. Test the yield against the base-rate rules. For loans made after Shrawan 2082, the yield should be roughly the MFI's own base rate plus at most 3 points. A yield well above that on new loans is either a disclosure or compliance problem, or a sign that much of the book is still old 15 percent loans — either way, it changes your forecast margin.
  4. Check merger and crossholding exposure. Look for bank promoter stakes near or above the 10 percent and 51 percent thresholds. A forced merger, dilution or restructuring is a real possibility that a simple earnings multiple will miss.
  5. Stress-test for linked risk. Do not model losses as independent across thousands of loans. Model them as linked within centres and across your whole basket of MFI holdings. Once over-indebtedness breaks repayment in one part of the network, the group mechanism that once enforced discipline can spread the stress just as fast.
PRACTICAL TOOL A five-line table for every MFI you follow: (1) wholesale funding share and lender concentration; (2) loss-adjusted NPL ratio against the sector; (3) base rate and spread against the 3-point ceiling; (4) bank promoter holdings against the 10 percent and 51 percent thresholds; (5) centre and group repayment trend, if disclosed. An MFI that passes all five is a very different investment from one that passes none, even if their ROE and EPS look alike.
CAUTION Microfinance profit is unusually easy to overstate compared with banking, because the two levers that would expose weakness — how much to provide and when to write off — are both management judgements, in a business where recovery is genuinely hard on the 85 percent of loans with no collateral. Treat an MFI's stated book value and profit as the start of your research, not the end.

Chapter recap

  • Class D MFIs lend small, mostly collateral-free amounts through group lending. Joint liability enforces repayment but concentrates risk: one default warns of others in the same centre.
  • Most listed MFIs are funded by wholesale loans from banks, which use them to meet deprived-sector quotas. Funding costs follow the banking cycle, funding is concentrated, and wholesale MFIs have much better loan quality than retail MFIs.
  • Rate rules have moved from a 7-point spread cap (2016) to a flat 15% ceiling, then, for new loans from Shrawan 1, 2082 (July 2025), to the MFI's own base rate plus at most 3 points, also capped at commercial banks' average base rate plus 9 points; old loans stay at 15%. In December 2025 the collateral-backed loan limit rose from Rs 7 lakh to Rs 15 lakh. A higher yield now signals higher costs, not strength.
  • Track PAR30 as the early warning and check write-off policy; use a loss-adjusted NPL ratio. About 85% of MFI loans have no collateral.
  • The sector NPL ratio jumped from about 7% to 11.32% by mid-April 2026, with bad loans of about Rs 54.08 arba on about Rs 500 arba of loans.
  • Multiple borrowing is a sector-wide governance and lending-discipline failure; a basket of MFI shares does not diversify it. NRB's 2022 crossholding directive forces mergers where bank stakes cross 10% or 51%.
  • Use the five-step checklist before valuing any MFI, and treat reported profit as a starting point.
