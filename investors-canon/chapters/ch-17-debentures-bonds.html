Shares get the headlines. IPO listings, bonus announcements and tea-shop tips about hydropower companies are what most Nepali investors talk about. Debentures, bonds and treasury bills rarely excite anyone. Yet they matter for two reasons. First, they are investments in their own right — in 2025/26 (2082/83) debenture trading on NEPSE grew about tenfold as bank deposit rates fell to around 3%. Second, government interest rates are the starting point for judging what any other investment should earn, including shares.

This chapter starts with what a debenture is and who gets paid first when a company fails. It then looks at the debentures actually listed on NEPSE, at how the government borrows through Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB) auctions, at the arithmetic linking price and yield, at what the record shows about defaults, and finally at how government yields feed into share valuations. Throughout, it separates three things: what the law says, what the data show, and what is only a useful rule of thumb.

Lesson 17.1 — What Is a Debenture: Debt Instrument Basics

The Fundamental Nature of Debt

A company that needs money has two choices. It can sell part-ownership (shares), or it can borrow. A debenture is a written promise to repay a loan. Think of lending Rs 1,00,000 to a relative's business with a signed paper: "I will pay you Rs 10,000 every year and return your Rs 1,00,000 after ten years." A debenture is that paper, made standard, sold to many lenders at once and, if listed, tradable on NEPSE.

Under Nepal's Companies Act, 2063, a public company may raise loans by issuing debentures, with or without security, and a public issue of debentures needs a debenture trustee — an institution that acts for all the holders together. A secured debenture is backed by a charge over particular assets, such as a mortgage on a factory or a hydropower plant's equipment. An unsecured debenture has no such backing; its holder is an ordinary creditor.

Anatomy of a Debenture

Face value (par value): The amount repaid at maturity. Nepali corporate debentures are normally Rs 1,000 a unit.

Coupon rate: The yearly interest rate on the face value. A 10% coupon on Rs 1,000 pays Rs 100 a year.

Coupon frequency: How often interest is paid — yearly, every six months, or every quarter. Practice varies between issues, so the prospectus and trust deed decide it, not a market habit. Government development bonds announced in 2023 paid interest every six months.

Maturity: When the face value is repaid. Listed bank debentures run five to ten years, most of them ten. Government development bonds have recently been issued for three to fifteen years.

Call and put features: A call lets the issuer repay early, usually when rates have fallen. A put lets the holder demand early repayment. Few Nepali debentures have them, but always check.

Seniority: Where the debenture stands in the queue of creditors. This matters more than almost anything else, and it is explained below.

Credit rating: A public issue of debentures needs a rating from a SEBON-licensed agency, such as ICRA Nepal or CARE Ratings Nepal. A rating is the agency's opinion of how likely the borrower is to pay on time. It is not a guarantee. Note also the difference between an issuer rating (about the company) and an issue rating (about one particular debenture): Kumari Bank's 10% debenture of 2090, for example, was sold in 2023 with ICRA Nepal's issuer rating of BBB+ cited in the offer news. An issuer rating says nothing direct about how much a particular debenture would recover in a failure.

Debenture or bond? Some textbooks say a bond is secured and a debenture is not. There is no such universal rule, and Nepali law does not use the words that way. In Nepal, listed corporate debt is usually called a debenture, and government debt a bond or bill. What matters is the actual terms of each instrument.

Seniority: Not All Debentures Stand in the Same Place in the Queue

Think of the queue at a bank counter after a cooperative collapses. Some people hold a token that sends them straight to a particular cash drawer; others wait in the general line; some agreed in writing to stand at the back. Debentures come in the same three types:

TypeWhat it meansNepali example
Senior securedBacked by a charge over named assets; paid from those assets firstProject debentures with a charge on plant and equipment; the August 2026 SEBON draft would require non-financial companies to issue only secured debentures
Senior unsecuredNo charge; ranks with other ordinary creditorsPlain corporate debt without security
SubordinatedAgreed in its terms to rank behind other creditors — for a bank, behind depositorsMost listed bank debentures, which are issued to count as regulatory capital

This is why the phrase "debenture holders stand near the front of the queue" is too simple. A subordinated bank debenture stands behind every depositor. It is ahead of shareholders, but in a bank failure that may not be worth much.

What Happens If a Company Fails

Two separate things happen, and they should not be mixed into one "waterfall".

1. Secured creditors deal with their security. A secured creditor's claim is tied to its collateral. Under the Insolvency Act, 2063, a secured creditor can claim against the company only for the shortfall — the amount owed minus the value of the security (Section 54) — and the court can decide disputes over that value. During insolvency proceedings, enforcing security can be paused by court order, and a court-approved restructuring plan can bind secured creditors only in limited cases (Sections 19 and 29). How much a secured holder actually recovers depends on whether the charge is valid and registered, whether another lender has an earlier charge on the same asset, what the asset sells for in a forced sale, and how long and costly enforcement is.

2. The liquidator pays everyone else in a fixed order. Section 57 of the Insolvency Act sets the order for the company's remaining money:

  • first, the costs and fees of the officials who run the insolvency process (the interim administrator, inquiry officer, restructuring manager and liquidator), and loans taken during the investigation and restructuring periods;
  • then workers' unpaid wages, followed by their leave, gratuity and provident fund dues (directors excluded);
  • then all other debt claims the liquidator accepts — which is where unsecured debenture holders stand;
  • and only if anything is left, interest on accepted debts, then preference shareholders, then ordinary shareholders.

Claims at the same level share proportionately if there is not enough to pay them in full (Section 57(2)). Tax dues do not appear as a separate level in Section 57. Note too that banks and financial institutions are generally resolved under the NRB Act and the Bank and Financial Institutions Act, which give NRB special powers, so a bank failure may not follow the Insolvency Act at all. A subordinated bank debenture would rank behind depositors in any case.

A debenture holder usually sleeps better than a shareholder — not because the company cannot fail, but because the law puts lenders ahead of owners. How far ahead depends on the exact terms of the debenture.

Why the jump? When bank fixed deposits pay under 4% — NRB put the average commercial-bank deposit rate at 3.11% in mid-September 2026, with the 91-day treasury bill at 1.35% — an old debenture paying 10% or 11% looks very attractive. Buyers bid prices up. In November 2026 every one of the 23 debentures in the Canon's dataset last traded above its Rs 1,000 face value, between about Rs 1,040 and Rs 1,460 (median about Rs 1,136). This is the price–yield rule of Lesson 17.4 happening in real life, and it is one more face of the liquidity cycle described in Part 0: a banking system flooded with cash — NRB was soaking up more than Rs 1.3 trillion (Rs 13 kharba) of excess liquidity in September 2026 — pushes money into anything that still pays a decent return.

Who Issues, and on What Terms

Almost all listed debentures come from banks and financial institutions: commercial banks, plus some development banks and finance companies. According to ShareSansar, most are subordinated debt issued to meet regulatory capital requirements. Coupons have ranged from about 7% to 12%; issues sold in 2025/26 (2082/83), when rates were low, carried about 6%–8%. A typical bank issue sells about 40% to the public and 60% by private placement, with 5% of the public part reserved for mutual funds (as in Nabil's and Kumari Bank's 2023 issues).

Do not assume all listed debentures are alike. For each one, check: secured or unsecured; senior or subordinated; what collateral, if any; who the trustee is; coupon and payment frequency; maturity and any call option; the rating; and how much is outstanding.

Liquidity: Better Than Before, Still Thin

Even after the 2025/26 (2082/83) boom, a typical debenture in the Canon's dataset traded on only about 81 of the roughly 240 trading days, and many trades were tiny. Kumari Bank's 10% debenture of 2090 shows what that means. It traded at Rs 1,500 in February 2026, at Rs 1,216 in May on a trade of three units, and at Rs 1,460 in late September in two block trades of 20,000 units. Prices that jump this much on a handful of trades are not reliable estimates of fair value. Expect a wide gap between buying and selling prices, and demand extra yield for the difficulty of selling (Chapter 11).

Rules: What Is in Force and What Is Proposed

A public issue of debentures today needs SEBON approval of a prospectus, a credit rating and a debenture trustee, under the Securities Registration and Issuance Regulations, 2073 and their amendments (the eighth amendment came in 2083). In August 2026, SEBON published for comment a separate draft Debenture Registration and Issuance Regulations, 2083. As reported, it would let non-financial companies with at least Rs 1 billion (Rs 1 arba) paid-up capital, a credit rating and a clean repayment record issue debentures, with debt kept within 70:30 of debt to equity, a mandatory trustee, and only secured debentures for such companies. It is a draft, not law; check SEBON's site for the final version.

What to Check Before Subscribing

Read a debenture prospectus mainly for what happens if things go wrong:

  • Ability to pay: debt-service coverage (cash from operations ÷ interest and principal due), leverage, and three to five years of profit history. For a bank, capital adequacy and bad loans (Chapter 14).
  • Seniority and security: where this debenture stands in the queue, and what the collateral would really fetch in a forced sale.
  • The rating report: the reasons, any outlook or watch, and whether it is an issuer or issue rating.
  • The trustee: who it is, whether it is independent of the issuer, and what powers the trust deed gives it.
  • Covenants: limits on borrowing, minimum coverage ratios, a negative pledge (no new lender may be given a better claim on the same assets), and a cross-default clause (a default elsewhere also counts as a default here).

Hydropower Debentures

A hydropower plant that is already running can have steadier cash flows than one still being built, because construction risks — cost overruns, geology, delays — are behind it. But "steadier" is not "safe". Check: river flows and dry-season output; the power purchase agreement's tariff, penalties and take-or-pay terms; how promptly Nepal Electricity Authority pays; transmission bottlenecks and spilled power; plant maintenance; and whether the company must refinance large loans before the debenture matures.

Lesson 17.3 — Government Bonds and Treasury Bills: NRB Auction Process

Who Does What

The Government of Nepal borrows at home to cover part of its budget deficit. Several bodies share the work:

BodyRole
Ministry of FinanceDecides how much to borrow, within the budget and the Public Debt Management Act, 2079
Public Debt Management Office (PDMO), set up in December 2018 under the MinistryPlans borrowing and keeps debt records and payments (the "middle" and "back" office)
Nepal Rastra BankRuns the auctions and sales as the government's agent (the "front office")
InvestorsBanks hold most of it; insurers, funds, other institutions and individuals hold the rest

The handover of all debt functions to the PDMO is not complete, so NRB remains the place where auctions are announced and results published.

The Instruments

Treasury bills mature in 28, 91, 182 or 364 days. They pay no coupon. You buy below face value and get face value at maturity; the difference is your return.

Development bonds are longer-term — recent issues run from three to fifteen years — and pay a fixed coupon, every six months in the 2023 issues.

Citizens Savings Bonds are sold to the public at fixed rates set by the government — for example, the 9% Citizens Saving Bond 2083 sold in mid-2021. Foreign Employment Savings Bonds are offered to Nepalis working abroad. They matter for the millions of workers in the Gulf, whose remittances are central to Nepal's economy (Part 0).

How the Auctions Work

An auction is like a village land sale where buyers write their offers on paper. For treasury bills, NRB uses a multiple-price auction: each successful bidder pays the price it bid. For development bonds, NRB's 2023 notices describe a single-rate auction: bids are ranked, a cut-off rate is set where the full amount is sold, and every successful bidder gets that same rate. The format matters: in a single-rate auction, bidders gain little by shading their bids; in a multiple-price auction, they must guess what others will bid.

Not all of each issue goes to competitive bidders. In the 2023 development-bond notices, 85% was for competitive bids and 15% for non-competitive bids, which accept the rate the competitive bidding sets. Banks, financial institutions, insurers, other organisations and Nepali citizens could bid, with a minimum of Rs 50,000. Check each notice for current terms.

Selling Before Maturity

What the Government Pays Now

In mid-September 2026, NRB reported the 91-day treasury bill at 1.35%, down from 2.13% a year earlier, and the interbank rate at 2.75%. A July 2026 press report put the 364-day bill at about 2.72%. Development-bond auction results are published on NRB's site but were not available to this book in a consistent series. So this book cannot yet draw a full Nepali yield curve — the line joining the yields of short and long government debt. When you can, plot the 28-, 91-, 182- and 364-day bill yields and recent development-bond cut-off rates by maturity. A rising line (long rates above short rates) is normal. A flat or falling line can mean that markets expect rates to fall, that banks are short of cash, or that the government is borrowing heavily at one maturity.

Tax

Under Section 88(3) of the Income Tax Act, 2058, banks, financial institutions, debenture issuers and listed companies withhold 6% tax when they pay interest to an individual, provided the interest has a Nepal source and is not part of the individual's business. Section 92 treats some such payments as final withholding — meaning no further tax is due on them — but the conditions differ by case. Institutions, and individuals receiving interest through a business, face different rules. Savings-bond terms have varied by issue. Check the current Finance Act and the issue notice rather than assuming any instrument is tax-free.

Lesson 17.4 — Yield, Coupon, and Price Relationship: The Inverse Rule

The Most Important Relationship in Fixed Income

When interest rates rise, bond prices fall. When rates fall, prices rise. This is arithmetic, not opinion. Think of a shop lease that pays you a fixed Rs 8,000 a month. If new leases in the same bazaar start paying Rs 10,000, nobody will pay full price for yours. If new leases pay only Rs 6,000, yours becomes worth more.

Take a bond with face value Rs 1,000, an 8% coupon paid once a year, and five years to maturity. It pays Rs 80 a year and Rs 1,000 at the end. Its fair price is the present value of those payments at the market yield:

Price = 80/(1.10)^1 + 80/(1.10)^2 + 80/(1.10)^3 + 80/(1.10)^4 + (80+1000)/(1.10)^5

Price = 72.73 + 66.12 + 60.11 + 54.64 + 670.60 = Rs 924.18

Market yieldPrice (annual coupon)Compared with par
6%Rs 1,084.25Premium
8%Rs 1,000.00Par
10%Rs 924.18Discount

If the same bond paid Rs 40 every six months, you would discount ten half-yearly payments at 5% each, and the price at a 10% yearly yield would be Rs 922.78 — slightly different. Always match the calculation to the actual payment schedule.

A Real Nepali Example

Kumari Bank's 10% debenture of 2090 was sold in October 2023 and matures about ten years later. Assuming yearly coupons and ignoring accrued interest, the Canon's rough calculation of its yield to maturity at different traded prices is:

DatePriceYears left (approx.)Yield to maturity (approx.)
August 2024Rs 1,04099.3%
May 2026Rs 1,21676.1%

At Rs 1,460, a buyer locks in a yield of under 3% a year if held to maturity — below the bank's own deposit rates — while taking the risk of a subordinated BBB+-rated bank debenture. The 10% coupon is not the return you earn; the yield at the price you pay is.

Accrued Interest: Clean and Dirty Prices

If you buy a bond between coupon dates, the seller has earned part of the next coupon. Suppose a 10% yearly coupon was last paid four months ago. The seller has earned Rs 100 × 4/12 = Rs 33.33. The "clean" price leaves this out; the "dirty" price — what you actually pay — includes it. Ask your broker how NEPSE debenture trades handle accrued interest before comparing yields.

Duration and Convexity: Measuring Price Sensitivity

Macaulay duration is the average time until you receive the bond's cash, weighted by each payment's present value. For our 8% five-year bond at a 10% yield, it is 4.28 years — less than five because coupons arrive early. Modified duration is Macaulay duration ÷ (1 + yield) = 4.28 ÷ 1.10 = 3.89. It means: if yields rise by one percentage point, the price falls by about 3.89%.

Duration is a straight-line estimate of a curved relationship. Convexity corrects for the curve. For this bond (convexity about 20.1):

Yield riseDuration estimateDuration + convexityExact change
+1 point (10% → 11%)−3.89%−3.79%−3.79%
+3 points (10% → 13%)−11.68%−10.77%−10.83%

Zero-coupon bonds have the highest duration (equal to their maturity); high-coupon bonds have lower duration. Long-dated, low-coupon bonds move most when rates change.

Duration is the clock inside a bond — it measures not how long you wait for your money back, but how much your price moves when the world changes its mind about interest rates.

Which Yield?

  • Current yield = coupon ÷ price. For the bond at Rs 924.18: 80 ÷ 924.18 = 8.66%. It ignores the gain of Rs 75.82 at maturity.
  • Yield to maturity (YTM) = the single rate that makes all future payments worth today's price: 10% here. It assumes no default and that coupons are reinvested at the same rate.
  • Yield to call uses the call date and call price instead of maturity. Yield to worst is the lowest of the yields to each possible call date and to maturity — the safe figure to use for callable bonds.
  • After-tax yield: for an individual taxed at 6% on interest, a 10% coupon is worth about 9.4%.
  • Holding-period return: what you actually earn if you sell before maturity — coupons received plus or minus the price change.

Building a Required Yield

To judge whether a debenture's price is fair, build the yield you should demand:

Required yield = government yield for the same maturity + credit spread + liquidity premium + extra for special features (such as a call option or subordination).

Then compare it with the YTM at the market price. Nepal publishes no regular series of corporate credit spreads, so the spread and liquidity premium must be estimated and stated openly. For example (illustration only): a seven-year government yield of 4%, plus 1.5 points for a BBB+ bank's credit risk, plus 1 point for subordination and thin trading, gives a required yield of about 6.5%. On that basis, Kumari Bank's debenture at Rs 1,460 (yield about 2.7%) would look expensive.

Lesson 17.5 — Default Risk on Nepali Corporate Debentures: What the Record Shows

What a Default Is

A default is a failure to pay interest or principal on time, as promised. Rating agencies treat even a short delay in a scheduled debenture payment as a default. Restructuring the terms — paying later or less — also counts.

What the Record Shows — and What It Does Not

This book has not found a publicly documented case in which a NEPSE-listed debenture missed a scheduled payment. That is not proof that none happened; it means a reader should not rely on stories of past debenture losses unless names, dates and amounts are given. There are reasons the record looks clean so far. Almost all listed issuers are banks and financial institutions supervised by NRB, and in the 2010s weak institutions were usually merged or taken over under NRB direction rather than left to default. In those episodes it was mainly depositors and shareholders who were exposed.

A clean record is not a guarantee. Most listed bank debentures are subordinated and rank behind depositors. Coupons look generous only because deposit rates are low. And the first debentures from non-financial companies may come under SEBON's new rules. A documented Nepali default database does not yet exist. If one is built, each case should record:

FieldEvidence needed

Estimating Expected Credit Loss

Expected credit loss = probability of default × loss given default × exposure at default.

  • Probability of default (PD): the chance the issuer defaults over a stated period, such as one year.
  • Loss given default (LGD): the share you lose if it does, after recoveries. Loss = 1 − recovery rate.
  • Exposure at default (EAD): how much you have at stake when it happens.

Example (illustration only): you hold Rs 1,00,000 of a debenture. You judge a 2% chance of default each year and a 40% recovery, so a 60% loss. Expected loss = 0.02 × 0.60 × 1,00,000 = Rs 1,200 a year, or 1.2 points of yield. If the debenture yields 6.5% and a government bond of the same maturity yields 4%, the extra 2.5 points must cover that 1.2-point expected loss and pay you for illiquidity, tax and the chance that losses are far worse than expected. Expected loss is an average; the loss in a bad case — the "unexpected loss" — is what can really hurt, which is why no single debenture should be a large share of your savings.

A higher coupon is not compensation — it is an invitation to quantify. The question is whether the extra yield, net of expected credit loss, is worth the illiquidity and the sleepless nights.

Watching a Debenture After You Buy It

  • Ratings: follow ICRA Nepal and CARE Ratings Nepal reports for any downgrade, negative outlook or watch.
  • The issuer: each quarter, check capital adequacy, bad loans and profits (for banks), or cash-flow coverage (for others).
  • Prices: a debenture trading well below the price justified by government yields can be a warning. But in a thin market, one small trade can move the price a long way (Lesson 17.2), so look at several trades before drawing conclusions.
  • The trustee: before you buy, read whether the trust deed lets the trustee act on covenant breaches, not only on missed payments, and whether the trustee is independent of the issuer.

Lesson 17.6 — How Bond Yields Affect Equity Valuations: The Risk-Free Rate Anchor

The Discount Rate

Every way of valuing a share — discounted cash flow, dividend discount, residual income — needs a discount rate: the return an investor requires for waiting and for risk. It is usually built as a "risk-free" rate plus a premium for the extra risk of shares. In practice, Nepal's government securities are used as a risk-free proxy. They carry very little risk of the government failing to pay rupees, but they still carry inflation risk, liquidity risk and the risk that coupons must be reinvested at lower rates.

Which Government Rate?

Match the rate to what you are valuing. A share's cash flows stretch over many years, so a long-term government bond yield — for example, a recent ten-year development-bond cut-off rate — is usually a better anchor than a 364-day treasury bill. The bill rate is useful for short-term comparisons, but it swings with banking-system liquidity: the 91-day bill fell from 2.13% to 1.35% in a single year to September 2026. Also keep the rate consistent: use a rupee rate for rupee cash flows, and do not mix an inflation-adjusted rate with forecasts that include inflation.

The Equity Risk Premium

The equity risk premium (ERP) is the extra return investors demand for holding shares instead of government bonds. In mature markets, estimates are usually around 4%–6%. For Nepal, Professor Aswath Damodaran's January 2026 country-risk table — a widely used, freely available source — gives:

Component (January 2026)Nepal
Moody's sovereign ratingBa3
Default spread for that rating3.06%
Country risk premium (spread × about 1.52, the ratio of share-price to bond volatility in emerging markets)4.66%
Mature-market ERP used4.23%
Total ERP for Nepal8.89%

This is one method on one date, not "the" answer. It is built from the sovereign rating, not from NEPSE's own history, and it is designed mainly for valuations in US dollars; adding it to a rupee bond yield is a common shortcut, not a precise method. An updated 2026 edition was published in July 2026, so check the latest figures. An earlier version of this chapter said Nepal's ERP was "often 10% to 15%"; this book cannot trace that range to a dated, reproducible source, so it has been removed.

Building a Cost of Equity

The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) says: cost of equity = risk-free rate + beta × ERP, where beta measures how strongly a share moves with the market. Example (illustration only): with a long-term government yield of 5%, a beta of 1.0 and Damodaran's 8.89% ERP, the cost of equity is about 13.9%. See how much the answer depends on the government rate:

Government yieldCost of equity (beta 1.0, ERP 8.89%)Fair P/E for a no-growth company paying out all profits (1 ÷ cost of equity)
3%11.9%8.4

A rise of four points in the government yield cuts the fair price of the same profits by about a quarter. That is the bond–equity link in one table.

Be careful with beta on NEPSE. Many shares trade irregularly, which makes measured betas too low; betas change from year to year; the history is short; and the index has been reshaped by waves of new listings. A beta from a spreadsheet regression is a starting point, not a fact. Sensible checks include comparing with peers in the same sector, using weekly or monthly rather than daily returns, and testing a range of betas.

How Interest Rates Reach NEPSE

The chain runs roughly like this:

  1. NRB policy and banking-system liquidity (driven heavily by remittances, Part 0)
  2. → bank deposit rates and credit demand
  3. → treasury-bill and development-bond yields
  4. → debenture yields and credit spreads
  5. → the discount rate investors apply to shares
  6. → where savers put their money, and NEPSE prices

This is a map, not a machine. Government borrowing, bank balance sheets, company earnings and plain sentiment can change the timing or direction of any link. Keep five effects apart: the mechanical change in discount rates; changes in the returns investors demand; changes in company profits (banks' margins, for instance, react directly to rates); money moving between deposits, debentures and shares; and mood.

Nepal's Rate Cycles in Practice

The pattern has repeated. In 2021–22, as NRB tightened and inflation and the trade deficit rose, some banks offered fixed deposits of 10%–12%, treasury-bill yields climbed, and NEPSE fell from its 2021 peak. In the earlier 2019–20 easing, deposit rates fell and liquidity surged, feeding the 2020–21 bull market. And in 2025–26, with deposit rates near 3%, money poured into debentures, pushing their turnover up tenfold and their prices well above face value. These episodes fit the chain above, but each also had other causes, so treat them as illustrations, not proof.

The Central Lesson

Government yields are a key reference for every other return in the market. They help set what debentures must pay, they anchor the discount rate for shares, and they set the hurdle any investment must clear. An investor who watches where NRB auctions clear, how wide debenture spreads are, and whether NEPSE's earnings yield (earnings ÷ price) offers enough over government yields is looking at the market as a whole, not as a collection of separate tips.

The bond market is the brain; the equity market is the mood. The brain sets the framework within which moods swing. Ignore the brain, and your mood will eventually surprise you.

Chapter recap

  • A debenture is a loan with a paper. Its value depends on its exact terms — especially seniority. Most listed bank debentures are subordinated and rank behind depositors.
  • Insolvency has two steps. Secured creditors deal with their collateral and claim only the shortfall; the liquidator then pays others in the order set by Section 57, with ordinary creditors after officials' costs and workers' dues, and shareholders last.
  • Nepal's debenture market woke up in 2025/26 (2082/83). Turnover rose about tenfold as deposit rates fell near 3%, and listed debentures now trade above face value — but trading is still thin and prices can jump on tiny trades.
  • Government borrowing: treasury bills by multiple-price auction, development bonds by single-rate auction, savings bonds at set terms. How easily you can sell early depends on the instrument.
  • Price and yield move in opposite directions. Use yield to maturity (or yield to worst) at the price you pay, after tax; use duration and convexity to measure rate risk.
  • No documented default is not the same as no risk. Quantify expected loss, watch ratings and issuers, and do not concentrate.
  • Government yields anchor share valuations. Use a long-term government yield, a dated and sourced ERP, and a range of betas — and remember the chain from rates to NEPSE is a map, not a machine.
