Chapters 0.1 and 0.2 described the credit cycle and the channels through which NRB policy reaches the market. In Nepal, these forces do not drift smoothly. They tend to settle into distinct phases, called liquidity regimes. In each regime, one main market pattern is easy to recognise, and it usually lasts for months or even years.

This chapter turns those regimes into a four-phase framework that you can use as a map for positioning. The aim is not to predict the future with certainty. It is to know which regime you are in now, what the main risks are in that regime, and how your portfolio should change as a result.

Framework Philosophy Regime-based investing is not market timing. It is simply accepting that different conditions reward different behaviour. Farmers do not plant the same crop in every season; they read the season first. An investor who behaves the same way in every regime will be doing the wrong thing for much of the time.

0.3.1 — Defining Regime: Why Discrete Phases Outperform Continuous Indicators for Investment Positioning

A regime is a stable set of market conditions — how easy it is to borrow, how much spare cash there is, how much risk investors will take, and which way NRB policy is pointing. A regime lasts long enough to produce recognisable patterns in share prices. Thinking in regimes is more practical than staring at indicators that change a little every month, for four reasons:

1. It makes you think in thresholds. Instead of asking whether credit growth is slightly better or worse this month, you ask whether the system has crossed from one state into another. A crossing is something you can act on. A small wobble is not.

2. It anchors your strategy. Within a regime, the main risks and opportunities are known. You do not need to rethink your whole portfolio every month. You follow the strategy that suits the regime until the regime changes.

3. It stops overtrading. Reacting to every small change leads to constant small trades, which add costs and weaken your conviction. Regime investing means a few large, well-considered changes.

4. It handles uncertainty honestly. You will not always know the regime. There are transition periods of two to four months when the signals are mixed. The framework has its own rules for those periods.

Historical Context: Two Cycles, Now Three

Nepal has lived through about two and a half cycles of this framework since 2010. The 2014–16 bull market and the correction after it was a short, squeezed cycle. The 2019–2022 boom and the 2022–2023 correction was a more extreme version. NEPSE ran to its August 2021 peak near 3,200, then collapsed to about 1,815 by late September 2022. That fall is still the clearest example of what the compression phase can do to a portfolio with no borrowed money — let alone one built on margin loans.

The cycle now under way is the freshest case study this framework has ever had. From the 2022 lows, NEPSE mostly stayed around 2,000 through 2023, then closed just above 3,000 on 15 August 2024 (31 Shrawan 2081; 3,000.81) and again on 29 July 2025 (13 Shrawan 2082; 3,002.07). It then slid to about 2,490 in October 2025, rebounded to about 2,960 in late March 2026, ended FY 2082/83 (2025/26) at 2,597.80 in mid-July 2026, and stood near 2,650 in mid-November 2026. The phases have broadly followed the order described here. But not every big fall fits the framework — Section 0.3.7 explains one important fall it missed.

What Is Happening in the Economy

Remittances are strong. Deposits are growing and feeding new loans. Banks compete hard for borrowers. Interest rates on both loans and fixed deposits are fairly low, so borrowing is affordable for businesses. Hydropower projects are drawing construction loans. Microfinance institutions are adding borrowers. Retail investors, seeing share prices rise, borrow against their shares to buy more.

Company profits across the market are growing. P/E and P/B multiples — share price compared with profit per share and with book value per share — are rising, because investors expect profits to keep growing. Many companies hold IPOs, and new shares jump sharply in their first days of trading.

The Late-Phase Warning Signs

Every expansion carries the seeds of its own end. An investor who misses the late-phase warning signs will give back much of the gains:

• Credit growth stays above 20 percent for two or more quarters in a row. Banks are lowering their lending standards.

• The CD ratio nears the 90 percent ceiling. The limit that has ended past Nepali expansions is coming back into play.

• Margin loans grow faster than deposits for a year. The pile of borrowed money under the market is getting taller and shakier.

• The interbank rate stays above the middle of the corridor. The spare cash is draining away.

• New investors open demat accounts at a record pace while new listings more than double in price. The latest buyers have never seen a falling market.

Where the Framework Meets Late 2026

Here the framework shows its honesty. Several Phase 1 signs are present right now. Deposits grew 13.9 percent in FY 2082/83 (2025/26) (13.7 percent year on year by mid-October 2026). The CD ratio is near 73 percent. The interbank rate sits on the corridor's 2.75 percent floor (mid-October 2026). Remittances are at a record, and NRB is soaking up spare cash. But the defining Phase 1 sign — credit growth above 15 percent — is missing (6.5 percent in FY 2082/83 or 2025/26, and 8.3 percent year on year by mid-October 2026). And the NPL ratio, 5.66 percent in mid-July 2026 against about 4.6 percent a year earlier, was still rising, not resolved. When the boxes you tick are spread across two phases, you are in a transition. The transition rules in Section 0.3.7 exist for exactly this situation.

0.3.3 — Phase 2 — Compression Regime: CD Ratio Binding, Remittance Slowdown, NRB Tightening Active

Defining Characteristics

The compression regime begins when the banking system hits its limits. The CD ratio reaches its ceiling, remittance growth slows, and NRB — worried about inflation or falling reserves — switches from easing to active tightening. Deposits become scarce and expensive. Interbank rates jump towards or above the corridor ceiling. Banks compete for deposits by offering ever higher fixed-deposit rates, and margin loans are the first thing they cut when money runs short.

The Defining Nepali Case: 2021–22

The compression after the 2021 mania is the framework's starkest lesson. Through late 2021, the CD ratio pressed against its ceiling, interbank rates ran hot, and margin loans sat at record levels against share prices that had never been higher. When the turn came, it followed a chain. Margin calls forced selling. Selling broke prices. Broken prices triggered more margin calls. Banks, short of funds, cut share-backed lending hardest. NEPSE fell from nearly 3,200 to about 1,815 by late September 2022 — a fall of about 43 percent. The damage to the real economy came later, through 2022–23, as the credit squeeze cut into company profits.

Notice what this compression did not need: no war, no pandemic, no fraud. A market full of borrowed money hitting a funding limit was enough. That is why regime investors hold more cash late in an expansion. It is not timidity. It is preparing for the one phase in which cash is king.

Positioning in Phase 2

Reduce your share holdings step by step — not in one dramatic sale, but by following clear exit rules (Chapter 58's circuit-trap modelling is built for this phase). Move the money into fixed deposits, which in a compression finally pay well: the 2022/23 (2079/80) fixed-deposit rates above 10 percent were the reward for that phase's risk. Take on no new margin loans at all. The compression phase punishes every habit that worked in Phase 1.

0.3.4 — Phase 3 — Balance Sheet Repair Phase: NPL Rising, Provisioning Heavy, Earnings Depressed

Defining Characteristics

After the fall comes the longest and least exciting phase: balance sheet repair. Credit growth is weak, often 5–10 percent. The NPL ratio climbs with a delay, usually peaking 12–24 months or more after the fall itself, as restructured loans fail and the renewal habit can no longer hide the stress. Banks set aside heavy provisions (money kept aside to cover bad loans), so their profits hit bottom. Price-to-book multiples sit at multi-year lows even as deposits recover. NEPSE moves sideways in a wide range. Prices change least in this phase, but the underlying businesses change most.

The Nepali Case: 2022–24

The repair phase after the 2022 compression ran on schedule. System NPLs climbed from about 1.3 percent (mid-July 2022) to 3.9 percent (mid-July 2024), and kept rising to about 5.7 percent by mid-July 2026. Provisions for bad loans ate bank profits for two years. Microfinance suffered most, because its borrowers had the thinnest income cushions. NEPSE spent much of 2023 and early 2024 in a range near the low 2,000s while its core of banks rebuilt from the inside. All the while, the external sector was quietly healing. Remittances kept growing, and foreign exchange reserves rebuilt from about seven months of import cover toward what would become nearly twenty months. That laid the monetary groundwork for the next phase — like a field left fallow for a season, quietly regaining strength before the next planting.

Reading the Exit From Phase 3

The repair phase does not end when NPLs return to their old lows — that takes years. It ends when three signals line up. First, the NPL ratio peaks: it stops rising, even if it stays high. Second, policy language shifts from discipline to revival. The FY 2083/84 (2026/27) monetary policy's promises on managing bad loans in struggling industries and reviving loans are exactly this kind of language. Third, funding costs drop, as old high-rate fixed deposits renew at lower rates. If you wait for NPLs to look clean, you will miss the phase. Almost all of Phase 4's return comes in the window when the repair is clearly ending but not yet reported as finished.

In the current cycle, the second and third signals arrived well before the first. Funding costs fell from 2024, and revival language came in July 2026. But the NPL ratio was still rising in mid-July 2026. That is why the market recovered while banks' reported bad loans still looked worse every year.

0.3.5 — Phase 4 — Recovery Regime: NRB Easing, Credit Unlocking, Equity Re-Rating

Defining Characteristics

The recovery regime begins when NRB, satisfied with inflation and the external sector, moves from tightening to active easing. It offers the best balance of risk and reward in the whole cycle. Prices still reflect the gloom of the repair phase, while cash is already flowing back in.

Nepal's latest recovery ran from 2024 into 2026. It is worth telling precisely, because almost every step was visible in policy documents before it showed up in prices:

• Policy rates were cut step by step. The policy rate fell from 5.5 percent to 5.0 percent (July 2024), 4.5 percent (July 2025) and 4.25 percent (December 2025). The bank rate fell to 5.75 percent and the corridor floor to 2.75 percent.

• The margin channel was reopened. NRB removed the Rs 20 crore cap on share-backed loans to institutions in July 2024 and the Rs 25 crore cap for individuals in October 2025. Broker margin lending expanded, and from FY 2083/84 (2026/27) limits are set by each institution's strength.

• Remittances surged to a record Rs 2,363 billion (Rs 23.63 kharba) in FY 2082/83 (2025/26). Deposits grew 13.9 percent, and the room to lend below the CD ceiling widened to about sixteen points.

• Bank profits turned up. Cheaper funding, as the high-rate fixed deposits of 2022/23 (2079/80) renewed at much lower rates, protected margins, but the bigger boost came from recovering bad loans and needing fewer provisions. Meanwhile the NPL ratio was still rising, reaching about 5.7 percent in mid-2026.

• NEPSE re-rated from the low 2,000s to just above 3,000 — closing at 3,000.81 on 15 August 2024 (31 Shrawan 2081) and 3,002.07 on 29 July 2025 (13 Shrawan 2082). This rise ran on spare cash that arrived before credit growth (only 6.5 percent in FY 2082/83 or 2025/26), just as the channel ordering in Chapter 0.2 predicts.

The False Recovery Signal — and the 2025–26 Test

Phase 4's biggest risk is believing the recovery is already Phase 1 before credit growth confirms it. A re-rating carried only by deposit cash and falling rates — without the broad rise in profits that credit growth brings — is a recovery standing on one leg. The falls since the July 2025 high are the framework's live lesson: to about 2,490 in October 2025, and from about 2,960 in late March 2026 to the mid-2,500s by mid-2026. Through both, liquidity signals stayed loose. There was no compression signature: interbank rates stayed below the floor, the CD ratio stayed far from its ceiling, and NRB was soaking up cash rather than draining it. That is what separates a one-legged recovery digesting its gains from the start of a new compression. One number separates a resumed Phase 4 run from a true Phase 1 boom: credit growth climbing past 8–10 percent. Until that number appears, position for recovery. Do not yet position for a boom.

0.3.6 — Government Capex Cycle Overlay: How Budget Spending Interacts With NRB Liquidity

NRB's liquidity regimes do not work alone. Laid over all four phases is the government's own cycle of capital spending — capex, meaning money spent on roads, bridges, power lines and other long-lasting assets. It can strengthen or weaken whatever regime is in force.

The Mechanics

Government capital spending adds cash directly to the economy. Payments to contractors, suppliers and workers become bank deposits, joining the pool filled by remittances. In expansion and recovery phases, strong capital spending adds fuel to deposit growth that is already strong — the multiplier from Chapter 0.1 running with an extra engine. In compression phases, capital spending usually falls short, because projects stall when money is tight, and that deepens the slowdown. There is also an odd yearly pattern: much capital spending is crammed into the last months of the fiscal year, especially Asar. That creates a burst of cash around June that seasonal analysis must allow for.

The Current Overlay

FY 2025/26 (2082/83) ended with an unusually strong external position. Foreign exchange reserves covered about 19.6 months of imports of goods and services by mid-July 2026, and remittances were at record levels. Average inflation was about 3 percent, though year-on-year inflation was rising toward 5 percent by year-end. Together, these gave the government real room to spend without worrying about a shortage of dollars. The FY 2083/84 (2026/27) budget targets — 7 percent growth and inflation around 5.5 percent — assume exactly this room. For the regime analyst, this is a confidence factor. It makes it more likely that the recovery's next leg comes through demand for credit — government-backed infrastructure and contracts pulling private lending along behind them — rather than the slower route of businesses borrowing on their own. Nepal's governments have often announced large capital budgets and then spent much less, so watch the monthly budget execution numbers: actual capital spending, not announcements.

The Decision Rule: What to Do When the Six Points Disagree

Six signals will often point in different directions, as they do now. Two readers with the same NRB report should still reach the same phase. So the phase is read by a fixed rule, checked in this order — the first line that fits decides:

1. Compression (Phase 2) if the interbank rate is above the middle of NRB's corridor.
2. Expansion (Phase 1) if private-sector credit growth is 15 percent a year or more.
3. Repair (Phase 3) if the system NPL ratio at the latest mid-July is at least 0.2 points higher than a year before.
4. Recovery (Phase 4) in every other case.

Think of a traffic policeman at a crossing: whichever lane he signals first moves, whatever the other lanes are doing. Three more rules complete it. Use levels, not trends, exactly as written above. Change the phase only when two quarterly readings in a row agree (Lesson 59.7). And if a reading is missing or late, keep the phase you have until it arrives. The other three data points — deposit growth, the CD ratio and the NEPSE trend — are context: they can make you more careful inside a phase, but they never change the phase. This is the version tested in Chapter 80, Lesson 80.8; the full checklist was also tested as a vote — five of the six points, since the CD ratio has no consistent history — and it did worse: it missed the 2021–22 crash and finished level with staying fully invested. Use the other points as context, not as votes.

Reading the Verdict — Including When It Is Ambiguous

Three of the six data points (deposits, CD ratio, interbank rate) show the loose cash of a recovery or expansion. Credit growth sits on the border between repair and recovery. Bad loans say the repair is not finished. And the market has already run ahead and is digesting its gains. The rule's verdict: Phase 3 (Repair), because the interbank rate is low (not Compression), credit growth is below 15 percent (not Expansion), and bad loans rose by about a point in the year to mid-July 2026. Lesson 59.7's settings follow this verdict. The author's judgement: late Phase 4 — a recovery still waiting for credit growth to confirm it — in a transition zone, not a clean regime. The two differ because the rule has no way out of Repair while bad loans keep rising (Lesson 80.8). Write down both; act on the rule unless you record a reasoned exception in your journal. One extra warning sits outside the six points. Inflation climbed to 6.37 percent by mid-October 2026, from 1.47 percent a year earlier, much of it from fuel prices after the 2026 war involving Iran (Chapter 6). If it keeps rising, NRB may stop easing, and the interbank rate — which Chapter 0.1's data suggest gave the earliest warning of past falls (a lead to watch, not a proven signal) — would be the first place to see it. The transition rules then apply:

• Move in half-steps within the rule's setting. Neither commit fully to recovery nor retreat into defence. Size your positions so that you can follow either outcome without fear.

• Watch two numbers, and know their jobs. Credit growth has two lines. Above 8–10 percent for two quarters in a row is the early warning that the recovery is broadening — a watch number, not part of the tested rule. At 15 percent the rule itself moves to Phase 1. (In mid-October 2026 credit growth was 8.3 percent: one reading inside the watch zone.) If deposit growth falls below about 10 percent while credit is still weak, treat it as a warning to be more careful within your phase.

• Re-check every quarter, when NRB's reports come out — not every day. Regime analysis deliberately runs on a slower clock than the market's daily noise.

WHAT THIS CHECKLIST CANNOT SEE The decision rule above — three of the six points — was run quarter by quarter over NRB data from 2015 to 2026 (Chapter 80, Lesson 80.8). That is a look back at history by someone who already knew it, not a test on unseen data. It caught the 2021–22 squeeze in time to halve the active part's fall for anyone following Lesson 59.7's settings. But the worst fall over the whole period was the same 37 percent with or without the settings. But it completely missed the 2016–18 fall, when NEPSE dropped from about 1,800 to about 1,140 on the quarter-end readings the test used, while credit kept growing faster than 15 percent a year. That fall did not begin with money getting tight — at the time, banks were issuing large numbers of new shares to meet NRB's higher capital requirement — so liquidity data had nothing to show. Think of the checklist as a rain gauge: it measures one kind of storm very well, and is blind to an earthquake. It also gives no clear signal for leaving Phase 3 while bad loans keep rising, which is why this chapter reports the author's judgement next to the rule's verdict, rather than instead of it.

The Sector Notes, Sharpened for Now

• Banks: the engine of the recovery, through cheaper funding and lower provisions. Much of both was already delivered in FY 2025/26 (2082/83), when net interest income barely grew but provisions fell. The next round of bank gains depends on the credit channel — loan growth. That is why the 8–10 percent credit threshold is also the banking sector's earnings signal.

• Hydropower: the main winner from the project finance channel. Today's plentiful, cheap construction loans become tomorrow's COD schedule (commercial operation date, when a plant starts selling power). The sector's profits turn up years after its funding does, which patient investors can use.

• Microfinance: hit hardest in the repair phase, and so the most sensitive to the recovery. The FY 2083/84 (2026/27) revival measures for bad loans in struggling sectors matter most here. Highest risk, and the biggest swings when the regime turns.

• Insurance: steadier than most. Insurers earn investment income on their float — the premiums they hold before paying claims — so falling interest rates reduce that income. But insurers also hold shares, which gain in a recovery. The two effects partly cancel out, so insurance shares tend to swing less with the regime than banks or microfinance.

The Discipline That Makes It Work

The four-phase framework fails in only one way: when you use it as a prediction instead of a posture. It does not tell you the index will be 3,000 or 2,300 next Asar. It tells you which behaviours the current conditions reward, which risks they hide, and which single data point would change your mind. In late 2026 that number is credit growth, with the interbank rate as the early-warning light. Check it every quarter, respect the thresholds, and let the regime — not your emotions, and not the market's mood — set the terms.

Carry This Forward Every tool in the next eighteen Parts — every ratio, model and checklist — assumes a regime in the background. A bank share at 0.9 times book value can be a bargain in Phase 4 and a trap in Phase 2. The regime is not one input among many. It is the setting in which every other input is read.

Chapter recap

Conditions in Nepal's market cluster into four regimes — expansion, compression, balance sheet repair and recovery. Each has its typical NRB behaviour, market pattern and portfolio needs. The framework's power is in identification: six free data points, checked every quarter, are enough. As of November 2026 they describe a late recovery. Spare cash is plentiful and deposits are strong. Credit growth has not yet confirmed the recovery. Bad loans have not yet clearly peaked, and rising inflation is a new risk. And a re-rating that twice took NEPSE just above 3,000 is now digesting its gains. Transition rules apply, with one confirmation number to watch. Position for the regime you are in, watch the number that would end it, and let thresholds rather than emotions move your money.

Chapter recap

  • The market moves through four seasons, or regimes: expansion, compression (squeeze), balance-sheet repair and recovery. A farmer reads the season before planting; an investor should read the regime before buying.
  • Six free data points identify the regime: lending growth, deposit growth, the CD ratio against its 90 percent ceiling, the interbank rate against the corridor, the bad-loan ratio and its direction, and NEPSE's trend and breadth.
  • November 2026: deposits, the CD ratio and the interbank rate show plenty of spare cash, but bad loans have not clearly peaked — recovery with repair scars, and a price correction within that recovery.
  • Each regime calls for a different portfolio: full weight in expansion; cutting step by step and no margin in compression; slow buying of quality in repair; rebuilding in half-steps in recovery (Chapter 59 turns this into settings).
  • Government spending can add to or drain spare cash, so read the budget alongside NRB's numbers.
  • The checklist has limits: tested on 2015–2026 data, it caught the 2021–22 squeeze but missed the 2016–18 fall. Treat it as a guide, not a guarantee.
