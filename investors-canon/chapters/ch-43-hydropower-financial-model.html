Lesson 43.1 — The Anatomy of a Hydropower Financial Model

One question, one long spreadsheet

A hydropower financial model is a very long spreadsheet that tries to answer one question. Will the water in one Himalayan river earn enough cash, over enough years, to repay the people who paid for the dam, turbine and powerhouse — and still leave something for the owners?

Everything else in this chapter is detail added on top of that question. But hydropower modelling in Nepal has its own words, quite different from modelling a factory, a hotel or a toll road. So we name the building blocks first.

Two lives: building, then running

A hydropower project lives two lives, and the model treats them completely differently.

  • The construction period. Usually three to six years for a run-of-river project in Nepal, longer for a storage scheme. The company spends money on civil works, tunnels, penstocks (the big pipes that carry water down to the turbines), turbines and transmission lines. It earns nothing.
  • The operating period. This starts on the Commercial Operation Date (COD) — the day the plant legally and physically starts selling electricity under its Power Purchase Agreement. It usually runs 25 to 30 years, matching the length of the PPA and often the rest of the generation licence.

Where construction spending goes: CWIP

During construction, every rupee spent piles up on the balance sheet as Capital Work in Progress (CWIP). CWIP is an accounting bucket for an asset that is not finished yet. Because it is unfinished, it earns no revenue and is not yet depreciated (written down year by year).

Think of CWIP as a construction diary. Cement, steel, turbine payments, contractor bills, consultant fees — and, importantly, the interest on loans used to pay for all of it — sit in this bucket until the plant is commissioned. At COD, CWIP is "capitalised": moved into fixed assets. Depreciation then begins.

Interest during construction (IDC)

That interest behaves unusually, so it has its own name: Interest During Construction (IDC). In a normal working company, interest is an expense in the profit and loss statement. During construction, it is not. It is added to the cost of the asset instead.

Think of a farmer in Ilam who borrows to plant a tea garden. For the first years there are no leaves to sell, so there is no income to pay interest from. The interest becomes part of the cost of setting up the garden, and is recovered later once the bushes yield. Hydropower IDC works the same way. It raises total CAPEX (capital spending) and only reaches the income statement, as depreciation and interest, once the plant is running and earning.

KEY CONCEPT Capital Work in Progress (CWIP) is the running total of everything spent on a not-yet-operating asset — materials, labour, equipment and capitalised interest. It moves from the CWIP line to "Property, Plant and Equipment" the moment the plant reaches Commercial Operation Date, and depreciation begins then.

The four moving parts after COD

Once the plant runs, the model repeats four moving parts every year for the life of the PPA:

  1. Revenue — how much water flows, and what the Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA) pays for the electricity.
  2. Operating costs — small and fairly predictable for a run-of-river plant, which burns no fuel.
  3. The fiscal regime — royalty to the Government of Nepal and income tax to the Inland Revenue Department. Both change shape at fixed points in the project's life.
  4. Debt service — principal and interest paid to the lenders who financed construction.

The output lenders, owners and regulators all watch most closely is the Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR). It is one number, worked out every year, that says how many times the year's cash could cover that year's loan payment.

A household budget over thirty years

Picture a household budget stretched across three decades. In the early years, the family (the project company) is paying off a big home loan (construction debt) while its income (PPA revenue) is still building. Government dues (royalty and, later, tax) start small and grow as temporary exemptions (the tax holiday) end.

What a bank really checks is whether the family can make its loan payment every year, even a bad year. Not the average year — the worst likely year. "Plan for the dry year, not the average year" is the idea behind almost every rule in this chapter: how tariffs are set, how river flow is forecast, and how DSCR limits are written.

Lesson 43.2 — The PPA Tariff Structure: Dry Season, Wet Season, and Escalation

Rivers follow the monsoon

Nepal's rivers follow the monsoon. From about mid-June to mid-November, glacier melt and rain swell river flows several times over. From December to May, flows fall to a fraction of their monsoon level.

Most Nepali hydropower so far is run-of-river (RoR). Such a plant has little or no reservoir. It generates more or less in step with whatever water is flowing past its intake at that moment; it cannot store water to use later. So its output is not steady. It may run near full capacity for six months and at a fraction of capacity for the other six.

Two prices for the same electricity

Today, the NEA is the only legal buyer of wholesale power from private developers. It buys under a Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) — a long-term contract fixing the price and terms on which the developer sells all its output to the NEA. To deal with the seasonal swing, the NEA pays two different prices for the same electricity, depending on when it is delivered. This is the single most important feature of Nepali hydropower revenue.

Think of a tomato farmer near Kathmandu. In the monsoon, everyone's tomatoes ripen at once. The market floods and prices fall. In the dry winter, few farmers can grow tomatoes, so those who can — with irrigation or a plastic tunnel — sell into a scarce market at a much higher price for the same tomato.

Electricity is the same:

  • Dry season (Poush to Chaitra, roughly mid-December to mid-April): most run-of-river plants produce well below capacity. Power is scarce and valuable, so the NEA pays a markedly higher dry-season tariff per unit.
  • Wet season (Baisakh to Mangsir, roughly mid-April to mid-December): every run-of-river plant runs near full at once. Power is plentiful, so the NEA pays a lower wet-season tariff.

As an illustration, a typical recent PPA for a small-to-medium run-of-river project sets the dry-season tariff at about NPR 8.40 per kWh and the wet-season tariff at about NPR 4.80 per kWh. The dry rate is close to 75% higher for physically identical electricity. This is deliberate. It nudges developers towards storage and peaking projects, which can shift monsoon water into dry-season generation. And it pays run-of-river developers fairly for the fact that their most valuable season is also their leanest.

KEY CONCEPT A Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) with two seasonal tariffs pays a higher rate per kWh for electricity delivered in the dry season (when supply is scarce) and a lower rate in the wet season (when supply is plentiful) — the same way a market pays more for off-season vegetables than for a monsoon glut of the same crop.

Escalation: a raise that stops

Most current PPAs add an escalation clause: a pre-agreed yearly increase in the tariff, for a limited number of years from COD. After that, the tariff freezes, in nominal rupees, for the rest of the PPA.

A common structure in recent NEA PPAs raises both the dry and wet tariffs by about 3% a year for the first eight years of operation. Then the tariff stays at whatever level it reached for the remaining 22 or so years. It is like a salary that rises each year for your first eight years in a job and then never rises again.

The escalation partly makes up for inflation in the early years, and is partly just a negotiated part of the standard PPA. It is not linked to actual inflation, and it does not go on forever. Every model must get this exactly right. Forgetting to stop the escalation after year eight can overstate 20-plus years of revenue by a very large margin.

WARNING Escalation is front-loaded and finite. A common modelling error is raising the tariff for the whole 25–30-year PPA instead of only the first several years the PPA actually allows. This can inflate total project revenue by a large margin and make an unbankable project look investable on paper.

The PPA is also the lender's safety net

The tariffs are fixed in the PPA, and the NEA is government-owned and government-backed. So the PPA is also the project's main credit support. Lenders are relying on the NEA's promise to pay as much as on the river's flow.

That is why two PPA clauses matter so much to whether a project can borrow:

  • Required COD — a contract deadline for starting operation. Missing it can bring liquidated damages (pre-agreed penalties) or, in newer PPA templates, fewer escalation years.
  • Deemed COD — a clause that lets the developer start earning on schedule even if the delay was caused by the NEA's own grid connection not being ready.

Sponsors should read these clauses as carefully as the tariff numbers. An attractive tariff can be worth very little if a delay outside the developer's control both costs escalation years and leaves months with almost no revenue.

CASE IN POINT The 456 MW Upper Tamakoshi project — a peaking run-of-river scheme built by an NEA subsidiary — ran years late, delayed mainly by the 2015 earthquake and later penstock problems, before it began generating in July 2021. Then, in mid-2024, floods and landslides in the Tamakoshi valley shut the operating plant for months. It resumed partial generation in December 2024 and full generation only in mid-2025. It is widely cited as a reminder that river and geological risk do not disappear once construction ends, and that flood insurance and force majeure (events outside anyone's control) clauses matter as much in a model as the tariff.

Lesson 43.3 — Hydrology Risk: P50, P90, and the Honest Revenue Forecast

Nature decides the output

Every hydropower revenue forecast rests on one uncertain input: how much water will flow down this river, at this spot, each future year.

A toll road's traffic is uncertain, but once it opens you can estimate it within a narrow band. A factory's output is a management choice. A run-of-river plant's output is set by nature, and nature does not send the same flow every year. Some years bring a heavy monsoon and good dry-season flow. Others bring drought.

A range, not a single number

Hydrologists (river-flow scientists) study decades of flow records — gauge readings where they exist, sometimes filled in with rainfall-runoff models where gauge history is thin. They produce not one "expected" energy number but a range of possible yearly outputs, each with a probability.

From that range, engineers pick reference points by their exceedance probability: the chance that actual yearly generation will equal or beat a given figure. The common ones are P50, P90 and P99:

  • P50 — the output with a 50% chance of being equalled or beaten in any year. In plain terms, the middle year: beaten half the time, missed half the time. It is the best single guess of the long-run average, and is usually used for the owners' base-case return.
  • P90 — the output with a 90% chance of being equalled or beaten. It is deliberately cautious, missed in only about one year in ten. Lenders strongly prefer P90 (sometimes the even more cautious P99) to size debt and test DSCR. A bank cares far more about surviving a bad year than about sharing in a good one.

Back to the household from Lesson 43.1. A family taking a home loan should size the monthly payment against income in a lean month, not a Dashain-bonus month, because the loan must be paid every month. A bank does the same with river flow. It wants to know the project can pay even in a year drier than nine years out of ten. That is exactly what P90 means.

CAUTION P50 and P90 energy estimates can differ by 10–20% or more for one river, depending on how variable its flow is and how well it has been measured. Sizing debt on P50 instead of P90 overstates how much the project can borrow and understates the chance of breaking the DSCR limit in a truly dry year. It is one of the most important single choices in the whole model.

Two cases, side by side

So a proper Nepali hydropower model carries at least two energy cases:

  • a P50 case, for the owners' expected return — their IRR (yearly return), payback period and dividend capacity;
  • a P90 (or more cautious) case, to size the loan and test whether DSCR stays above the lender's minimum in a dry year.

A model with one optimistic energy figure — often quietly near P50 or better — used for both the owners' case and the lender's test is a red flag. Either the hydrology work was not done properly, or the picture is meant to flatter. "Annual generation of X GWh" means nothing until you know which probability stands behind it.

PRACTICAL TOOL Before looking at a single tariff or DSCR number in any hydropower model or information memorandum, ask three questions. (1) What exceedance probability (P50, P90, P99) is behind the stated yearly energy figure? (2) How many years of river records — measured or modelled — support it? (3) Is the debt sized on the same energy case used to sell the shares to investors, or on a more cautious one? A gap between the selling case and the lender's case is the fastest way to spot an aggressively presented deal.

Storage and peaking plants are different

Reservoir and peaking projects can hold water back and release it when needed, rather than passing through whatever arrives. For them, the Plant Load Factor (PLF) is partly a design choice.

PLF (also called capacity factor) is the energy actually generated in a period as a percentage of the most the plant could make running at full capacity every hour. A peaking plant is built to run at a lower average PLF (often 30–45%) while packing its output into the most valuable hours of the day and season. A pure run-of-river plant's PLF (often 45–60%, depending on the river) is simply whatever the river delivers, hour by hour.

Both numbers matter, but they answer different questions. PLF tells you how hard the installed machines are worked. P50 and P90 tell you how much total energy that gives in a year, and how confidently.

Lesson 43.4 — Construction-Period Mechanics: CWIP, IDC, and the Drawdown Schedule

Back to the construction phase from Lesson 43.1. It is worth seeing exactly how CWIP and IDC move through a model, because getting them right changes the final project cost — and so the tariff and owners' return needed to make the project work.

How the money comes in

A typical Nepali run-of-river project borrows in a debt-to-equity ratio of around 70:30 or 80:20 during construction. Nepali commercial banks have been the main lenders to domestic hydropower, often in consortiums because one project is too big for one bank (Chapter 42). For larger schemes, international development lenders and export credit agencies increasingly join.

Owners' money (equity) is usually spent first, or alongside debt in proportion. Debt is then drawn down — released bit by bit — against certified construction progress, milestone by milestone.

Interest that piles up

Every rupee borrowed during construction starts earning interest at once, but there is no revenue to pay it. Two things can happen:

  • the developer pays it in cash from a separate reserve (uncommon, since there is no income yet); or
  • — the standard treatment — the interest is capitalised: added to the loan balance (and to CWIP) instead of being paid. It compounds, and is repaid later from operating cash, along with the principal.

A worked example

Take a simplified 25 MW run-of-river project. Its base construction cost — civil works, electro-mechanical equipment, transmission line, land and development costs — is NPR 5.4 billion (Rs 5.4 arba). It is financed 70:30, so the debt is NPR 3.78 billion, drawn step by step over four years.

Suppose the loan costs about 10.5% a year — fairly typical for a Nepali hydropower term loan in rupees — and most of it is drawn in the later years, after the equity is spent. The capitalised interest over those four years might add roughly NPR 500–650 million to the final cost. Total CAPEX rises from the base NPR 5.4 billion to about NPR 6.0 billion once IDC is included.

That NPR 600 million is not paper money. It becomes real loan principal, repaid from operating revenue over the next 15 years. That is why disciplined construction scheduling — avoiding delays that stretch the interest period — is one of the most valuable levers a developer has.

WARNING Every month of construction delay does double damage. It adds another month of capitalised interest to CWIP (more debt to repay). At the same time, it pushes back the COD that starts the revenue clock and, under many PPA templates, cuts the number of escalation years the developer gets. A one-year delay can cost far more than a simple "12 months of extra interest" estimate suggests.

After COD: two kinds of depreciation

At COD, CWIP — including all the capitalised interest — moves to Property, Plant and Equipment, and depreciation starts. Here a model must keep two sets of books apart:

  • Tax depreciation. Nepali tax law generally lets hydropower assets be depreciated on a written-down value (declining balance) method, using a depreciation pool and rate set by the Income Tax Act. This sets taxable income, and so the cash tax actually paid.
  • Book depreciation. In its own accounts, the company may use straight-line depreciation (an equal amount each year) over the asset's useful life — often 30–35 years or the PPA term — to match revenue for investors. This sets reported profit.

The two differ. A careful model tracks both. It is the tax schedule, together with the tax holiday in Lesson 43.6, that sets actual cash tax — and cash is what matters for DSCR.

KEY CONCEPT Interest During Construction (IDC) is capitalised interest: interest on construction-period debt that is added to the project's asset cost (through CWIP) instead of being expensed straight away. It raises total project cost and total loan principal, and only flows through the income statement — as ordinary interest and depreciation — after Commercial Operation Date.

Lesson 43.5 — The Debt Service Coverage Ratio: The Lender's Lens

The formula

If a Nepali hydropower lender watches one number above all, it is the DSCR. In its simplest form:

DSCR = Cash Flow Available for Debt Service (CFADS) ÷ Debt Service (principal + interest due that period)

  • CFADS is roughly revenue minus operating costs minus royalty minus cash tax paid. It is the actual cash the project makes in a period before anything is set aside for lenders.
  • Debt service is the principal repayment plus interest due to lenders in that same period.

A DSCR of 1.30x means the project made Rs 1.30 of cash for every Rs 1 it owed lenders that period — a comfortable cushion. A DSCR of 1.00x means it made exactly enough, with nothing left over. That would alarm any lender: no room for a bad month, a surprise repair or a slightly drier season.

The covenant and what happens if it breaks

Nepali bank hydropower loans usually include a minimum DSCR covenant — a promise in the loan contract, tested at each repayment date (often every six months) on the P90 energy case, that DSCR will not fall below a floor. The floor is commonly 1.20x to 1.30x. Some loans also set a slightly higher "average" DSCR across the whole loan.

Breaking the minimum does not automatically mean default. It usually triggers a cash sweep or dividend lock-up: the company may not pay dividends until DSCR recovers. Repeated or severe breaches can become an event of default, letting lenders demand early repayment or take other action.

PRACTICAL TOOL A simple, useful formula for CFADS in a Nepali hydropower model: CFADS = (energy sold to the NEA at the seasonal tariffs) − (O&M, operation and maintenance, costs) − (capacity royalty + energy royalty) − (cash income tax paid, after any tax holiday). Debt service = scheduled principal + interest for the period. Run this for every year of the loan on the P90 energy case. Flag any year where DSCR dips below the loan's minimum — that is where refinancing risk, a dividend lock-up or covenant renegotiation will actually bite.

Shaping repayments to the cash

Lenders often go further and require DSCR-sculpted repayment. The repayment schedule is shaped on purpose — not equal instalments — so payments are lower in years when cash is naturally tighter and higher when cash is stronger. Tight years include the early ones, before escalation has worked through, and the years when the tax holiday ends and cash tax first bites.

This is why the seasonal tariffs, escalation, and royalty and tax steps in this chapter matter so much. A repayment schedule that ignores when cash shifts can be unbankable even if the project's average economics look healthy.

Lesson 43.6 — Royalties, Taxes, Depreciation, and the Levelized View

Two kinds of royalty

The Government of Nepal collects two separate charges from every operating hydropower project. Both are called "royalty", but they work very differently. Both step up sharply after the project's fifteenth year of operation.

  • Capacity royalty — a fixed yearly charge per kilowatt of installed capacity. It is due whatever the plant generates, like a fixed rent for using the river.
  • Energy royalty — a percentage of the plant's gross energy revenue (or, in some versions, of the value of energy generated). It rises and falls with how much electricity is actually sold.

Rates in recent years give the broad picture below. Like all Nepali tax and royalty rates, they can be changed through the yearly Finance Act and Electricity Regulatory Commission decisions, so always confirm current rates before modelling a live deal. Rates differ for domestic sale versus export, and for run-of-river versus storage, because the government charges storage, peaking and export projects more, given their larger revenue potential.

REGULATORY DETAIL The royalty step-up at year 15 is one of the most important single dates in a Nepali hydropower model. Energy royalty on domestic sale can roughly quintuple (from under 2% to about 10% of revenue), and capacity royalty can rise tenfold or more. It comes just when the original loan is usually nearly repaid. So the step-up mostly hits the owners' later-year cash and any levelized return, not the ability to repay debt, which is usually planned to be cleared before year 15.

Generous tax breaks

Hydropower also enjoys one of the most generous tax-break regimes of any sector in Nepal. The government has long wanted private money in electricity, given how little of Nepal's huge hydropower potential is built.

For projects reaching COD within windows set by successive Finance Acts, the common pattern has been:

  • a full (100%) income tax rebate for an initial period, often ten years from COD;
  • a partial (commonly 50%) rebate for a further period, often five more years;
  • then full tax at the normal rate for the rest of the PPA — 20 percent for hydropower, which is taxed as a special industry (most other companies pay 25 percent).

On top of this, hydropower companies usually pay reduced customs duty on imported plant, machinery and equipment (Nepal makes almost no turbines or major electro-mechanical equipment), and no value-added tax (VAT) on qualifying imported equipment not made at home. Both cut the cost of building, not the yearly operating cash.

REGULATORY DETAIL A commonly cited tax-holiday structure is: 100% rebate (zero corporate income tax) for the first 10 years of operation, then a 50% rebate on the standard rate for years 11 to 15, then full tax. Because the annual Finance Act sets and adjusts this, any live model should be checked against the Finance Act in force when the project reaches COD; the number of holiday years and the rebate percentages have changed over successive budgets.

Why one year tells you little: the levelized view

The tax holiday, the royalty step-up and the tariff freeze all happen at different points. So one year's numbers — even a carefully chosen "typical" year — tell you very little about whether the project is a good investment over its whole 25–30-year PPA.

Practitioners therefore use levelized analysis. Instead of looking year by year, it squeezes the whole multi-decade stream of costs or revenues into one equal yearly figure, using a discount rate so that rupees from different years can be compared (a rupee in 20 years is worth less than a rupee today).

The best-known measure is the Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE): the constant price per kWh which, if received every year of the plant's life, would exactly cover all its capital and operating costs, discounted back to the start of construction. It lets you compare different sites or technologies fairly, regardless of any one PPA's split, escalating-then-flat tariff.

KEY CONCEPT Levelized analysis turns an uneven, decades-long stream of cash — seasonal, escalating for a few years, then flat, broken by a royalty step-up at year 15 and tax changes around years 10 and 15 — into one equivalent constant figure, using time-value-of-money discounting. It answers "what are the true average economics of this asset over its whole life?" — a question no single year's DSCR or profit can answer.

The owner's view: equity IRR

For an owner, the levelized idea leads to the project's equity Internal Rate of Return (IRR): the single yearly rate at which the present value of all future cash to owners (dividends, plus any exit or end value) exactly equals the equity they put in.

Nepali hydropower cash has an unusual shape:

  1. years of zero revenue during construction;
  2. tight but improving DSCR and modest dividends during the tax-holiday years while the loan is being repaid;
  3. a step down in kept cash as the tax holiday ends;
  4. a further step down after the year-15 royalty rise — but by then the original loan is usually mostly or fully repaid.

So investors judging a project's equity IRR should always ask for the full year-by-year projection across the whole PPA, not a "steady-state" year picked from the middle. No single year in a Nepali hydropower project's life truly stands for the whole.

CAUTION Beware any hydropower investment memorandum that shows a single "typical operating year" DSCR or owner cash flow without the full year-by-year schedule. With so many step-changes — escalation ending around year 8, the tax holiday tapering around years 10–15, and royalty stepping up at year 15 — one chosen year can look far better, deliberately or not, than the levelized reality over the full term.

Chapter recap

  • A hydropower model asks one question: will the river earn enough, for long enough, to repay lenders and reward owners? It treats the construction period (no revenue; spending piles up as CWIP, with IDC capitalised) and the operating period from COD (revenue, costs, royalty and tax, debt service) differently.
  • NEA PPAs pay a higher dry-season tariff (about NPR 8.40/kWh) than wet-season (about NPR 4.80/kWh), with escalation of about 3% a year for eight years, then flat. Required COD and Deemed COD clauses matter as much as the tariff.
  • Energy forecasts come as probabilities: P50 (the middle year, for owners' returns) and P90 or P99 (cautious, for sizing debt). They can differ by 10–20% or more. Always ask which one a figure uses.
  • In the 25 MW example, IDC lifts cost from NPR 5.4 billion to about NPR 6.0 billion. Every month of delay adds interest and can cost escalation years.
  • DSCR = CFADS ÷ debt service; covenants are commonly 1.20–1.30x on the P90 case, and a breach usually locks up dividends. In the Himal Khola table, Year 1 is tightest at 1.28x, and a 5% shortfall pulls it towards 1.21x.
  • Royalty steps up sharply after year 15; the tax holiday is commonly 10 years at 100% then 5 years at 50%, with hydropower then taxed at 20%. Because so much changes over time, judge a project on its full-term, levelized picture and equity IRR, never one "typical" year.
