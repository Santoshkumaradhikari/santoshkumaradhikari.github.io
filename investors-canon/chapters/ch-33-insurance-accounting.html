WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Premium — what a policyholder pays the insurer. Claim — what the insurer pays when the insured event happens. IBNR — "incurred but not reported": claims that have happened but not yet been made, estimated by the actuary. Contractual service margin (CSM) — under NFRS 17, the profit an insurer expects from policies, released slowly over their life, like a school fee paid in advance and earned term by term. Solvency ratio — the capital an insurer holds divided by the capital the regulator requires; 1.30 is the target. Actuary — a specialist who estimates future claims using statistics.

Reserves swell, reported profit stays flat, and the share price does something else entirely. At this point most Nepali investors give up on an insurer's accounts and look at its dividend history instead. That is an expensive mistake.

Insurance is one of the few NEPSE sectors where the accounting itself — not just the business — decides what the reported number means in a given year. An insurer's income statement is not a simple scorecard like a manufacturer's. It is the output of actuarial assumptions, a regulatory reserving formula and, since FY 2082/83, a new accounting standard that Nepal's insurers are still learning to apply consistently. This chapter builds the tools to read through that machinery.

Lesson 33.1 — Why an Insurer's Balance Sheet Looks Nothing Like a Normal Company's

Selling a promise, not a product

A shop sells goods today and mostly knows what they cost today. An insurer sells a promise today — to pay a claim next month, next year or in thirty years — and does not know the final cost until the promise is fully kept. Between the premium received and the claim paid lies a long, uncertain gap. All of insurance accounting exists to estimate that gap, provide for it and revise it.

Two things dominate the balance sheet

  • Technical reserves — liabilities for future claims and for cover not yet provided.
  • A large investment portfolio — government securities, bank fixed deposits, corporate debentures and listed shares.

For a life insurer such as Nepal Life Insurance or a non-life insurer such as Shikhar or Prabhu Insurance, the investment portfolio is several times larger than paid-up capital and free reserves. It is not a side detail. It is a second business run alongside insurance, and often the more important one for shareholders (Lesson 33.6).

KEY CONCEPT An insurer sells a promise, not a product. Its balance sheet is a running estimate of the cost of promises not yet kept — the technical reserves — funded by premiums already collected and invested. Reading an insurer means questioning that estimate, not just following the cash.

Life and non-life are different businesses

  • Non-life (fire, motor, marine, engineering, micro-insurance) writes short-tail contracts, usually for twelve months. Claims are usually known and settled within a year or two.
  • Life writes long-tail contracts. An endowment or term policy can run twenty or thirty years or more, so the insurer is pricing mortality and investment returns decades ahead.

That is why life insurers carry actuarial liabilities far larger than non-life insurers relative to premium, and why the appointed actuary (Lesson 33.4) is a permanent part of a life insurer's governance. Comparing a life insurer's ratios with a non-life insurer's is close to meaningless — like comparing a mango orchard with a vegetable plot.

The regulator's new name

For decades the regulator was Beema Samiti (the Insurance Board). Under the Insurance Act, 2079 (2022), it became the Nepal Insurance Authority (NIA), operating from August 2022 with wider powers over licensing, solvency, actuarial work and market conduct. Every directive in this chapter — the Financial Statement Directive, the Risk-Based Capital and Solvency Directive and the actuary guideline — is an NIA instrument, even though older filings and some data sites still say Beema Samiti.

REGULATORY DETAIL Beema Samiti became the Nepal Insurance Authority under the Insurance Act, 2079, starting operations in August 2022. A document that refers to "Beema Samiti" comes from before that change; the rules are now issued and enforced by the NIA.

Lesson 33.2 — NFRS 17 Arrives: Nepal's Insurance Contracts Standard

When it started

The biggest change to insurance accounting on NEPSE in a decade is NFRS 17 (Insurance Contracts), Nepal's version of IFRS 17. The Accounting Standards Board (ASB) moved its start date more than once; its current table lists 17 July 2024. In practice, listed insurers began publishing NFRS 17 accounts a year later. Shikhar Insurance was first, with its report for the first quarter of FY 2082/83, published in Kartik 2082 (November 2025). So FY 2082/83 (from mid-July 2025) is the first year of NFRS 17 reporting, and comparisons with earlier years need care.

What it changes

Under the old system, an insurer mostly counted premium as revenue when written, or spread evenly over the policy term, and set reserves with regulator formulas only loosely linked to the real economics of the contracts.

NFRS 17 instead measures contracts using:

  • a current, discounted, probability-weighted estimate of future cash flows;
  • plus a risk adjustment for non-financial risk;
  • plus — critically — a contractual service margin (CSM): a liability holding the profit the insurer expects to make on a group of contracts, released into income slowly as the insurer actually provides the cover, not booked up front.

Why the CSM matters

The CSM is why NFRS 17 profit and cash profit differ. A life insurer can write a large, profitable batch of new policies in a quarter and still show modest profit, because most of the expected profit sits in the CSM, waiting to be released over the policies' lives.

So under NFRS 17, growth temporarily lowers reported profit — the opposite of most sectors. Think of a school that collects a full year's fees in Baisakh: it has the cash, but it earns the fee month by month as it teaches.

KEY CONCEPT The contractual service margin (CSM) is the unearned profit on policies in force, held as a liability and released into income as cover is provided. A fast-growing insurer can show weak current profit under NFRS 17 while writing very profitable new business — the profit is deferred on the balance sheet, not missing.

A difficult transition

Nepal's move has not been smooth. Industry commentary through 2024 and 2025 flagged the same obstacles again and again:

  • a severe shortage of actuaries and specialist IT staff to build the cash-flow and CSM models;
  • impact studies that stayed descriptive rather than working through the numbers;
  • staff training that stopped at basic awareness;
  • the cost of data systems to track contract groups, discount rates and risk adjustments over decades.

As late as mid-2026, the ASB and the NIA were still holding joint sessions on implementation questions — a year after insurers began reporting under the standard. Do not assume two insurers made the same judgements on discount rates, contract boundaries or risk adjustments; the standard is principles-based and practice is still settling.

WARNING NFRS 17 is a set of principles, not a fixed formula, and Nepal's insurers were still building common practice in FY 2082/83, their first year under it. Two insurers' NFRS 17 profits are not automatically comparable: differences in discount rates, how contracts are grouped and risk-adjustment methods can each move profit a lot with no difference in the underlying business.

The regulator's response: dividends from the lower profit

NFRS 17 profit and profit under the NIA's own Financial Statement Directive, 2023 can differ a lot, depending on how much profit is held in the CSM. Insurers could report one figure and use it to propose dividends the regulator judged unwise given their real cash and capital.

The NIA's response, tightened from the fourth quarter of FY 2025/26:

  • insurers prepare two sets of quarterly accounts — one under NFRS 17, one under the Financial Statement Directive;
  • dividends may be paid only from the lower of the two profits;
  • where NFRS 17 retained earnings are higher, the excess goes to a non-distributable regulatory reserve, released only with NIA approval;
  • bonuses added to participating life policies were tied to the Risk-Based Capital and Solvency Directive rather than to whichever profit looked more generous.
CASE IN POINT From the fourth quarter of FY 2025/26, a listed insurer cannot simply declare a dividend from its NFRS 17 profit. It must also work out profit under the Financial Statement Directive, 2023, and distribute only the lower figure, with any NFRS 17 surplus locked in a regulatory reserve. Read the dividend notes in the annual report before projecting from the headline profit.

The upside is real: comparability later on. Once insurers converge on common practice, NFRS 17's use of current assumptions and one CSM method should make it far easier to compare one life insurer's book with another's — across the dozen or so listed life insurers and a similar number of non-life insurers. Your job now is to read the transitional numbers correctly, not to wait for that to finish.

Lesson 33.3 — Premium Recognition: When Does an Insurer Actually Earn Its Revenue

Even after NFRS 17, the timing of premium is the easiest place to start reading an insurer's income statement, and it differs between non-life and life.

Non-life: the unearned premium reserve

A one-year motor policy sold on 1 Poush has not been "earned" on that day. The insurer has taken on twelve months of risk and has been paid in advance for a service not yet delivered. It counts the premium as income gradually over the year (NFRS 17 calls this releasing the "liability for remaining coverage") and holds the rest as a liability: the unearned premium reserve (UPR).

An insurer that wrote a lot of new business in the last month of the year shows a big cash and premium inflow — and a big rise in UPR, so little of it reaches earned income yet. If you look only at gross written premium growth and skip the UPR, you will overstate how much growth has become income.

Life: matched against long-term liabilities

A life policy is not one risk period but a bundle of long-term obligations. Premium counts as revenue when due, but is matched against growing actuarial liabilities (under NFRS 17, the liability for remaining coverage: discounted future cash flows plus risk adjustment plus CSM).

So a life insurer's premium line says almost nothing about profit on its own. A policy priced too cheaply for its mortality and investment assumptions can produce strong premium growth for years while quietly destroying value. It is the actuarial reserve movement, not the premium line, that eventually reveals it.

Premium growth is volume. Reserve movement is where the economics live.

PRACTICAL TOOL In a non-life insurer's quarterly report, compare the growth of gross written premium with the growth of the unearned premium reserve. If UPR grows much faster than earned premium, the insurer is loading new business into the last weeks of the period. Check it against earlier years before calling it sustainable growth.

Micro-insurance

Nepal also has micro-insurance — small policies sold in large numbers — with its own listed companies, including Nepal Micro Insurance and Crest Micro Life. The same principles apply, but policies are shorter and far more numerous, so calculating UPR and claims reserves policy by policy is a much heavier job relative to premium. Expect these newer, smaller insurers to lag the established ones in NFRS 17 maturity.

Lesson 33.4 — Claims Reserving and IBNR: Where the Appointed Actuary Earns Their Fee

The most judgement-heavy number

Premium timing tells you when an insurer books revenue. Claims reserving tells you whether it has honestly estimated what that revenue will cost. It is the most judgement-heavy number in insurance accounts, and the one most likely to be quietly wrong for years.

Three kinds of claims

  1. Reported and admitted — the customer has claimed, the loss is assessed, and a reserve is booked.
  2. Reported but not settled (RBNS) — still being assessed or disputed, but known.
  3. Incurred but not reported (IBNR) — losses that have already happened but have not yet been claimed. An accident in the last week of the year may be claimed weeks later; in liability lines, a loss may not surface for years.

IBNR cannot be read from a claims register, because no claim exists yet. It must be estimated statistically, usually by projecting how past years' claims grew over time onto the current year's business. It is like a cooperative estimating how many members' cattle fell sick this month, before all the reports have come in from the far villages.

WARNING IBNR is built entirely from past claims patterns, not from known losses. An insurer can under-reserve for years — flattering profit each year — with the gap showing only when patterns change (more lawsuits, a catastrophe year, a different mix of policies) and past reserves must be topped up all at once. A sudden, large adjustment to prior-year reserves is a red flag, not a technicality.

The appointed actuary

This is where the appointed actuary matters. The NIA's Guideline Related to Actuary Appointment for Insurers, 2024 (2081) formalised the role: the professional legally responsible for certifying reserve adequacy, mortality and illness assumptions (for life business) and solvency calculations.

Because Nepal has few actuaries, the NIA also required every insurer, from 16 July 2024, to build a supporting team of actuarial analysts, whose performance reviews must include the appointed actuary's feedback — formalising a reporting line that smaller insurers had lacked.

REGULATORY DETAIL Every insurer must now have an appointed actuary (who signs off reserves and solvency) and a team of actuarial analysts, required across the sector from 16 July 2024. This answered a shortage of actuarial skills flagged repeatedly during the NFRS 17 transition. Treat an insurer's actuarial strength as a real credit and governance signal, not a compliance footnote.

What an outside investor can watch

Without access to an insurer's internal claims data, watch two things across annual reports:

  • whether last year's claims reserves are later released as excess (they were set cautiously) or strengthened (they were too thin);
  • whether the actuarial valuation report — which insurers must obtain, and which annual reports increasingly summarise — flags changes in key assumptions such as discount rates, mortality tables or expense loadings.

A change in the discount rate alone can move a life insurer's liability, and so its profit, a lot — without one more policy sold or claim paid. That is why NFRS 17's requirement to disclose how reserves respond to key assumptions is so useful, once insurers report it specifically rather than in boilerplate.

CAUTION A reserve release that lifts this year's profit is not automatically bad, but check whether it reflects genuinely better claims or just a changed assumption. Releases that recur every year in exactly the amount needed to keep profit flat or rising deserve scepticism, not celebration.

Lesson 33.5 — Solvency Margin and Risk-Based Capital: The Regulator's Real Lever

What the solvency ratio measures

Solvency is the number the Insurance Authority cares about most, because it measures directly whether an insurer can pay the claims it has promised. The solvency ratio is available capital divided by the capital the regulator requires for the insurer's risks. Above 1.0 means more than the bare minimum; the higher, the bigger the cushion.

The risk-based directive and its warning bands

Nepal moved from a simple factor-based formula to a full Risk-Based Capital and Solvency Directive, first issued by the Insurance Board in 2078 (approved 26 January 2022, applied from Shrawan 2080), then reissued by the Insurance Authority in 2081 (2024) and 2082 (2025), and issued again as the Risk-Based Capital and Solvency Directive, 2083 (2026) — a sign the regulator is still adjusting it.

The target is the same for life and non-life: a solvency ratio of 130 percent (1.30 times). Below it, the directive works like warning lights on a bus dashboard:

  • 100–130%: the insurer must submit a plan and may be barred from paying cash dividends;
  • 70–100%: the regulator can restrict new business, investments and dividends, and change management;
  • 45–70%: it can stop new business altogether;
  • below 45%: the licence itself is at risk (Clickmandu and Beema Post, September 2026).

Some older reports and earlier editions of this chapter cite a 1.5 floor for non-life insurers; under the risk-based directive the published target is 1.30 for both. Full enforcement is being phased in, with the final step reported for the end of Asar 2084 (mid-July 2027).

Non-life insurers run well above the target. Fourth-quarter figures for FY 2082/83 (the year to mid-July 2026) for fourteen non-life insurers (Beema Post, 8 September 2026):

  • Life insurers tell a very different story. Two lessons follow. First, being above the minimum is now normal for non-life insurers, so it is not praise on its own; compare with peers and with the insurer's own trend. Second, the spread — 5.85 down to 2.73 among non-life insurers — is real. A company persistently near the bottom of its group has less room to absorb a bad claims year or a fall in its investments.

PRACTICAL TOOL Never read one insurer's solvency ratio alone. Build a simple peer table each quarter from NIA and insurance-media disclosures, and rank the insurer against its direct peers — life against life, non-life against non-life. A non-life ratio of 2.7 looks safe against 1.30 but is less than half the leader's cushion. A life ratio of 1.35 is different: one bad year could trigger dividend limits.

Capital floors and forced mergers

The insurance sector also went through a merger wave driven by a capital increase. The regulator raised minimum paid-up capital to Rs 5 arba for life insurers and Rs 2.5 arba for non-life insurers, extending the deadline to Asar 2080 (mid-July 2023) when most insurers found the first timeline unworkable. Insurers that could not raise the money through rights or bonus shares had to merge or fall short.

CASE IN POINT Surya Life Insurance and Jyoti Life Insurance merged to form Suryajyoti Life Insurance, operating jointly from Poush 7, 2079 (20 December 2022). Neither could reach Rs 500 crore alone: Surya Life had about Rs 255 crore and Jyoti Life about Rs 242 crore — Rs 497 crore together, still just short, so the merged company needed a small top-up. This was a capital-driven merger, not a strategic one, and a template for how Nepal's insurance mergers have actually worked.

Non-life saw the same: Sanima General Insurance and General Insurance Company merged into Sanima GIC Insurance against the Rs 2.5 arba floor. Capital directives in Nepal's insurance sector have directly reshaped which companies exist on NEPSE. A shareholder in a small, thinly capitalised insurer should treat "the regulator may raise the floor again" as a live scenario with real merger, dilution or delisting consequences.

Lesson 33.6 — Investment Income: The Engine Behind the Underwriting Number

Two accounts in one company

Where does an insurer's profit actually come from? For most Nepali insurers, not mainly from insurance itself. The income statement splits into:

  • a technical account — premiums, claims, reserve movements and underwriting costs: the pure insurance business;
  • an investment account — income on the large pool of invested premiums ("float"): government securities, bank deposits, debentures and listed shares, plus some property and other assets allowed by NIA investment rules.

Why investment income carries the bottom line

For a typical non-life insurer, underwriting margins are thin, and sometimes negative in a bad claims year. Interest on deposits and government securities, dividends, and gains on investments carry the profit.

For life insurers the link is tighter still. A long life policy works only if the insurer earns at least the investment return assumed when the policy was priced. The actuarial reserve discounts future claims at that assumed yield, so if actual returns keep falling short, the gap slowly drains solvency — showing up as reserve top-ups long before any headline loss.

Why insurer share prices follow the market and interest rates

When NEPSE rallies, insurers with sizeable share portfolios show gains in investment income. When bank deposit rates fall, insurers rolling over large deposits slowly earn less, which eventually squeezes profit and the assumptions behind their reserves. Analysing an insurer only by loss ratio, expense ratio and combined ratio — without tracking its investment portfolio's mix, yield and sensitivity to rates and the market — misses the half that usually matters more.

KEY CONCEPT Split every insurer's profit in two: the technical result (premium minus claims, reserve movements and expenses) and the investment result (yield on the float). For most Nepali insurers, especially life insurers, the investment result is the bigger and steadier driver. Underwriting discipline matters, but it is not the whole story — sometimes not even the main one.
CAUTION Do not mistake a strong year driven by unrealised share gains in a NEPSE rally for a lasting improvement in underwriting. In the notes, separate realised investment income (interest and dividends actually received) from unrealised value gains, and weight them very differently when looking ahead — unrealised gains reverse when the index turns.

Chapter recap

  • An insurer sells promises. Its balance sheet is dominated by technical reserves and a large investment portfolio. Life (long-tail) and non-life (short-tail) are different businesses; do not compare their ratios. The regulator is now the Nepal Insurance Authority (formerly Beema Samiti).
  • NFRS 17 — ASB date 17 July 2024; listed insurers first reported under it in FY 2082/83 — measures contracts with discounted cash flows, a risk adjustment and a contractual service margin that releases profit over time, so growth can depress reported profit. Practice is still settling.
  • From the fourth quarter of FY 2025/26, insurers may pay dividends only from the lower of NFRS 17 and Financial Statement Directive profit, with any excess locked in a regulatory reserve.
  • Watch the unearned premium reserve (non-life) and actuarial reserve movements (life); premium growth alone says little.
  • IBNR is estimated from past patterns and is where reserving can be quietly wrong. Watch reserve releases and strengthening, and assumption changes. Appointed actuaries and actuarial analysts are required (analysts since 16 July 2024).
  • The risk-based solvency target is 1.30 for both life and non-life, with warning bands below it. Non-life averaged 4.35 at mid-July 2026; life only 1.84 after the risk-based method. Capital floors (Rs 5 arba life, Rs 2.5 arba non-life) drove mergers such as Suryajyoti Life and Sanima GIC.
  • For most insurers, investment income drives profit. Separate the technical and investment results, and realised from unrealised gains.
