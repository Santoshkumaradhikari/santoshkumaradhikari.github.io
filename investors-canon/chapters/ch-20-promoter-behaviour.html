WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Pledge — using shares as security for a loan, like pawning gold at the bank: if the loan is not repaid, the lender sells the gold. Lock-in — the years during which promoter shares cannot be sold. OTC transfer — a sale outside the normal NEPSE order book, usually by auction for promoter shares. Rights issue — new shares offered to existing holders, in Nepal usually at Rs 100. Unsubscribed ("devolved") shares — rights shares nobody took up; the company auctions them. ISIN — a share's identification code, like a citizenship number for a security.

Some losses on NEPSE come from bad years. Others come from the people in control. A bank can report a clean quarter and a comfortable capital ratio, yet still be leaking value: a contract handed to a promoter's brother-in-law, a rights issue that squeezes shareholders without spare cash, or promoter shares quietly pledged against a personal loan that nobody notices until the lender starts selling them into a falling market. None of this shows up in a normal ratio check. All of it shows up in the share price in the end — usually when you can least afford it.

NEPSE has three features that make this risk bigger than in large markets: most companies are controlled by a founding family or business group, the regulator is still building its enforcement strength, and disclosure tells you what a promoter did months after they did it — if at all. This chapter gives you the tools to price that risk on purpose instead of discovering it the hard way.

Lesson 20.1 — Why Promoter Behaviour Is NEPSE's Most Underanalysed Risk

Two kinds of share, one big difference

Nepali companies, and banks above all, have two kinds of share: promoter shares and public (ordinary) shares. Both have the same face value, the same dividend per share and the same vote per share. The difference is how easily they can be sold.

  • For most companies, promoter shares are locked for three years from the IPO allotment under SEBON's Securities Registration and Issue Regulation, 2073.
  • For hydropower companies, the lock-in is set in each company's prospectus and is often shorter (Chapter 13).
  • For banks and financial institutions, promoters may not sell for at least five years from the start of operations, and large transfers need NRB approval.

Public shares trade on NEPSE from the first day. So the promoter group is a separate, restricted class of owner. Its shares are "sticky". In theory that is good — owners who cannot easily leave should care about the long term. In practice, because board seats follow shareholding and small shareholders rarely attend AGMs, sticky ownership becomes permanent control. The families who listed a company ten years ago usually still choose its CEO, approve its related-party contracts and set its dividends today.

CONTEXT Under NRB policy, commercial banks keep about 51 percent of their shares with promoters, and promoter shares are released to the public in limited batches rather than all at once. NRB does this so a bank's control cannot change hands overnight. The side effect: public shareholders in a Nepali bank are, by design, permanent minority owners. Short of a merger or an NRB-forced change, they cannot join together to take control.

Weak enforcement and patchy information

The second problem is enforcement. SEBON is small compared with regulators in larger markets. Its record so far — a broker fine here, an insider-trading case there, and a 2024 bill to raise penalties whose passage this book could not confirm (Chapter 21) — is that of an institution still building its strength in a market where the same families sit on many boards and know the auditors, issue managers and brokers who are meant to check them.

The third problem is information. CDS and Clearing Limited (CDSC), the NEPSE subsidiary that runs the depository, tags each holding as promoter or public and blocks transfers of locked shares. But for most companies both kinds of share carry the same identification code (ISIN — think of it as a citizenship number for a share), so the difference is not visible in ordinary market data. A 2025 CDSC draft proposed separate codes; in early 2026 the dispute over it held up the listing of promoter shares for about a dozen companies, and it was still unresolved at this check (Chapter 11, Chapter 13). Related-party deals — the main way value leaks to promoters — are disclosed only in broad totals. So the people best able to take value are the least likely to be caught quickly, and small shareholders are the least able to see it coming.

Put simply: Nepali share analysis spends a lot of time on ratios — EPS growth, interest margins, provisions, capital — and little on the people above those numbers. That imbalance is why promoter behaviour stays underanalysed. The risk is not small; the tools to measure it have not caught up.

Lesson 20.2 — The Incentive Misalignment: Promoter Economics versus Minority Economics

Start from money, not morals. Promoters are not villains and small shareholders are not helpless. Both respond to their incentives. Your job is to see where those incentives point the same way as a rising share price, and where they point away.

Where interests line up

A promoter with a large, locked, hard-to-sell stake has strong reasons to grow the business. Their wealth is tied up in it, their family name is on it, and they cannot easily sell after a bad quarter. A family that plans to hand the business to its children wants it to grow for decades. When this holds, small shareholders ride along with a committed, well-informed owner.

Where interests split

Trouble starts when the promoter can take value out without the share price rising. Three common ways:

  • Related-party business. A promoter who also owns a supplier, contractor, insurance agency or building can steer company business there at better-than-market terms. The promoter keeps that margin alone instead of sharing profit with every shareholder.
  • Soft control of management. A promoter who picks the CEO can reward loyalty with high pay or easy targets.
  • Pledging. A promoter who needs cash can pledge shares as security for a personal loan — like pawning gold at the bank. The promoter gets cash without a public sale. But if the loan goes bad, the lender sells the shares.
WARNING Pledged promoter shares are a hidden forced-sale risk. If the promoter's personal loan goes wrong, the lender can sell the shares into the market whatever the company's results. Because pledges are not reported promptly and in detail, this usually stays hidden until the selling starts — and then it looks like a sudden, unexplained collapse.

Rights issues: where the split becomes measurable

Nepali companies usually issue rights shares at Rs 100 (par value), however high the market price. A shareholder who cannot pay loses most of the value of the entitlement. Rights cannot yet be sold on NEPSE (a SEBON proposal of 19 November 2026 may change this), and unsubscribed ("devolved") shares go to a company auction open to investors generally, with the auction money going to the company (Chapter 15). So value moves away from whoever lacks cash on the day — towards the company and the auction buyers. The shareholders best placed to keep their full share are those with cash ready: most often the promoter group, which is organised, well funded and often advised by the merchant banker running the issue.

Small shareholders, by contrast, often face a rights issue at a bad moment for their own cash. They must find fresh money or accept a smaller slice of the company. Over several rights issues, this lets the promoter group keep or raise its share of control, partly paid for by small holders who could not keep up. None of this is illegal. That is exactly why you should read a company's rights history — how often, at what price, who took up the shares and who did not — as governance information, not a footnote.

Lesson 20.3 — The Promoter Playbook: Tactics to Watch For

Promoters rarely need to break the law to keep control or take value. The usual tactics are legal, which is why they survive. You have to learn to spot them yourself.

Related-party transactions

Many Nepali business groups span banking, insurance, hydropower, hotels and trading under one family. The common tactic is to steer business — supplies, insurance, rent, inter-company loans — to group companies at terms an outsider would not get. A December 2025 commentary on Nepali bank governance put it plainly: related-party loans "often enjoy preferential treatment," reflecting boards that "remain heavily influenced by promoter interests." Do not wait for proof in the annual report; disclosure is rarely detailed enough. Treat heavy related-party dealing, cross-shareholdings and shared directors as a standing discount on the quality of reported profits.

Packing the board — and the NCC Bank lesson

Board seats follow shareholding. With locked, stable shares and scattered public owners who rarely vote, a promoter group can fill the board with loyal directors year after year. When two promoter groups fight, everyone can lose. In early 2014, a dispute between two promoter factions at Nepal Credit and Commerce Bank — one holding 51 percent — left the board unable to meet for three months. On 4 February 2014 NRB suspended the board, put a three-member NRB team in charge and told it to raise paid-up capital to Rs 2 billion (Kathmandu Post, 5 February 2014). Board fights are not just a small shareholder's complaint; they can hurt the institution itself.

Timing a rights issue

Promoters can also choose when to call for money — for example, when they have just received dividends or other cash, and when small shareholders are least ready. Unsubscribed rights shares go to a company auction open to investors generally (Chapter 15), so promoters have no special right to them. But a group that knows early how much will go unsubscribed, and has cash ready, can plan its bids far better than scattered small shareholders can. After the auction, check the company's shareholding report to see who actually bought the shares, rather than guessing.

Knowing first

Promoter directors see monthly, sometimes weekly, figures — loan quality, deposits, claims, project delays. The market sees them a quarter later, in a polished report. Many NEPSE companies have no independent analyst following them, so the only story the market hears is management's own. Treat any company with a dominant promoter and no independent research as a case of unequal information by default.

RED FLAG Watch for promoter-linked directors buying shares just before strong results, or reducing their holdings just before weak ones. Insider-trading enforcement has relied on a few high-profile cases, and SEBON's own call in 2026 for "zero tolerance" shows it knows detection is still low. Weak enforcement is a reason for more care, not less.

Lesson 20.4 — Reading Promoter Shareholding Trends as a Signal

Related-party deals and board politics are hard to see. The promoter shareholding percentage over time is one of the few numbers you can actually track — if you read it carefully.

First, separate a fall caused by the promoter's own action from a fall caused by arithmetic. If a company issues new shares and the promoter takes up its full share, its percentage may still change for mechanical reasons; that is neutral. If the promoter is selling through off-market (OTC) transfers, that is a real signal about the controlling owner's view — or about its need for cash.

Promoter signalWhat it may mean
Promoter buying more, in the market or through an approved transferConfidence; willing to put in more money at today's price
Promoter stake steady through a rights issue (full take-up)Still committed; no intent to dilute small holders
Promoter stake falling through OTC sales, with no stated reasonLost confidence, family exit or cash need — find out before assuming it is harmless
Promoter pledging rising or newly reportedPersonal money stress; hidden forced-sale risk
Promoter buying many of the auctioned (unsubscribed) rights sharesControl tightening at the expense of holders who could not pay
Promoter shares converted and sold heavily right after lock-in endsA classic lock-in dump; expect short-term price pressure

Second, mark lock-in expiry dates on your calendar. When a lock-in ends, a large block of shares can come up for sale at once, like a dam opening its gates. SEBON requires listed companies to announce the end of a promoter lock-in 30 days in advance, and since October 2026 promoters must give five trading days' notice before selling 15 percent or more of their holding (Chapter 9). Use those notices to review your position before the event, not after.

Third, remember that the shareholding numbers you see can be late, and that one shared ISIN hides the split between locked and free shares. Cross-check against the company's own notices, recent rights results and any SEBON notice about that company.

Lesson 20.5 — Red Flags: Pledging, Below-Market OTC Transfers, and Related-Party Contracts

Shares pledged against personal debt

This is the most dangerous and least visible flag. A promoter who pledges a large part of the holding has tied the share price to a personal loan. If that loan goes wrong — a failed side business, a lender's call, a credit squeeze like Nepal's in recent cycles — the lender can sell the shares, whatever the company's condition. Nepal does not require prompt, standard disclosure of promoter pledges, so it often shows up only as steady, unexplained selling. Treat any credible sign that a promoter has pledged a large share of its stake — consistent reports from several sources, or a note in a lender's filing — as a serious warning.

Promoter shares sold below the going rate

Promoter shares change hands off the main order book, usually by auction for large blocks, and bank promoter transfers need NRB checks on the buyer and the source of money. Promoter shares usually sell at a discount to public shares because they cannot be traded freely. So a single sale far below recent promoter-share deals deserves a question. Is it a distressed sale by a promoter who needs cash fast? Is it one related party passing a stake cheaply to another inside the same group, at other shareholders' expense? Or is it just a thin market? Only the last answer is harmless.

Related-party contracts with no price check

Related-party deals are common in diversified business groups and are not wrong in themselves. The warning sign is the absence of any stated price check or independent approval. If an annual report shows a promoter-linked insurance agent placing the company's cover, a promoter-linked contractor building its branches, or a linked finance company on the other side of loans — and says nothing about tendering, market comparison or review by independent directors — assume a discount to reported profits until shown otherwise.

WARNING No scandal is not the same as clean governance. It may only mean disclosure is thin and nobody independent is looking. Treat large, opaque related-party dealing as a standing discount on profits, not a risk that only starts once it is exposed.

Lesson 20.6 — A Practical Promoter Quality Scorecard

Bring the lessons together in one repeatable check. Run it before you buy, then again at every results season, rights issue and lock-in expiry.

What to checkGood signBad sign
Shareholding trendPromoter stake steady or rising, through market buying or full rights take-upStake falling through OTC sales with no clear, harmless reason
PledgingNo credible sign of pledging, or pledging disclosed and smallCredible reports of large, hidden or rising pledges
Rights historyIssues explained; price with some regard to value; wide take-upRepeated issues timed badly for small holders; promoters buying most auctioned shares
BoardGenuinely independent directors with stated qualificationsBoard of family members, employees and long-time associates
Related-party disclosureCounterparties named, amounts and terms given, price checks mentionedOnly totals; no terms or approval process
Dividends and capitalDividends in line with profits and capital needsPayouts that jump around events linked to promoters' cash needs
Regulatory and dispute historyNo SEBON or NRB action, board takeover or promoter fightRegulatory action, board takeovers or public promoter disputes
InformationSome independent research; timely, detailed disclosureNo independent coverage; legal minimum only, often late

No company scores well on every row, and one bad row is rarely enough to rule a company out. A dominant but disciplined promoter with a clean rights history is a very different risk from one with pledging rumours and a recent board fight. The point is to give governance the same written, scheduled attention you give to ratios. For the book's official score, record your answers in the Governance dimension of the Canon Score (Chapter 64), where a promoter check of 0 — for example, repeated unexplained promoter selling — caps the whole score at 54.

CONTEXT On NEPSE there is no tradition of activist investors, small shareholders rarely organise at AGMs, independent research is thin outside the largest companies, and SEBON and NRB are still building their enforcement tools. Until that changes, your own checking is the main defence.

Chapter recap

  • Promoter behaviour is NEPSE's most underanalysed risk because ownership is concentrated and locked, enforcement is still developing, and related-party disclosure is thin.
  • Promoters and small shareholders want the same thing when the promoter's locked stake pushes it to build value. They split when the promoter can take value out through related-party deals, soft control of management or pledging.
  • Rights issues at Rs 100 make the split measurable: shareholders without cash lose most of their entitlement's value (rights cannot yet be traded; unsubscribed shares go to an open company auction), while those with cash — often promoters — keep their full share.
  • Promoter shareholding trends are a real signal if you separate arithmetic changes from active selling, mark lock-in expiry dates, and remember that one shared ISIN hides the locked/free split.
  • The biggest red flags: pledged promoter shares, promoter shares sold far below recent deals, and related-party contracts with no price check or independent approval.
  • Use the scorecard at every results season and corporate action, and carry the answers into the Canon Score's Governance dimension (Chapter 64).
