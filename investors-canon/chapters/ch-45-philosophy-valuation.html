WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Price — what the market charges today; a fact. Value — what the business is really worth; an estimate. Intrinsic value — value worked out from the business's own earnings and assets, like valuing a field by the crops it will grow. Margin of safety — the gap between your value estimate and the price you pay; your protection against mistakes. Valuation method — a way of estimating value, such as DCF, relative valuation, dividend discount or book value. Control premium — the extra a buyer pays to control a company rather than own a small stake.

Lesson 45.1 — Price Is What You See; Value Is What You Are Trying to Find

Three prices for one tomato

Walk through Ason market in Kathmandu any morning. You will see the same tomato selling at three prices at three stalls, all within fifty metres.

  • A tourist pays the highest price.
  • A regular customer who knows the seller pays the middle price.
  • A wholesaler buying forty kilos for a hotel pays the lowest price per kilo.

The tomato has not changed. Its taste, ripeness and use in a meal — its "worth" to whoever eats it — is the same in all three sales. Only the price changed, and it moved for reasons that have nothing to do with the tomato: bargaining skill, information, hurry, relationship and quantity.

Price is what you pay; value is what you get

This gap — between what something costs and what it is really worth — is the most important idea in this Part of the book, and perhaps in all of investing. It is often summed up in a line popularly credited to Warren Buffett, paraphrasing his teacher Benjamin Graham: "Price is what you pay; value is what you get."

The idea is older than Buffett. Graham's 1949 book The Intelligent Investor built a whole philosophy around the gap between a share's market price and its underlying worth. The one-line version became the most quoted sentence in investing because every investor eventually learns it the hard way.

From tomatoes to shares

Now apply this to a share. The price of Nabil Bank, Chilime Hydropower or Unilever Nepal on the NEPSE (Nepal Stock Exchange) screen at 11:45 on a Tuesday is simply where the latest buyer and seller agreed to trade. It tells you what someone was willing to pay a moment ago.

It does not, by itself, tell you what the business is worth — its factories, licences, deposits, power purchase agreements, brand and future profits — to a sensible, well-informed owner planning to hold it for years. Value, in this sense, is an estimate of a business's true worth, built from its assets, earning power, growth and risk. It does not come from what the screen says right now.

KEY CONCEPT Price is a fact: you can see it, it is public, and at any moment there is exactly one. Value is an estimate: you cannot see it, it needs judgment, and sensible analysts using sensible methods can and do reach different numbers for the same company. Valuation exists because the two are not always equal — and the gap between them is where both investment opportunity and investment risk live.

Why price and value drift apart

A market price is set by whoever happens to be trading at that moment, and their reasons may have nothing to do with a careful judgment of worth:

  • Someone needing cash for a hospital bill sells below what they think the shares are worth, because they need money now.
  • Someone who just read an exciting rumour in a Viber group buys above what a calm analysis would support, because they are excited.
  • A large institution rebalancing at month-end sells, not because the business got worse, but because a target percentage says so.

None of these trades is "wrong"; each has a real reason. But none is a statement about intrinsic value.

Intrinsic value

Throughout Part IX, intrinsic value means that patiently estimated, underlying worth of a business. It is the price a sensible, informed buyer with a long horizon should be willing to pay, based on the cash the business should produce over its life, converted into what that future cash is worth today. It is called "intrinsic" to set it apart from market price: it belongs to the business, not to the crowd's mood on a given day.

This is not an academic point for Nepal. It is arguably more relevant here than in almost any other market, for reasons this chapter builds up over six lessons. The starting point is simple. Your job as a serious investor is never to predict what the price will do tomorrow. It is to estimate, as carefully as the information allows, what the business is worth — and then compare that with the price the market offers. Everything else in valuation is a set of tools for making that comparison well.

Lesson 45.2 — Why Different Methods Legitimately Disagree

Newcomers are often unsettled to find that three capable analysts, looking at the same company on the same day with the same public accounts, can produce three quite different values. This is normal and expected. It does not mean valuation is fake or useless.

Understanding why is essential before learning any method. Otherwise you will either trust one number far more than it deserves, or decide valuation is a waste of time because "the answer is never the same twice".

Three ways to value a house in Lalitpur

A house-valuation analogy will keep coming back in this Part, because it matches company valuation unusually well. Suppose you want to know what a house in Lalitpur is worth. There are at least three honest ways to answer, and they need not agree:

  1. Look at what similar houses nearby recently sold for — three bedrooms, similar plot, similar road access — and adjust for differences. This is comparable sales. In company valuation it is relative valuation (also called "comps" or multiples): see what the market now pays for similar companies, as a ratio such as price-to-earnings or price-to-book, and apply that ratio to your company.
  2. Estimate what it would cost today to buy the land and rebuild the same house from scratch — bricks, labour, permits, everything. This is replacement cost or asset-based valuation. In company valuation it is book value or net asset value (NAV): add up what the company's assets are worth (adjusted to reality, not just old accounting cost) and subtract what it owes.
  3. If the house is rented, estimate the rent it will bring in over coming years and work out what that stream of future rupees is worth today. This is income capitalisation. In company valuation it is the discounted cash flow (DCF) method — or, for a bank, where the income that reaches shareholders is the dividend rather than the whole firm's cash, the dividend discount model (DDM).
KEY CONCEPT No single valuation method is "the" correct one — each answers a slightly different question. Comparable sales asks "what does the market pay for something similar now?" Replacement cost asks "what would it take to recreate this from nothing?" Income capitalisation asks "what is the future stream of benefit worth today?" A skilled valuer checks across methods rather than relying on one, and a skilled share analyst does exactly the same.

Why honest valuers disagree

Three honest valuers using these three methods on the same house can reach three different figures, and none is lying:

  • The comparable-sales valuer may land higher if the area is in a speculative boom and recent prices reflect hope rather than lasting worth.
  • The replacement-cost valuer may land lower if land prices rose faster than building costs, making the house itself cheap to rebuild.
  • The income valuer may land lowest of all if rents in the area are low compared with purchase prices.

All three numbers can be "correct" — each worked out properly for its method and assumptions — while still disagreeing, because they measure different things and assume different futures.

Company valuation is the same. Every method needs assumptions, and reasonable people can disagree about them without anyone being incompetent or dishonest:

  • A DCF of a hydropower company depends heavily on the discount rate (the return investors demand for the risk), and on assumptions about river flow, tariff escalation and the terminal value after the power purchase agreement (PPA) ends.
  • A relative valuation of the same company depends on which "comparable" companies you pick, and on whether the market's pricing of them is itself sensible or inflated by sector-wide excitement.
  • A book-value approach depends on how well the balance sheet reflects reality — whether a dam built fifteen years ago at old cost is really worth what the accounts say today.
WARNING A common mistake is to run one method, get one number, and treat it as "the valuation" — as if it were a fact, not an estimate resting on a chain of assumptions. A far better habit is to run at least two independent methods and examine why they differ. If a DCF says a share is worth NPR 450 and a peer comparison says NPR 650, the gap itself is information. Either the market is paying an unusually rich multiple for the peer group (perhaps from sentiment, as later lessons discuss), or your DCF assumptions are too cautious, or the company truly deserves a different price because it is less comparable than it looks. Investigating that gap is often worth more than either number alone.

That is why Part IX is built this way. This chapter lays the foundation. The chapters that follow each take one method in depth — DCF, relative valuation, the dividend discount model, and book value/NAV — because no single tool is enough. A carpenter does not arrive at a job with only a hammer. A serious investor does not arrive at a valuation question with only one method.

Lesson 45.3 — Margin of Safety: Valuation's Purpose Is Not Precision, It Is Protection

If valuation is imprecise, why bother?

Once you accept that any single valuation is uncertain — your DCF may be too hopeful, your "comparable" companies may be mispriced, your book-value adjustments may be wrong — a fair question follows: why bother at all?

The answer, developed most fully by Benjamin Graham and carried forward ever since, is the margin of safety. It means buying a share at a meaningful discount to your estimate of its intrinsic value. Then even if your estimate is somewhat wrong — the business does worse than expected, or your assumptions were too rosy — you are still likely to avoid a serious permanent loss, and still have room to profit. It is not a formula. It is a habit of humility built into the buying decision.

The bridge engineer

An engineer told that a bridge must carry trucks up to 10 tonnes does not design it to hold exactly 10 tonnes. She designs it for 30 tonnes or more. Her models of stress, metal fatigue and load are imperfect, and the cost of being wrong (a collapsed bridge) is far worse than the cost of over-building (some extra steel).

Margin of safety works the same way. You demand a price well below your estimated value, not because you doubt your arithmetic, but because every valuation model simplifies a messier reality. You want room for reality to disappoint you without wrecking your capital.

KEY CONCEPT Margin of safety is the gap between your estimate of intrinsic value and the price you actually pay. It absorbs three kinds of error at once: errors in your assumptions, errors in your model, and plain bad luck in how the future turns out. The wider the margin, the more of these errors you can survive and still come out ahead.

Why it matters more in Nepal

Several features of NEPSE make both price swings and model error more likely than in a deep, institution-dominated market.

First, retail investors dominate. Individuals trading their own savings, rather than professional fund managers, make up the great majority of NEPSE trading and ownership — a pattern documented repeatedly in research on Nepal's market and discussed in earlier chapters on market structure. Many trade part-time alongside other jobs. Such a market tends to be driven by mood: prices can swing on a rumour, on one piece of news spread across social media and Viber/Telegram investment groups, or on simple herd behaviour, rather than on a fresh look at a company's earning power.

CASE IN POINT NEPSE has repeatedly seen whole sectors — hydropower in one period, microfinance in another, life insurance in another — rally sharply within weeks on a wave of retail buying, often with no matching change in the companies' earnings, licences or cash prospects, before giving back much of the gain over the following months. Analysts and commentators on Nepal's market have flagged this pattern of retail-driven momentum and later correction as a recurring feature, not a one-off. It is exactly the price-value gap this chapter is about, and why "hot money" rotating between sectors deserves the same doubt whether it appears in hydropower, banking or anything else.

Second, many shares are illiquid. Many NEPSE counters — especially smaller hydropower companies, smaller finance companies and thinly traded manufacturers — see only a few shares change hands on many days. So one moderately sized order can move the price several percent without any real change in the business.

In a large, liquid foreign market, heavy two-way trading by big, well-resourced institutions tends to pull prices back towards value fairly quickly, because mispricing attracts arbitrage money (traders who profit by closing price gaps). NEPSE has far less of this self-correction. A share can trade well above or below a careful estimate of its value for a long time, because there may not be enough patient, analysis-driven money to close the gap.

WARNING Illiquidity cuts both ways for the disciplined investor. A truly undervalued NEPSE share can stay undervalued for a long time, testing your patience. And when mood turns euphoric, prices can drift further from fundamentals, and for longer, than in a more liquid market, because there is no large pool of institutional short-sellers or arbitrageurs to push back. In this setting, margin of safety is not optional. You need a big enough discount to survive the market ignoring you for months or years without your thesis being wrong — and enough discipline not to chase a price that has run far ahead of any defensible value.

A defence against our own minds

The behavioural biases from earlier chapters — anchoring on a share's old high price, following the herd into hot sectors, overconfidence after lucky gains, and loss aversion that keeps people holding falling shares too long — are not separate from valuation discipline. They are the reason it is needed.

A valuation framework is, in a real sense, a structured defence against your own psychology and the crowd's. When everyone around you is buying a hydropower share because "it only goes up", a calm DCF of the company's real cash-earning capacity over its PPA is one of the few tools that can interrupt the momentum with a numbers-based question: at this price, am I really being paid for the risk I am taking — or just paying for other people's excitement?

Lesson 45.4 — Four Lenses, One House: An Overview of the Methods to Come

We have seen why price and value differ, why methods honestly disagree, and why margin of safety matters especially on NEPSE. This lesson previews the four methods that the rest of Part IX covers in full. Think of it as being handed a map before a long trek — enough to see how the pieces connect before you go deep into any one.

1. Discounted cash flow (DCF)

This estimates the cash a business should produce for its owners in future years, then discounts it back to today using a rate that reflects how risky that cash is. The further away and riskier the cash, the more it is discounted.

It is the most complete method in theory, because it captures everything that matters — growth, profitability, capital needs and risk — in one framework tied to real cash. Its weakness is sensitivity: small changes in the assumed growth or discount rate can swing the value a lot. A DCF is only as good, and as humble, as its inputs.

2. Relative valuation (comps)

This skips forecasting the distant future. It asks what the market now pays for similar businesses, as a multiple of some figure — price-to-earnings (P/E), price-to-book (P/B), EV/EBITDA and so on — and applies a comparable multiple to your company.

Its strength is that it rests on real, visible market prices rather than a long chain of guesses. Its weakness is that it inherits any mispricing already in the market. If a whole NEPSE sector trades at an inflated multiple because of retail excitement, a comps valuation of one company in it simply repeats that inflated value, dressed up as "the market says".

3. Dividend discount model (DDM)

A specialised cousin of DCF, the DDM values a share on the dividends a shareholder actually expects to receive, discounted to today.

It suits businesses where rules limit how much cash can leave the company and reach shareholders — commercial banks being the main NEPSE example, since Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB) capital rules restrict payouts. For a bank, dividends (cash and, in Nepal, often bonus shares) are a more reliable guide to shareholder value than "free cash flow", which is hard to define for a business whose raw material is deposits and whose product is loans.

4. Book value / net asset value (NAV)

This values a company on what its assets are worth (adjusted, where possible, to realistic current values rather than old accounting cost) minus its liabilities.

It is the natural lens for asset-heavy or asset-backed businesses: banks and financial institutions (whose regulatory capital is measured against book equity), insurance companies, and to some degree hydropower companies with physical, licensed infrastructure. It is also the natural floor check for any company in distress or liquidation, where the question becomes not "what can this business earn?" but "what would its assets fetch if sold off piece by piece?"

PRACTICAL TOOL A simple memory aid for what each method really asks. DCF: "what will this business earn, and what is that worth today?" Comps: "what is the market paying for similar businesses right now?" DDM: "what cash will actually land in my hand as a shareholder?" NAV: "what would be left if we sold everything and paid off every debt?" Four different questions about the same company — which is exactly why they can give four different numbers, and why comparing the answers tells you more than picking one and ignoring the rest.

None of these methods is best in the abstract. Each fits better or worse depending on two things, covered next: the type of company, and the purpose of the valuation.

Lesson 45.5 — Matching the Method to the Company

A common error is to use a favourite method on every company regardless of fit — running a DCF on a bank the same way as on a factory, or valuing a hydropower company on P/E as if it were a trading company. The method must fit the business. A valuer would not value a working farm in Chitwan the same way as an apartment in Kathmandu; the income, the assets and the risks are too different.

Take three typical kinds of NEPSE company.

A commercial bank

A bank's balance sheet is its business. Deposits are its raw material; loans and investments are its product. Its capital adequacy ratio — how much capital it holds against its risk-weighted assets, set and enforced by NRB — directly limits how fast it can grow and how much it can pay out.

A from-scratch free-cash-flow DCF for a bank is notoriously hard. "Capital spending" and "working capital" do not mean for a lender what they mean for a factory; taking deposits and making loans blurs the usual line between operating and investing cash.

So banks worldwide are mainly valued with:

  • price-to-book — market price against the bank's book equity, adjusted for loan quality;
  • the dividend discount model — because NRB capital buffers directly limit what a bank can pay, and Nepali investors have long prized banks' bonus shares and cash dividends;
  • comparison with other listed banks — unusually reliable here, because Nepal has more than two dozen listed banks and finance companies with truly similar business models, rules and disclosure formats.

A hydropower company

Hydropower is nearly the opposite. It is a long-lived, asset-heavy business with a contract-defined life. Most Nepali hydropower companies sell power to the Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA) under a PPA with a fixed tariff schedule and term, after which terms may change or the licence may need renewing.

A known (if long) horizon, contract-bound revenue, and heavy up-front spending already sunk into dams, tunnels and powerhouses make hydropower a textbook DCF case — like valuing a toll road or a pension annuity. You:

  1. forecast electricity generated (from river flow and plant capacity);
  2. apply the PPA tariffs (with escalation and the wet/dry season rates common in Nepali PPAs);
  3. subtract operating costs and debt service;
  4. discount the result at a rate reflecting river risk, the NEA's own payment reliability, and Nepal's country risk.

Book value is a much weaker lens here. A dam's old construction cost has little to do with its earning power, which depends on rainfall and tariff terms, not on what steel and cement cost when it was built.

A manufacturing or consumer company

A company making noodles, cement, steel or drinks for the Nepali market is closest to the classic case most textbooks are written for. It buys raw materials, turns them into products, sells them and reinvests profit in more capacity. Its capital spending and working capital behave the textbook way.

So it suits a standard free-cash-flow DCF. And because Nepal has several listed manufacturers and consumer companies (some direct peers, some rough), it also suits relative valuation using P/E or EV/EBITDA against local peers — checked against regional peers when local ones are too few.

REGULATORY DETAIL Nepal Rastra Bank sets minimum capital adequacy ratios and related prudential rules for banks and financial institutions (BFIs). These directly limit how much of a bank's profit can be paid out as dividends versus kept as core capital in a given year. So a bank's dividends cannot simply be modelled as "profit after tax", as one might for a manufacturer. They must be modelled against what the bank can actually distribute while staying within the rules — which is why the dividend discount model, applied carefully with NRB's rules in mind, tends to work better than a naive DCF for this sector.

Lesson 45.6 — Matching the Method to the Purpose

The type of company is only half the story. The other half, often forgotten, is the purpose of the valuation: what decision is this number meant to support? Valuing a company to buy a few shares on the NEPSE screen is a truly different exercise from valuing it to buy the whole thing. Mixing the two up leads to real mistakes.

Buying a small slice

When you buy shares of, say, Himalayan Bank or Chilime through your broker, you buy a minority stake: a small slice of a business you will not control. You cannot appoint its board or set its dividend policy, and you vote on big decisions alongside thousands of other shareholders.

For this purpose, the question is: given what a passive minority owner can expect to receive (dividends, bonus shares and eventually a sale at some future price), and given today's market price, is this an attractive deal for the risk? Comparison with listed peers, and dividend-based methods, are usually very relevant. A minority shareholder's return depends mainly on what the company chooses to pay out and what the market will pay for similar minority stakes — not on any control premium.

Buying the whole business

Valuing a company for a full takeover, a merger or a controlling investment is different. This is more relevant to Nepal's bank merger waves (NRB has repeatedly pushed BFIs to merge to strengthen capital) or to family-owned factories and hydropower companies considering a sale.

Here the buyer is not stuck with management's dividend policy. The buyer can change it, replace management, change the borrowing, sell unproductive assets, or redirect all the business's cash. So takeover valuations lean more on:

  • a full enterprise DCF — valuing all the cash the business could make under new, presumably better, control; and
  • NAV and asset-based approaches — because a controlling buyer can actually realise asset value directly (sell a building, use a licence) in a way a minority shareholder cannot.

Takeover prices also usually include a control premium: an amount paid above the minority-stake market price, because control over cash and assets is worth more than a passive claim on whatever the current board decides to pay out. It is like the difference between renting one room in a house and owning the whole house: the owner can rebuild, re-let or sell; the tenant cannot.

KEY CONCEPT A minority stake and a controlling stake in the same company are not, strictly, the same asset for valuation. The minority investor's claim is limited to what the current management and board choose to do. The controlling investor's claim extends to everything the business could be made to do under new owners. So the "right" value depends on which of the two you are actually buying, not only on which company it is.

What this means for most readers

This matters a great deal, because most readers of this book are building a NEPSE portfolio of minority stakes, not buying companies outright. For most everyday decisions in this Canon, the DDM and relative valuation will often carry more practical weight than a textbook enterprise DCF built as if you were about to buy the whole company.

A DCF is still valuable for minority investing, because it forces clear thinking about growth, risk and long-run earning power that a simple multiple can hide. The purpose does not rule out any method. It changes which one deserves most weight, and which extra adjustments — a control premium, or a discount for a hard-to-sell minority holding — must be added on top.

PRACTICAL TOOL Before opening a spreadsheet, a disciplined analyst answers two questions out loud. (1) What kind of business is this — balance-sheet-driven like a bank, contract-and-asset-driven like a hydropower plant, or operating-cycle-driven like a manufacturer? (2) What decision does this valuation support — buying a few shares on the NEPSE screen, or acquiring real control? Only after both are answered does it make sense to choose a main method and a set of supporting cross-checks.
CASE IN POINT Nepal's banking sector has been through several NRB-encouraged merger waves, as the central bank raised minimum capital requirements and pushed smaller banks and finance companies to combine. In these merger talks, the share-swap ratio between merging banks has usually been anchored mainly to book value and DDM-style thinking about each bank's earning power and capital — a very different conversation from the day-to-day P/E chatter about the same bank's shares among retail traders. Both are "valuing the same bank", yet they stress different methods because they serve different purposes.

Chapter recap

  • Price is a fact you can see; value is an estimate you must work out. Your job is not to predict tomorrow's price but to estimate intrinsic value and compare it with the price on offer.
  • Methods disagree honestly, like three house valuers: comparable sales (comps), replacement cost (book value/NAV) and income (DCF/DDM). Run at least two and study the gap.
  • Margin of safety — buying well below your value estimate — protects against wrong assumptions, wrong models and bad luck. It matters more on NEPSE, where retail mood and illiquidity let prices stray far from value for long periods.
  • The four lenses: DCF (what will it earn?), comps (what does the market pay for similar?), DDM (what cash reaches me?), NAV (what is left after selling everything?).
  • Match the method to the company: banks → price-to-book and DDM; hydropower → DCF; manufacturers → DCF and P/E or EV/EBITDA; distressed companies → NAV.
  • Match the method to the purpose: a minority stake leans on DDM and comps; a takeover leans on full DCF and NAV and includes a control premium.
