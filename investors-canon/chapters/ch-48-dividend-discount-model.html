WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Dividend discount model (DDM) — valuing a share as the present value of its future dividends, like valuing a rented house by its future rent. Gordon growth model — the one-stage version: next year's dividend divided by (required return minus growth). D1 — next year's expected dividend per share. Payout ratio — the share of profit paid out as dividends. Multi-stage DDM — a model with a period of higher growth before a steady long-term rate. Tax-holiday trap — mistaking profits boosted by a temporary tax break for permanent profits.

Imagine you own a small rental house in Baneshwor. You do not plan to sell it next year. What it is "worth" to you, in a very real sense, is the rent it brings in year after year for as long as you hold it — discounted to today, because a rupee promised in five years is worth less than a rupee in your hand now.

If the tenant pays reliably and the rent rises a little each year, the house is worth more. If the tenant is unreliable, or the area is going downhill, it is worth less — even if the paint is fresh and the plot is large.

A share in a NEPSE company is not so different. You do not own the factory, the bank branch or the powerhouse. You own a claim on the cash the company chooses to pay you over time — the dividend. The Dividend Discount Model (DDM) values a share just as you would value that rental house: as the present value of the cash it should pay you, forever, discounted at a rate that reflects how risky that cash is.

This chapter builds the DDM from its simplest form to a more realistic multi-stage version. Because this is a book about Nepal, it also spends real time on why this model, more than almost any other in Part IX, fits NEPSE so well — and where it can mislead an investor who uses it carelessly on the wrong company at the wrong stage of its life.

Lesson 48.1 — The Rental House Logic: Gordon Growth Model Mechanics

The simplest dividend stream

Start with the simplest case: a company pays a dividend, and that dividend is expected to grow at a steady rate forever. This is the Gordon Growth Model, named after the economist Myron Gordon, who set it out in the 1950s and 1960s. It is the workhorse of DDM. Almost every more complex DDM is just several Gordon Growth calculations joined together.

Value per share = D1 / (r − g)

  • D1 — the dividend expected next year (not the one just paid);
  • r — the cost of equity: the return shareholders need for holding this share, given its risk (Lesson 46.3);
  • g — the steady expected growth rate of the dividend, forever.
KEY CONCEPT The Gordon Growth Model says a share is worth next year's expected dividend divided by the gap between your required return and the dividend's growth rate. The smaller that gap, the more the share is worth — which also means value is extremely sensitive to small changes in either r or g.

Back to the rental house

D1 is next year's rent. r is the return you need for tying your money up in this house instead of a government bond or fixed deposit — it includes the risk that the tenant stops paying, the building needs repairs, or the area declines. g is how fast you expect rent to grow each year, forever — perhaps 4–5% as the area develops.

If you expect Rs 100,000 rent next year, need 12% for the risk of being a landlord there, and expect rent to grow 4% a year forever:

Rs 100,000 / (0.12 − 0.04) = Rs 100,000 / 0.08 = Rs 1,250,000

r must be bigger than g

The model needs r to be strictly greater than g. If growth equals or beats your required return, the formula breaks: it gives an infinite or negative value. That is maths telling you no company can grow its dividend faster than the required return forever.

A company can grow explosively for five or ten years, but not forever. Competition, a saturated market or sheer size eventually pull growth back towards the growth of the whole economy. You will lean on this again in Lesson 48.5.

A NEPSE bank example

Take Himalayan Ridge Bank Ltd., an imaginary commercial bank used throughout this chapter:

  • expected cash dividend next year: Rs 15 a share;
  • required return for banks of this risk: 14% (built, as in Lesson 46.3, from a risk-free rate plus an equity risk premium adjusted for the bank's beta and Nepal's risks);
  • expected dividend growth: 6% a year forever, roughly in line with nominal GDP and steady growth in bank lending.

Value per share = 15 / (0.14 − 0.06) = 15 / 0.08 = Rs 187.50

If the bank trades at Rs 150 on NEPSE, the model says it is undervalued against its dividend capacity, on these growth and discount assumptions. If it trades at Rs 260, the market is pricing in faster growth, a lower required return, or both — a signal to recheck your inputs, not proof that the market is wrong.

PRACTICAL TOOL To build D1, do not just take last year's dividend per share. Start from expected earnings per share for the next twelve months, apply the payout ratio you expect the company to sustain (based on its capital needs, what the regulator allows and its stated dividend policy), and work out D1 from that. This ties your dividend forecast to the business, not to an extended trend line.

The two inputs that make or break the model — r and g — are exactly where an analyst's judgment, not a formula, does the real work. Lesson 46.3 covered how to build r. Most of this chapter is about g: how to estimate it responsibly for a Nepali company, and why Nepal's dividend culture makes this both easier and, in specific ways, trickier than in markets where dividends matter less.

Lesson 48.2 — Why DDM Is Nepal's Natural Valuation Tool

Elsewhere, dividends are small

In many developed markets, dividends are almost a rounding error in total shareholder return. American technology companies often pay no dividend for decades, returning cash through share buybacks (the company buying back its own shares) instead. A simple DDM of Amazon or a similar growth company would give a value of roughly zero — obviously wrong, because it ignores almost every way that company creates value for its owners.

In Nepal, dividends are the main event

NEPSE is completely different. Nepali listed companies — above all commercial banks, development banks, finance companies, microfinance institutions, insurers and hydropower companies — have a deep habit of paying out a large share of yearly profit as dividends: in cash, in bonus shares, or both. Three features of the market explain why:

  1. Buybacks are rare. For most sectors they are effectively unavailable or heavily restricted. A Nepali company wanting to reward shareholders has essentially one channel at scale: declare a dividend.
  2. Banks pay out what rules allow. Regulated financial institutions must put part of profit into capital and reserves (Lesson 48.4). The rest is usually paid out rather than hoarded, partly because minority shareholders — a large part of most NEPSE banks' registers — expect and demand it.
  3. Running hydropower plants need little new spending. Once a plant is operating and earning steady cash under a long-term Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) with the Nepal Electricity Authority, it has little further capital spending for that plant. So free cash turns into dividends, at least until the company starts a new project.
KEY CONCEPT The dividend payout ratio is the share of a company's net profit paid to shareholders as dividends in a year, as opposed to the share kept (retained earnings) to reinvest or hold in reserves. NEPSE banks and mature hydropower companies often run payout ratios that would be seen as unusually generous in many developed markets.

So DDM is often the first tool to reach for

Dividends are the main way Nepali companies return value, and they are what most NEPSE investors actually watch, budget for and reinvest. So the DDM is not an academic curiosity here — it is close to the most natural lens there is.

A Silicon Valley analyst might reach first for a free-cash-flow DCF (Chapter 46). A NEPSE analyst covering a bank or a seasoned hydropower share can often go straight to a well-built DDM and get a defensible answer, because the dividend is not a fragment of the return story — it is most of the story.

CASE IN POINT Take two imaginary companies. Sagarmatha Commercial Bank has paid a mix of cash and bonus dividends in nine of the last ten years. Rolling Hills Trading Pvt., a growth business, has reinvested essentially all its profit in expansion and has no dividend record. DDM gives a sensible, well-anchored value for the first. Applied literally to the second, it gives nothing useful — there is no dividend stream to discount. This is the situation with early-stage and high-growth firms discussed further in Lesson 48.6.

DDM is not the only tool you need. The DCF and relative valuation of Chapters 46 and 47 remain essential — especially for companies without a long dividend record, or where you suspect the dividend policy itself does not reliably show underlying value creation. But for the core of NEPSE — banks, insurers and seasoned hydropower names — DDM deserves to be the first model you reach for, not the last.

Lesson 48.3 — Cash Dividends, Bonus Shares, and the Dilution Trap

Here Nepal's dividend culture becomes tricky. A careless analyst can produce a badly wrong value even while applying the DDM formula correctly.

Two kinds of dividend at once

Nepali companies routinely declare dividends in two forms together:

  • a cash dividend — real money per share, with 5% dividend tax withheld at source for individuals (see the tax chapters);
  • a bonus share dividend — extra shares given free to existing shareholders in a fixed proportion to what they hold.

A company might declare a "20% dividend" made up of 15% bonus shares and 5% cash. A shareholder with 100 shares gets 15 new shares plus Rs 500 in cash (5% of the Rs 100 face value, before tax).

KEY CONCEPT A bonus share (also called a stock dividend) is a free extra share issued to existing shareholders in proportion to their holding, paid for by turning the company's reserves into share capital rather than by paying out cash. It raises the number of shares but does not, by itself, change the company's total value or hand any cash to shareholders.

Bonus shares do not create wealth

This is the most important point in this lesson: a bonus share does not create wealth. It is not a real cash return in the way a cash dividend is.

When a company issues bonus shares, it moves an amount from reserves (retained earnings) to paid-up capital on the balance sheet and issues new shares to match. Its total equity value is unchanged. The same value is just divided among more shares, so the share price falls on the ex-bonus date roughly in proportion to the bonus.

A shareholder with shares worth Rs 100,000 before a 15% bonus still has about Rs 100,000 just after: 100 shares become 115, and the price per share falls by close to the same ratio. It is like cutting the same pizza into more slices — you have more pieces, but not more pizza.

WARNING Never put the announced "dividend percentage" straight into a DDM's D1 without separating cash from bonus. A 20% total dividend that is 15% bonus and 5% cash is not a Rs 20-per-Rs-100-face-value cash dividend. Treating it as one wildly overstates the real cash return — and the resulting value.

Two ways to handle bonus shares

Approach 1 (recommended): value only the cash dividends. Treat the bonus issue as a non-event for valuation — a cosmetic rise in share count, not part of "the dividend" in the economic sense DDM captures. D1 is strictly next year's expected cash dividend per share, and g is the expected growth of that cash dividend per share on an already adjusted, post-bonus share count. This is cleaner and more rigorous, because it never confuses a reserves-to-capital book entry with a real cash return. It is this book's default.

Approach 2 (rough shorthand): read bonus shares as a sign of growth. The idea is that a company keeps earnings (instead of paying cash) to reinvest in growth, and bonus shares are one visible marker of that. A large bonus beside a small cash dividend signals more profit being kept for growth — which, if reinvested well, should mean a higher future g for cash dividends.

The intuition is not wrong, but it is dangerous to apply mechanically. A bonus share linked to truly value-adding reinvestment (funding a bank's loan growth without a rights issue, for example) is very different from one issued just to make shareholders feel good about a "big dividend number", with no matching rise in earning power per share.

PRACTICAL TOOL When a company regularly issues large bonus dividends, always restate EPS and dividends per share (DPS) on a bonus-adjusted basis before building a multi-year dividend history — the same way you would adjust old prices for a stock split. Comparing an unadjusted DPS from five years ago (before three bonus issues) with today's DPS makes growth look artificially low, understating the cash shareholders really received per original share.

The tax side

This links to the cost-basis material in the tax chapters (Chapters 36 and 37). Bonus shares change the denominator — the number of shares, and so the cost per share for capital gains tax — without changing the numerator, the total value or total cash invested.

A shareholder does pay tax on bonus shares. Nepal treats a bonus issue as a dividend and taxes it at 5 percent of its face value when allotted. That is why companies usually declare a small cash portion with the bonus, to cover that tax (Lesson 36.3). After that, the bonus shares join the shareholder's cost pool at face value, so the cost is spread over more shares — which matters when they are sold and gains are calculated.

In a real sense, a bonus share is a reshuffling device: it turns kept profit into more shares, taxed lightly now and again on any gain at sale, rather than creating new value. An analyst who treats a bonus-heavy "dividend yield" as equal to a cash-heavy one from another company is comparing unlike things. One is real income you can spend; the other is more shares in the same company, already taxed once and worth only what the diluted price says.

Lesson 48.4 — NRB's Regulatory Leash on Bank Dividends

DDM suits NEPSE banks because they pay out so much. But Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB), the central bank and banking regulator, does not let banks pay dividends however they like. Its dividend rules directly limit a bank's dividend capacity — and so limit the D1 and g a sensible analyst should ever project, however strong the bank's profit looks on paper.

Why the regulator holds the leash

NRB's logic is that a bank's capital is not simply its owners' money to hand out. It is also the cushion protecting depositors and the financial system from losses.

So NRB links the most a bank may pay out to:

  • its capital adequacy — how much regulatory capital it holds against its risk-weighted assets; and
  • the quality of its loan book, usually measured by its non-performing loan (NPL) ratio.

A bank sitting at or just above the minimum capital ratio, or with rising NPLs, may pay out less of its profit — sometimes no cash at all — because the regulator wants that capital kept inside as a buffer. It is like a family whose savings are below the emergency level: before spending on a festival, they must first refill the emergency fund.

REGULATORY DETAIL NRB's dividend directives require banks and financial institutions to keep capital adequacy comfortably above the minimum, and non-performing loans within acceptable limits, before they may pay dividends at all. The maximum dividend is scaled to how far above those minimums the institution sits — a bank near its floor faces sharply lower dividend capacity even in a year of strong headline profit. NRB has revised these thresholds from time to time (including guidance in 2025 tightening reporting and approval steps before dividend declarations), so check the directive currently in force rather than assuming last year's rule still applies.

What this means for a bank DDM

A naive analyst forecasts D1 as "expected EPS × last year's payout ratio" and stops. A careful analyst asks: given this bank's current and projected capital ratio and its NPL trend, what dividend will NRB actually allow next year, and for several years after?

A bank growing its loans fast uses up capital, because more risk-weighted assets need more capital at the same ratio. It may earn healthy profit yet be forced to plough most of it back into capital. Its true sustainable D1 is then lower than a simple extension of past payouts suggests, especially during aggressive branch or loan expansion. A mature bank with slower loan growth and plenty of spare capital may sustain a high payout for a long time — exactly where DDM earns its keep.

CASE IN POINT Suppose Himalayan Ridge Bank grew its loans 25% last year (strong headline growth), but this pushed its capital ratio to just above NRB's minimum. Under NRB's framework, its permitted dividend for the coming year would be sharply cut compared with its raw net profit, whatever shareholders want. An analyst modelling D1 purely from past payout ratios, ignoring the capital limit, would overstate next year's dividend — and overvalue the share.

So a bank DDM works best paired with a capital forecast: project risk-weighted asset growth and the capital added by kept earnings, then work back to a realistic maximum payout that keeps the bank safely above NRB's minimums — rather than simply extending the old payout percentage.

Regulation cuts both ways. The same system that gives Nepali banks a fairly stable, licensed, limited-competition setting (a lasting advantage discussed in earlier chapters) can, in any given year, cap how much of that earning power reaches shareholders as cash.

CAUTION Do not assume NRB's dividend rules apply the same way to hydropower companies, insurers or other non-bank companies. The capital-linked dividend cap in this lesson is specific to NRB-regulated banks and financial institutions. Hydropower companies face different limits — mainly loan covenants from their project lenders, which often block dividends until debt-service coverage tests are met — while insurers follow the Nepal Insurance Authority's own solvency-linked rules. Always identify which regulator, and which specific limit, applies to the company you are valuing.

Lesson 48.5 — When One Growth Rate Isn't Enough: The Multi-Stage DDM

The Gordon Growth Model assumes one steady growth rate forever. That is fine for a mature, stable business, such as an established bank growing roughly with the economy. It breaks down badly for a company in a distinct phase of unusually fast (or slow, or negative) growth that will not last.

The fix: split the future into stages

The multi-stage DDM models an early period with one or more higher (or lower) growth rates, then assumes the company settles into a steady long-run rate from some year onwards. You apply the Gordon formula to that steady stream and discount it to today alongside the early dividends. The two-stage version:

  1. Forecast dividends year by year for the fast-growth or transition period — say five years.
  2. At the end of that period, work out a terminal value with the Gordon formula, using the first "steady" year's dividend and the long-run growth rate.
  3. Discount each early dividend, plus the terminal value, back to today at the cost of equity, and add them up.
KEY CONCEPT A multi-stage DDM splits a company's future into a transition period (often faster growth), forecast year by year, followed by a terminal period of steady, sustainable growth valued with the simple Gordon formula. Because no company can grow dividends faster than the required return forever, every DDM eventually needs a steady, defensible long-run growth assumption at its core.

A worked example: Trishuli Ridge Hydropower

Take an imaginary hydropower company, Trishuli Ridge Hydropower Ltd., which recently started commercial operation and is still in the early years of its PPA tariff structure (more in Lesson 48.6). Assume:

  • dividend per share grows 18% a year for three years, as the tariff escalates and the company pays down its early debt;
  • then 10% in years four and five as growth slows;
  • then a steady 6% from year six, roughly matching the sector's long-run nominal growth;
  • cost of equity for a hydropower share of this risk: 13%;
  • current dividend per share (D0): Rs 8.00.

PRACTICAL TOOL Notice how much of the total — about 75% here — comes from the terminal value, not the forecast years. That is normal in a multi-stage DDM, but it makes the terminal growth rate the single most important number in the model. Get it wrong and the error swamps everything you did carefully in the early years. Always check terminal g against the long-run growth of the Nepali economy (nominal GDP growth). A terminal g permanently above nominal GDP for a mature company is a red flag: it implies the company eventually becomes bigger than the whole economy.

The example already hints at the danger in the next lesson. The whole calculation depended on correctly spotting when the 18% phase ends and the steady 6% phase begins. Get that wrong — assume 18% for eight years instead of three — and the value changes dramatically. You compound a high growth rate over a much longer period, and push a large terminal value to a later date, without capturing what actually stops growth being that fast.

WARNING A common modelling error is making the change between growth stages too sudden — jumping straight from 18% to 6% with nothing in between. Real businesses rarely slow that sharply. Where the real driver of growth (such as a PPA tariff escalation schedule, covered next) has its own multi-year phase-out, build your stages to follow that driver's actual timeline, not a generic three-stage template applied without looking at how the business really works.

Lesson 48.6 — The Tax-Holiday and Tariff-Escalation Trap

This lesson covers the most dangerous way DDM goes wrong on NEPSE: applying one extended growth rate to a company that is in a temporary, artificially boosted phase, and mistaking that phase for its normal state.

Two temporary boosts

Chapter 43 showed two features of Nepali hydropower:

  • Income tax holidays in the early operating years — a set number of years at 0% tax, then a period at a reduced rate, before the normal corporate rate.
  • Escalating PPA tariffs with the NEA — a rate that rises by a fixed percentage each year for a set number of years, then stays flat for the rest of the PPA.

Both are deliberate policy, meant to help project finances in the early, most fragile years, when debt payments are heaviest. Both are also, for valuation, temporary — and temporary is exactly the word a DDM analyst must take seriously.

WARNING The most dangerous DDM error on NEPSE is taking a hydropower (or any tax-holiday) company's current, boosted dividend growth — driven by an escalating PPA tariff and a temporary tax holiday, both with a known end date — and putting it into a single-stage Gordon model as if it would last forever. It will not. When the tariff escalation stops and the tax holiday ends, both dividend growth and the dividend itself can drop sharply, sometimes in the same year.

Three tailwinds blowing together

In a hydropower company's early operating years, three tailwinds often combine to make dividend growth look spectacular on past figures:

  1. the PPA's built-in escalation raises the average tariff per unit each year;
  2. the company pays little or no income tax under its holiday, so more revenue turns into profit;
  3. the plant, recently switched on, may still be ramping up to full expected output as early problems are fixed.

Together they can produce 15%, 20%, even 25%+ yearly growth in dividend per share for several years in a row — not because the business is compounding at that rate sustainably, but because three separate, time-limited tailwinds are blowing the same way at once.

Then they stop

In one year, or a short window, all three can end. The tariff escalation flattens at its top rate. The tax holiday expires and the tax rate steps up, cutting the margin on the same revenue. Full output, if not already reached, levels off.

So dividend growth does not gently slow to a steady rate. It can fall off a cliff, or the dividend can even fall in rupees in the transition year — while the physical asset (the powerhouse, the water rights, the PPA itself) is unchanged and arguably just as valuable a long-run cash earner as the year before.

It is like a new shop on a busy road that gets a three-year rent waiver from the landlord and a festival rush in its first seasons. Its early profit growth is real, but it is not the shop's normal pace.

CASE IN POINT Take Trishuli Ridge Hydropower from Lesson 48.5. Suppose an analyst, seeing only its last three years of 18% dividend growth, put that rate into a single-stage Gordon model forever: 9.44 / (0.13 − 0.18) gives a negative, meaningless value, because the growth rate exceeds the discount rate — the model breaking is itself the warning. Even a milder error — assuming 18% for ten years instead of three, because the analyst did not check the remaining tax-holiday or tariff-escalation years — would give a value far above the correctly staged Rs 174.48. The gap between the naive and the properly staged answer is not a rounding error; it can easily be a value 50% or more too high.

The discipline

Before building any DDM on a hydropower company (or any company with a temporary tax break or a time-limited, scheduled boost to cash), go straight to its PPA and its tax status:

  • Find exactly how many years remain in any tariff escalation schedule.
  • Find exactly how many years remain in the tax holiday or reduced-tax period, and the rate the company will move to afterwards.
  • Build your forecast stages around those actual, contract- or law-defined dates — not a generic five-year template.
  • Move to a steady terminal growth rate only once the tariff has flattened and the full tax rate applies.

If the tax holiday ends in year four but tariff escalation runs to year seven, your model needs at least two separate transition stages before a truly steady terminal phase, not one.

CAUTION This trap is not unique to hydropower. Any company with a special economic zone tax break, an export incentive, a temporary subsidy or any other time-limited government or contract benefit carries the same risk. An analyst who extends a growth rate produced by a temporary tailwind, without checking when it ends, will consistently overvalue the share. The fix is always the same: find the actual end date of the benefit, and build the model's transition points around that date, not around a rule-of-thumb forecast horizon.

Done with this care, DDM becomes one of the most reliable tools for valuing exactly the kind of company that dominates NEPSE — and done carelessly, one of the most dangerous for that same company. The formula never changes. What changes is whether the analyst has looked beneath the dividend history to understand what drives it, and for how much longer.

Chapter recap

  • The DDM values a share like a rental house: the present value of the dividends it will pay. The Gordon Growth Model: value = D1 ÷ (r − g), and r must exceed g. Example: Rs 15 ÷ (14% − 6%) = Rs 187.50.
  • DDM suits NEPSE because buybacks are rare and banks, insurers and mature hydropower companies pay out much of their profit. It is useless for companies with no dividend record.
  • Bonus shares do not create wealth — they cut the same value into more shares. Value only the cash dividend, restate past DPS for bonuses, and remember bonus shares are taxed at 5% of face value on allotment.
  • NRB rules link bank dividends to capital adequacy and NPLs; fast loan growth can cap payouts. Hydropower faces lender covenants and insurers face Nepal Insurance Authority solvency rules instead.
  • A multi-stage DDM handles changing growth. Trishuli Ridge (D0 Rs 8, 18% then 10% then 6%, at 13%) is worth about Rs 174.48, with about 75% from terminal value; keep terminal g at or below nominal GDP.
  • Beware the tax-holiday and tariff-escalation trap: early hydropower dividend growth is temporary. Set stage dates from the actual PPA and tax-holiday end dates.
