Nepal's stock market has almost no foreign money in it. Foreign institutional investors (big overseas funds) do not trade NEPSE shares in any real volume. Capital account rules — the rules on moving money in and out of the country — restrict foreign portfolio buying. And the people who do most of the trading are domestic, small and mostly financed by Nepali bank loans. So it is tempting to think NEPSE is sealed off from whatever moves Wall Street, the Gulf or Shanghai. That is wrong, and this chapter explains why. Global shocks do reach Nepal. They just travel through narrower, slower and more roundabout pipes than in Mumbai or Jakarta. Those pipes are: remittances earned by Nepali workers in the Gulf, Malaysia and South Korea; the cost of imported petrol and diesel, which Nepal must buy in dollars; the fixed link between the Nepali rupee and the Indian rupee; and Nepali banks' willingness to lend against shares. When a US Federal Reserve decision moves the dollar, or an oil shock moves crude prices, the shock does not reach NEPSE through a foreign broker selling Nepali shares. It arrives when Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB) tightens the taps on the same banks that lend to NEPSE's margin traders. Learn this chain — remittances to reserves, reserves to the rupee peg, the peg to NRB policy, NRB policy to bank liquidity, bank liquidity to NEPSE trading — and "global" events will stop catching you by surprise.

Lesson 6.1 — Why NEPSE Moves Without Foreign Money: The Indirect Transmission Model

Textbooks on markets like India, Vietnam or the Philippines start their chapter on global risk with foreign institutional investor (FII) flows. Foreign money comes in, buys local shares, and leaves just as fast when the mood sours, so the local index moves almost in real time with world sentiment. That mechanism barely exists in Nepal. Under the foreign investment framework and NRB's capital controls, foreign individuals and institutions cannot freely buy and sell NEPSE shares the way they can in Mumbai, Colombo or Dhaka. Non-resident Nepalis (NRNs) have narrow, specific routes to invest, with their own limits — a trickle, not a channel. So there is no "FII selling" figure to watch in NEPSE's daily data, because there is almost no FII money to sell.

Because of this, many Nepali investors treat world news as background noise — interesting, but irrelevant to their holdings. That instinct is only half right. NEPSE will not drop tomorrow just because the US S&P 500 fell three percent overnight, the way India's Sensex or Korea's KOSPI might. But the Fed, oil prices and global panics still matter a great deal to NEPSE. They simply arrive later, and through different routes. The right picture has four links. First, a global event changes how much Nepali migrants can send home, or what Nepal pays for oil, or the value of the currency the Nepali rupee is tied to. Second, that change shows up in Nepal's balance of payments (all money flowing in and out of the country) and in NRB's foreign exchange reserves. NRB watches and defends these reserves closely, because a fixed exchange rate has no automatic shock absorber. Third, when reserves come under pressure, NRB uses the only tools it has: raising the policy rate, raising the cash reserve ratio (the share of deposits banks must park with NRB), enforcing the credit-to-deposit (CD) ratio ceiling, and in extreme cases restricting imports or making consumer and margin loans harder to get. Fourth — the link a NEPSE investor must never forget — Nepal's banks are both the biggest sector on the exchange and almost the only source of margin loans that fuel retail trading. When bank money tightens for reasons that have nothing to do with company profits, NEPSE falls anyway. It falls hardest among margin-financed investors, who are forced to sell and turn a dip into a rout.

KEY CONCEPT NEPSE is macro-exposed but not portfolio-exposed. Global events reach the index through remittances, oil-driven reserve pressure, and the NPR-INR peg's effect on domestic monetary policy — not through foreign funds buying or selling Nepali shares. The transmission is slow and indirect, but it is not weak.

This difference changes what you watch and how soon you expect to see effects. A Mumbai investor reading the Fed's minutes expects a reaction the same day or week. A NEPSE investor reading the same minutes should think in quarters. How will this affect hiring in the Gulf over the next two or three quarters? How will it move the dollar–Indian rupee–Nepali rupee chain, and so Nepal Oil Corporation's import bill? How will NRB's reserve position six months from now limit the credit growth NEPSE trading depends on? The delay is real, but so is the eventual impact — and the delay is exactly what gives a careful investor time to get ahead of it.

Lesson 6.2 — The Remittance Channel: Gulf Labor Markets as Nepal's Real "Foreign Flow"

NEPSE has no real foreign fund flow, but it has something that plays the same role: remittances sent home by the several million Nepalis working abroad. Nepal is one of the most remittance-dependent economies on earth. Remittances regularly run at about a quarter of GDP or more — putting Nepal alongside Tajikistan, Kyrgyzstan and a few small Pacific and Caribbean states, and far above India or Bangladesh. This is not a side detail. Remittances are Nepal's largest source of foreign currency: bigger than exports, bigger than tourism, and bigger than foreign aid and foreign investment combined. They pay for household spending. They fill bank deposits. And — most important for this book — in boom years they supply much of the money that ends up in NEPSE as margin collateral, IPO applications and speculative trading.

The workers behind this money are concentrated in a few countries: Malaysia, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Kuwait and, through the Employment Permit System, South Korea. So Nepal's remittance income is not spread across the world. It is, in effect, a bet on Gulf construction and service jobs and on Malaysian factory and plantation jobs. Anything that changes hiring in those countries reaches remittance growth within a few months to a couple of quarters. Remittance growth then reaches Nepali bank deposits, spending and market liquidity after a further delay.

Working out which way these effects run takes care. It is tempting to think anything bad for the world economy is bad for Nepal's remittances, but it is not that simple. Sometimes it runs the other way. Higher oil prices hurt Nepal's import bill, as the next lesson shows. But they also fill Gulf governments' budgets, and rich Gulf governments tend to spend more on building — exactly the sector that employs the most Nepali workers. Qatar's construction boom ahead of the 2022 World Cup is the clearest example. For most of a decade, it pulled in Nepali labour on a huge scale and lifted remittance growth, before slowing once the stadiums, roads and metro lines were finished. An investor who sees Gulf oil wealth only as a cost (through Nepal Oil Corporation's import bill), and not also as a gain (through Gulf building and jobs), is seeing half the picture.

Other shocks are simply bad news. A recession or spending cuts in a Gulf country reduce construction and service hiring and can lead to layoffs. Remittance growth then slows, and in the worst cases more workers come home than leave. Malaysia has several times frozen new foreign recruitment for long periods — sometimes to protect local jobs, sometimes for health reasons. Each freeze shrinks the pipeline of new departures that would otherwise keep remittances growing over the next two or three years. COVID-19 was the starkest case: mass return of stranded workers, a sudden stop to new departures, and real fear that remittances would shrink for years. What actually happened was more complicated, and it shows something this chapter keeps returning to: effects inside Nepal's own financial system can outweigh the global shock itself.

WATCH FOR Year-on-year remittance growth, published monthly by Nepal Rastra Bank in its "Current Macroeconomic and Financial Situation" report. A deceleration or contraction in this figure — even before it shows up in forex reserves — is typically the earliest visible sign of stress that will eventually reach NEPSE through the bank liquidity channel described in Lesson 6.4.

So for a NEPSE investor, Gulf and Malaysian job markets are not distant "geopolitics". They are the nearest thing Nepal has to foreign fund flows, and they deserve the same attention an Indian investor gives FII data. Follow Gulf construction spending; Saudi and UAE policies that replace foreign workers with local citizens (the "Saudization" and "Emiratization" drives); Qatari and Kuwaiti project pipelines; and Malaysian and Korean recruitment quotas. Doing so is the same forward-looking work a Mumbai analyst does with FII data — just with a slower, thinner signal.

Lesson 6.3 — Oil, the Peg, and the Reserve Squeeze

If remittances are Nepal's main inflow, imported petroleum is its most volatile outflow — and the hardest to replace. Nepal produces and refines no crude oil. Every litre of petrol, diesel, kerosene, aviation fuel and LPG is imported by Nepal Oil Corporation (NOC), which by law has a monopoly and buys only from Indian Oil Corporation under a long-standing agreement. This has two big consequences. First, Nepal cannot escape oil price shocks in the short run. When crude prices spike, NOC's import bill rises almost one-for-one. After a short lag, the cost is either passed on to drivers at the pump or absorbed by NOC — and so by the state — as losses and unpaid bills. Second, and less obvious, the bill is not just a dollar oil price. It passes through the Indian rupee, because Nepal pays Indian Oil Corporation through a currency chain linked to India's own import costs and exchange rate.

Here is the most important fact in this chapter. The Nepali rupee is pegged — fixed — to the Indian rupee at a set rate (Rs 1.60 per Indian rupee), and has been for decades. NRB maintains this as long-standing policy. It is not a loose guide. It is a hard peg that NRB actively defends with its own foreign exchange reserves, much of which it holds in Indian rupees and other convertible currencies for exactly this purpose.

REGULATORY DETAIL Nepal Rastra Bank maintains a fixed exchange rate peg between the Nepali rupee and the Indian rupee. Because the peg is fixed, Nepal effectively imports India's monetary and exchange-rate conditions rather than setting fully independent domestic monetary policy — and India's own currency moves in response to the US dollar and Federal Reserve policy. This is the direct structural link between Fed decisions in Washington and NRB decisions in Kathmandu.

Follow the chain step by step, because it is the heart of this chapter and many market players get it wrong. When the US Federal Reserve raises interest rates, or signals more rises than expected, the dollar usually strengthens against most currencies, including the Indian rupee. As the Indian rupee weakens, India's imports — oil above all — cost more, and the Reserve Bank of India often tightens its own policy. Because the Nepali rupee is tied to the Indian rupee, not to the dollar, Nepal inherits this pressure automatically. The Nepali rupee falls against the dollar in step with the Indian rupee. So Nepal's dollar-priced oil bill, and any other dollar import or debt, gets more expensive in rupees just when the Fed is raising rates — even if Nepal's own economy needs something quite different. Think of two boats tied together: when the bigger one is pushed by the current, the smaller one goes with it. In effect, Nepal imports India's monetary stance, which itself reacts more and more to the Fed. An investor who calls Fed meetings irrelevant to a market with no foreign flows is missing the point. Fed decisions reach Kathmandu within weeks — not through NEPSE share registers, but through the exchange rate that decides how many rupees NOC pays for a barrel of crude.

When an oil price spike and a Fed-driven currency move happen together, Nepal's foreign exchange reserves are squeezed from two sides at once. More rupees are needed to buy the same oil, because oil costs more in dollars. And more rupees are needed to buy each dollar, because the rupee has weakened. NRB publishes one figure that sums up this pressure: foreign exchange reserves measured as "months of import cover" — how many months of Nepal's imports of goods and services today's reserves could pay for if no new foreign currency came in. In this book's view, it is the single most important economic number for a NEPSE investor to check regularly — more important for the market than GDP growth — because it tells you how much room NRB has before it is forced to tighten.

WATCH FOR NRB's disclosed foreign exchange reserves, expressed as months of import cover, published in its periodic "Current Macroeconomic and Financial Situation" reports. A sustained decline toward roughly six months of cover has historically preceded periods of import restriction, monetary tightening, and NEPSE weakness. A comfortable, rising import cover is one of the more reliable green lights for risk-taking in NEPSE.

Nepal felt exactly this squeeze after Russia invaded Ukraine in early 2022. Crude prices spiked while the dollar strengthened against most currencies, as the Fed raised rates aggressively. The two forces added up: NOC's import bill jumped in rupee terms from both the oil price and the weaker currency, just as Nepal was also importing a post-pandemic surge of vehicles, electronics and other goods. Reserves fell sharply through the first half of 2022. The government and NRB took the unusual step of banning imports of a list of "luxury" goods — vehicles, large motorbikes, liquor, expensive mobile phones, gold beyond personal limits and similar items — purely to save foreign currency. That is about as direct as evidence gets that a global commodity and money shock had reached Nepal's real economy. Its effect on NEPSE came next, and is the subject of the next lesson.

Lesson 6.4 — From Macro Stress to Market Stress: The Liquidity and Margin-Lending Transmission Belt

The last two lessons showed that world events reach Nepal through remittances and oil-driven pressure on reserves, and that both end up as stress on NRB's foreign exchange position. This lesson closes the loop: it explains how stress on reserves becomes stress on NEPSE. Most retail investors skip this step — which is why global events seem to them to come "out of nowhere".

Nepal's banks and financial institutions (BFIs) — commercial banks, development banks and finance companies — matter to NEPSE for two separate reasons. First, their own shares are one of the biggest sectors in the index by value and trading, so anything that hurts bank profits or balance sheets hits a large part of the index automatically. Second, and more important here, BFIs are almost the only lenders of margin loans — loans against pledged shares — which finance much of NEPSE's retail trading in busy markets. Margin lending is NEPSE's leverage engine. It lets investors hold bigger positions than their own money allows, and it makes both rallies and falls much bigger than company profits alone would justify.

When NRB responds to pressure on reserves — whether from an oil shock, a Fed-driven currency move, a remittance slowdown or all three — almost all its tools work by tightening bank liquidity and slowing credit. It can raise its policy rate, making central-bank money dearer and pushing banks to raise their own deposit and loan rates. It can raise the cash reserve ratio (CRR), the share of deposits banks must hold back, leaving less to lend. It can enforce or tighten the CD ratio ceiling — 90 percent since it replaced the older credit-to-core-capital-cum-deposit (CCD) ratio in 2021–22 — forcing banks near the limit to slow new lending or call in existing loans. And, in steps that hit NEPSE most directly, it can cut the maximum loan-to-value ratio on margin loans, or otherwise restrict lending against listed shares.

Each of these steps is taken to defend the rupee peg and the reserves. But each has a side effect: it drains the very money NEPSE's retail investors need to keep trading and to avoid forced sales. A margin trader does not feel the Fed's decision or the oil spike directly. They feel their bank suddenly demanding more security, raising the interest rate, or refusing to renew the loan. Their response — selling shares to meet the bank's demand — is forced and mechanical. It has nothing to do with any company's profits, and everything to do with a chain that began months earlier on a Gulf building site, at an OPEC+ meeting or in a Fed statement.

CASE IN POINT Through 2022, as Nepal's forex reserves came under pressure from the combined weight of the Russia-Ukraine oil shock and a strengthening dollar, Nepal Rastra Bank raised its policy rate over successive revisions from 3 percent to 7 percent (with the bank rate reaching 8.5 percent), while the 90 percent credit-to-deposit ceiling turned binding across much of the system, and margin lending was squeezed by the FY2021/22 Rs 4 crore/Rs 12 crore caps and the general liquidity drought. NEPSE, which had touched an all-time high near 3,200 points in mid-2021 on the back of a domestic liquidity glut, fell sharply through the rest of 2021 and into 2022 (touching roughly 1,850 points by June 2022) before grinding to its ultimate trough near 1,815 points on 25 September 2022, a decline of roughly 43 percent from the 3,198.60 close of August 2021 — a decline driven overwhelmingly by BFI-sector liquidity tightening and margin-call selling rather than by any deterioration in listed companies' underlying earnings.

This also explains something that looks like a paradox: why NEPSE's biggest bull run of the past decade began right after one of the worst global shocks in a century. When COVID-19 struck in early 2020, NEPSE stopped trading for about two months during the national lockdown. Almost everyone expected it to reopen into a long bear market, like the sharp falls in world markets at the time. Instead NEPSE rallied, from about 1,200 when trading resumed in mid-2020 to an all-time intraday high above 3,220 about fourteen months later. The reason was entirely domestic liquidity, not global mood. Interest rates were cut and money was pumped in at home. Lockdowns shut off other uses for savings — travel, spending, informal investment. And returning or stranded remittance money flowed into bank deposits, and from there into margin-financed shares. NEPSE became the main outlet for a pile of domestic cash. The lesson cuts against the easy assumption that "global crisis means NEPSE crash". What decides NEPSE's direction is Nepali bank liquidity. A global shock only becomes a NEPSE shock once it has worked through remittances and reserves into that liquidity. A global event that tightens Nepali bank money (like the 2022 oil and Fed shock) hurts NEPSE. One that ends up loosening it (like the pandemic-era rate cuts and forced savings) can lift NEPSE even while the rest of the world sells.

WARNING Margin-financed positions in NEPSE are exposed to a risk that has nothing to do with the shares being held: a change in NRB's macroprudential settings, driven by forex reserve conditions entirely outside any company's control, can trigger a margin call regardless of that company's earnings trajectory. Investors carrying leveraged positions should track NRB's monetary policy stance and reserve trajectory as closely as they track sector fundamentals.

Lesson 6.5 — Historical Case Studies: Earthquake, Blockade, and Pandemic

It is worth walking through Nepal's three biggest shocks of the past decade in more detail. They show the mechanisms above in concrete form. And they are often confused with each other, or lumped together as "global" events, when they differ in where they came from and how they spread.

The April 2015 Gorkha earthquake was a domestic natural disaster, not a global shock. But it belongs here because it shows how fragile NEPSE is to any big outside disruption. The exchange stopped trading for close to a month while the country dealt with the immediate crisis. When it reopened, the index fell sharply in its first sessions, then settled into a long, quiet spell. The hardest-hit shares were predictable: hotels and tourism (with trekking routes and heritage sites damaged); cement and building materials (hurt by disrupted supply at first, though rebuilding later helped them); and general insurers facing a wave of claims. The wider lesson is about NEPSE's thin trading and its reliance on a small number of domestic investors. Any shock big enough to shake confidence broadly — wherever it starts — tends to cause bigger and longer index moves than its direct economic cost would suggest. There is no foreign buyer waiting to snap up bargains and steady the market, as there might be in a market with active foreign funds.

The 2015–16 India trade and transit blockade, which followed the earthquake and the new constitution, is more directly relevant to this chapter. It worked much like the oil-and-peg channel in Lesson 6.3, but it was triggered by a political dispute with India rather than a world commodity shock. For about five months, most of Nepal's overland trade with and through India — the source of nearly all its fuel, most consumer goods and most industrial inputs — was disrupted. The real economy suffered badly: severe fuel and cooking gas shortages, factories short of inputs, and GDP growth for the year falling close to zero, one of the weakest readings in Nepal's modern history. Yet NEPSE kept rising through the blockade, carried by the bank capital-raising rally, while industrial, manufacturing and consumer shares lagged. The parallel with an oil shock is useful: whether fuel and trade are cut off by a border dispute or by a war affecting world oil, the damage reaches Nepal's economy — and from there NEPSE — through the same choke point: NOC's ability to get fuel, and the economy's dependence on open overland trade.

CAUTION Not every shock that hits NEPSE is a "global" event in the sense of originating from Fed policy, oil markets, or international risk sentiment. Domestic and bilateral political shocks — an earthquake, a border or trade dispute, a fuel supply disruption with India — can produce effects on NEPSE that are observationally similar to global macro shocks because they travel through the same reserve and liquidity chokepoints. Diagnosing the actual origin of a shock matters for judging how long it will last and what would resolve it.

COVID-19, already covered in Lesson 6.4 for its liquidity effects, deserves a second look as a case of a truly global shock producing a very local market result. Everything about the pandemic shock was global: the virus, the near-simultaneous lockdowns, the collapse in travel and tourism, and the panic that hit nearly every stock market in March 2020. Nepal's first experience fitted that pattern exactly: stranded migrant workers in the Gulf and Malaysia, a collapse in tourism earnings, and a lockdown that stopped NEPSE trading altogether. What did not fit was the multi-year bull run once trading resumed, driven by the liquidity effect already described. That divergence is the lesson. An investor who expected NEPSE to follow the S&P 500 or the Sensex after COVID would have badly misjudged both the timing and the size of the recovery, because they were reasoning from global mood instead of from Nepali bank liquidity, remittances and NRB policy.

Lesson 6.6 — Building an Event-Risk Framework for the NEPSE Investor

With the mechanism, the history and the channels in place, the remaining question is practical: what should you watch, month by month, to see global risk coming before it reaches NEPSE?

The framework below follows the same chain as this chapter, from the most distant, slowest signals to the closest, fastest ones. You do not need to check them daily. A monthly review, timed roughly around NRB's own publications, is enough for most retail investors — and far better than noticing economic stress only after NEPSE has already fallen.

Furthest away are world interest rates and commodity prices: the US Federal Reserve's rate path and guidance, the general direction of the US dollar, and the price of Brent or Dubai crude oil. These are early signals of early signals. If you see a surprise tightening by the Fed or a lasting oil spike, expect pressure on Nepal's reserves within one or two quarters — well before it shows up in any Nepali data.

One step closer are Gulf and Malaysian job markets. Watch for news of construction projects slowing or speeding up in Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Qatar; changes to policies replacing foreign workers with locals; and Malaysian or Korean recruitment quota decisions. These feed remittances with a delay of roughly two to four quarters.

Closer still are NRB's own reports, especially the regular "Current Macroeconomic and Financial Situation" release. It shows remittance growth, the balance of payments and — the figure this chapter keeps stressing — foreign exchange reserves in months of import cover. This is where global pressure first shows up in Nepali numbers, usually one or two quarters before NRB tightens.

Closest of all, and most useful for action, are NRB's policy announcements: changes to the policy rate, the CRR, the CD ratio ceiling and margin loan-to-value limits. Watch also the banks' own liquidity, usually summed up in the interbank rate (what banks charge each other overnight) and how much they borrow from NRB's standing liquidity facility. These signals have the shortest delay to NEPSE, often showing effects within weeks.

PRACTICAL TOOL A monthly event-risk checklist for the NEPSE investor: (1) Has the Fed signalled a shift in its rate path, and how has the dollar index moved? (2) Has Brent or Dubai crude moved more than roughly ten percent in the past month? (3) Is there news of hiring freezes, project slowdowns, or labor policy changes in Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Malaysia, or South Korea? (4) What does NRB's latest macroeconomic report show for remittance growth and months of import cover? (5) Has NRB changed the policy rate, CRR, CD ratio ceiling, or margin lending LTV limits in the past quarter? (6) What is the current interbank lending rate, as a proxy for how tight or loose BFI liquidity actually is right now? A negative or deteriorating reading across several of these should raise an investor's caution level on leveraged NEPSE positions well before the index itself shows visible weakness.

Be clear about what this framework is not. It is not a timing tool that tells you to sell on a particular day. Nepal's data comes out late, so by the time a reserve or remittance figure is published, some of its message has already shown up in bank behaviour and market talk. Nor does it say global events are the only, or even the main, driver of NEPSE. Company profits, sector rules (a hydropower tariff decision, an insurance regulation, a bank merger policy) and domestic politics often dominate in the short run, and most of this book deals with exactly those. What the framework gives you is this: when a global shock does happen, a rough sense of how big and how lasting its NEPSE effect is likely to be, and how long it will take to arrive. That is exactly what you need to decide whether to cut borrowing, tighten your exit levels or simply hold — because the process, however indirect, is neither mysterious nor unpredictable once you understand it.

Chapter recap

NEPSE has almost no foreign institutional investors, but that does not make it immune to global shocks; it means they travel through slower, more indirect channels. The main channel is remittances: Nepali workers in the Gulf, Malaysia and South Korea send home money that acts as Nepal's real foreign capital inflow, and changes in hiring there reach Nepali banks and NEPSE liquidity within a few quarters. A second channel is Nepal's total dependence on imported fuel combined with the fixed peg to the Indian rupee. Oil price spikes and a Fed-driven strong dollar hit together, raising the oil bill and weakening the currency it is paid in, which squeezes NRB's foreign exchange reserves. The reserve position, best tracked through NRB's "months of import cover" figure, decides how much room NRB has before it must tighten policy or restrict imports. That tightening — higher policy rates, a higher cash reserve ratio, a binding CD ratio ceiling and lower loan-to-value limits on share loans — is what actually reaches NEPSE, because banks are both the biggest sector on the exchange and almost the only source of margin loans. A global shock that tightens bank money causes forced selling unrelated to any company's profits. This is why NEPSE rallied after COVID-19, on a domestic cash surplus, and why it fell in 2022 on a chain that began with a distant Fed decision and an oil spike. The 2015 earthquake, the 2015–16 India blockade and the COVID-19 pandemic each show a version of this chain, and telling a truly global shock from a domestic or bilateral one helps you judge how long an episode will last. A disciplined investor watches Fed signals and oil prices, Gulf and Malaysian job news, NRB's monthly remittance and reserve data, and NRB's policy settings — and uses worsening signs as an early warning to cut borrowed exposure before the index itself shows stress.
