A market is not an abstraction. It is a collection of human beings and institutions, each with distinct objectives, constraints, information sets, and behavioural tendencies, interacting through a common mechanism — the price auction — to buy and sell claims on productive enterprises. The character of a market, its volatility, its efficiency, its susceptibility to manipulation and euphoria, its capacity for rational price correction — all of these emerge from the specific mix of participants who trade within it.

NEPSE's participant structure is unusual by any global standard. It is one of the most retail-dominated equity markets in Asia. Its institutional investor base is thin, constrained by mandate and bureaucratic culture. Its mutual fund sector is small and limited in depth. Its broker network has grown from about 50 firms to more than 90, but most are small — and one collapsed amid fraud in 2026. Foreign institutional participation is legally restricted and practically minimal. And it has no market makers whatsoever — a structural absence with profound consequences for liquidity and price formation.

This chapter maps everyone who trades on NEPSE: who they are, how they are organised, what drives them and how they affect each other. Most important, it explains what this particular mix of players means for you — why NEPSE behaves the way it does, and how to place your money sensibly within it.

Lesson 9.1 — Retail Investors: The Dominant Force and the Structural Consequences

The Scale of Retail Dominance

In most developed equity markets, retail investors — individual citizens trading their personal savings — constitute a minority of daily trading volume, typically 15–25%. The majority is accounted for by institutional participants: mutual funds, pension funds, hedge funds, insurance companies, proprietary trading desks, and market makers. These institutional participants bring larger capital pools, more analytical resources, longer investment horizons (in some cases), and more disciplined risk management frameworks.

NEPSE turns that picture upside down. Retail investors — ordinary individuals — make up an estimated 80–85% of daily trading. Institutions such as Citizen Investment Trust, the Employees Provident Fund, insurance companies and mutual funds make up the rest, and even they behave only partly like institutions, as later lessons show. NEPSE is, quite literally, a people's market. Its prices are set mainly by the combined decisions of millions of individual Nepalis, most trading on their phones. By September 2026 there were about 8.1 million demat accounts but only about 3.6 million trading accounts with brokers — so the people who actually trade number in the low millions, not eight million. Most decide using social media mood, tips from friends and gut feeling, rather than careful study of company accounts.

Who Are Nepal's Retail Investors?

Who the retail investor is has changed sharply over the past decade. Before MeroShare (roughly before 2018–19), the typical investor was urban, male, middle-aged and dealt with the market through a broker. Since then the base has widened a great deal. It now includes students and graduates in their twenties, Nepalis working in the Gulf and further abroad, small traders in district towns, and housewives and women entrepreneurs who were almost absent from the market ten years ago.

What most of these investors share is little formal financial education. Nepal's schools and universities have not usually taught personal finance, investing or how markets work. Most NEPSE investors have learned from family, colleagues, social media and their own trial and error. That is an expensive way to learn — the fees are paid in early losses — and it tends to reinforce certain mental biases and habits.

The Behavioural Profile of the NEPSE Retail Investor

Understanding the systematic behavioural patterns of NEPSE's retail base is one of the most practically useful analytical frameworks an investor can develop. These patterns are not unique to Nepal — they are well-documented in behavioural finance literature globally — but they manifest with particular intensity in a retail-dominated, illiquid market with rapid digital information spread.

Herding. NEPSE retail investors exhibit strong herding behaviour — the tendency to follow the crowd rather than form independent judgments. When a stock begins rising, the movement attracts attention through social media, broker networks, and word of mouth. Each incremental price rise attracts new buyers who fear missing out, which produces further price rises, attracting yet more buyers. This momentum cascade can drive stocks far above any reasonable fundamental value. It is like a queue outside a shop that grows simply because people see a queue. The reversal, when it comes, is equally cascade-driven: early sellers trigger price declines, which trigger panic and margin calls (NEPSE has no automatic stop-loss orders, so this selling is done by people, not machines), which trigger forced selling, which produces further declines. In a market dominated by institutional investors with contrarian mandates and long horizons, these cascades are dampened. In NEPSE, they are amplified.

Recency bias. Retail investors systematically overweight recent experience in forming expectations about future outcomes. Investors who entered NEPSE during the 2020–21 bull market, and who experienced rapid gains in their first year of participation, formed expectations of market returns based on that exceptional period. When prices began falling after August 2021, these investors initially interpreted it as a temporary dip — a buying opportunity — rather than a structural reversal, because their mental model of 'normal' was calibrated to the extraordinary. Conversely, investors who first lived through the long 2016–19 fall, before the pandemic boom, may have remained underexposed to equity for too long as prices recovered, anchored to their loss experience.

Dividend and bonus fixation. NEPSE investors pay unusual attention to dividend and bonus share announcements, often more than to the quality of earnings. A bonus share issue turns retained profits into new shares. It is almost always greeted with buying, even though, like a stock split, it does not make the company worth any more. Think of cutting a roti into eight pieces instead of four: you have more pieces, but no more roti. Someone who buys at NPR 1,000 before a 50% bonus issue, hoping to gain from the bonus, usually forgets that the price falls in proportion once the bonus is given. This habit creates predictable trading patterns around announcement dates, which careful investors can spot and sometimes use.

Margin overuse. NEPSE retail investors have a historical propensity to use margin lending — borrowing against their share portfolios to purchase additional shares — beyond levels consistent with their actual risk tolerance and financial capacity. Margin lending amplifies both gains and losses. In rising markets, investors who borrowed to buy more experienced spectacular returns that reinforced the behaviour. In falling markets, the same leverage produced margin calls that forced selling at depressed prices, locking in losses that took years to recover. The 2021–22 cycle produced a generation of Nepali investors who have experienced margin calls first-hand — a painful but potentially durable lesson in leverage risk.

IPO lottery mentality. As discussed in Chapter 8 (Lesson 8.5), the consistent IPO listing premium in Nepal's market history has conditioned retail investors to treat every IPO application as a lottery ticket with a positive expected value. This mentality drives extraordinary IPO oversubscription (popular issues often draw applications for dozens of times more shares than are on offer) and crowds out fundamental analysis of the issuing company's actual quality. When IPOs list below their issue price — as happens periodically, particularly in weak market conditions — investors who applied on auto-pilot absorb losses that a prospectus-reading approach might have avoided.

The Structural Consequences of Retail Dominance

The behavioural patterns of retail investors, operating in aggregate across millions of accounts, produce structural market characteristics that every NEPSE participant must understand and incorporate into their investment approach.

First, mood-driven swings. NEPSE prices move much more than the underlying news justifies. A change in NRB's policy — which in a deeper market of professional investors might be priced in calmly over several days — can move NEPSE several percent in a single day, as retail investors react together in panic or excitement. Swings like this are why, since April 2026, a 5% index move in the first two hours pauses the whole market for 15 minutes, and an 8% move closes it for the day. Social media spreads each change of mood instantly, to everyone at once.

Second, calendar patterns. Retail behaviour follows the calendar: the dividend season (roughly Bhadra to Chaitra, mid-August to mid-April, when audited results, dividend proposals and AGMs follow the mid-July end of the fiscal year), the AGM season and IPO allotment periods. Knowing when retail investors tend to buy (before dividend announcements, at the start of a new fiscal year) and when they tend to sell (after the book-closure date for a dividend) is practical market knowledge that sits alongside company analysis.

Third, the thin-market premium. Because retail investors in illiquid NEPSE stocks trade in small quantities, a relatively small coordinated buying campaign can push prices significantly above fundamental value — and a relatively small coordinated selling campaign can push them significantly below. This creates opportunities for well-capitalised, patient investors who understand fundamental value and can wait for the retail herd to push prices to attractive entry points in the correction phase.

Retail Investor Behaviour
  • Herding: Momentum is amplified in both directions — use fundamental anchors to avoid crowd capture.
  • Recency bias: Bull market entrants systematically underestimate correction depth; bear market entrants underestimate recovery.
  • Bonus fixation: Price typically adjusts post-bonus — buy the business, not the bonus announcement.
  • Margin overuse: High aggregate margin debt is a leading indicator of correction vulnerability — track NRB margin data.
  • IPO lottery mentality: Read the prospectus; not all IPOs list at premiums, and quality filtering adds edge.
  • Social media sentiment as a contrarian indicator: Peak social media bullishness historically precedes corrections; peak pessimism precedes recoveries.

Lesson 9.2 — Institutional Investors: CIT, EPF, Insurance Companies — Who They Are and How They Move the Market

The Institutional Investor Landscape

Institutions are few, but they trade in big amounts. When one of them decides to buy or sell heavily, it can move a share's price on its own, and other investors often read its moves as a signal. So it pays to know who the main institutions are, what rules they follow and what holds them back.

Citizen Investment Trust (CIT)

Citizen Investment Trust (CIT) is Nepal's oldest and best-known institutional investor, set up in 1991 under the Citizen Investment Trust Act 2047. Its main business is managing savings schemes. The best known are its Unit Scheme, which works like an open-ended mutual fund for Nepali citizens, and its Retirement Savings Scheme, which collects contributions from employees — especially civil servants and staff of state bodies — toward their retirement. CIT's own shares are listed on NEPSE. By 2025–26 it had more than 800,000 members and a fund of about Rs 300 billion.

CIT may invest in shares, government bonds, debentures and property. But it manages retirement savings for hundreds of thousands of Nepalis who depend on that money later in life, so it is cautious. Most of its money goes into low-risk assets such as government bonds, fixed deposits and treasury bills. Its shareholding is large in rupees, but it is a minority of the total it manages.

CIT's equity investments in NEPSE tend to be concentrated in the large-cap banking sector — the same concentration bias that characterises the headline NEPSE index. CIT rarely holds meaningful positions in smaller hydropower developers, microfinance companies, or manufacturing stocks, because the liquidity of those stocks is insufficient to allow CIT to build or exit positions of any significant size without materially moving the price.

CIT affects the market in two ways. It buys large bank shares when its own process finds them cheap, which can act as a rough floor under prices. And it sells near market peaks when it needs to rebalance or pay out savers. CIT does not report its trades in real time — it is not required to, the way a listed company must report major events — so observers guess its activity from price moves in particular shares and from its periodic portfolio reports.

Employee Provident Fund (EPF)

The Employees Provident Fund (EPF, or Karmachari Sanchaya Kosh) is Nepal's oldest and largest provident fund. It holds the retirement savings of government and other formal-sector employees — about 575,000 contributors in 2026. Its fund runs to several hundred billion rupees, more than CIT's. A newer player is growing fast: the Social Security Fund (SSF), set up for private-sector workers, had collected about Rs 117 billion from more than 3 million contributors by mid-July 2026. Like EPF, it keeps most of its money in bank deposits.

EPF's investment mandate is even more conservative than CIT's. As a pension fund managing the retirement security of working Nepalis, EPF is structurally averse to equity risk and has historically allocated the overwhelming majority of its portfolio to government bonds and bank fixed deposits. Its equity allocation to NEPSE, while real, has been a small fraction of its total assets.

For NEPSE investors, EPF matters less for what it does today than for what it could do. If EPF raised its share investments from, say, 5% to 15% of its assets — as pension funds in similar markets have done — the extra money flowing into NEPSE would be huge. Such a shift moved closer in 2026. In May the government widened what EPF, CIT and the SSF may invest in, including mutual funds, and its September 2026 capital-market plan called for the legal changes needed for them to invest more in securities by mid-December 2026. Barriers remain: EPF's own law limits some kinds of investment; its board must approve any big change; and putting pension money "at risk" in shares is politically sensitive. It is worth following this debate, because a real change would bring a large new source of demand. But it may take years, or never happen, so it is a reason to stay informed, not to buy in advance.

Insurance Companies: Life and Non-Life

Nepal's insurance sector is regulated by the Nepal Insurance Authority (Nepal Beema Pradhikaran), which replaced the old Beema Samiti under the Insurance Act 2079 in 2022. Mergers have cut the number of companies to about fourteen life insurers and fourteen non-life insurers in 2026, down from the high teens and twenties around 2024; most are listed on NEPSE. Together they hold a large pool of money to invest. Life insurers in particular build up big reserves from long-term policies.

The Nepal Insurance Authority's investment regulations require insurance companies to maintain specified proportions of their investible assets in approved categories: a minimum in government securities, a portion in infrastructure bonds, and a permitted allocation to equity. The equity allocation is capped by regulation, which limits the maximum position any single insurance company can take in a listed security — preventing concentrated risk-taking and ensuring diversification.

Insurance companies are among the more analytically disciplined participants in NEPSE, because the Nepal Insurance Authority requires them to justify their equity investments through documented investment policies and board-approved frameworks. However, their investment teams are small (many insurance companies have only one or two investment professionals), and their analytical capabilities — while improving — remain limited compared to global standards.

Insurance company share portfolios matter most around the end of the fiscal year in Ashadh (mid-July). Insurers often rebalance then to meet the regulator's rules, which can create noticeable seasonal buying or selling in particular shares.

How Institutional Investors Move the Market

Even with their limits, Nepal's institutions can move NEPSE prices sharply when they act, because they are large compared with typical retail trades. If CIT decided to put NPR 500 million into one bank's shares — a small trade in India or any developed market — it could push that bank's price up 5–8% over a week of steady buying.

The practical implication for retail investors: watching for unusual volume accumulation in large-cap stocks — significantly above average daily turnover, sustained over several sessions — can sometimes signal institutional accumulation or distribution. This is not a foolproof signal, but combined with fundamental analysis and valuation context, it can provide useful corroboration of a thesis.

Lesson 9.3 — Mutual Funds in Nepal: Structure, NAV, Available Schemes, and Their Limited Market Depth

The Mutual Fund Ecosystem

Mutual funds in Nepal are registered and regulated under the Securities Act 2063 and the Mutual Fund Regulation 2067. A mutual fund is a pooled investment vehicle — it collects capital from multiple investors, issues units (not shares) representing proportional ownership of the pool, and invests the pooled capital across a portfolio of securities. The fund's Net Asset Value (NAV) — total assets minus total liabilities, divided by the number of units — is what one unit is really worth. Think of a group of friends pooling savings into one pot: the NAV is the value of one person's share of the pot. Open-end funds publish it every working day, and investors buy or sell units at that price. Closed-end funds publish it at least every week.

Nepal's mutual fund sector has grown meaningfully since the current generation of funds began launching around 2010 (earlier precursors include CIT's Unit Scheme and NCM Mutual Fund 2002), but remains small relative to the overall market and to comparable sectors in peer countries. By mid-2026, Nepal had about 60 schemes — 46 closed-end funds listed on NEPSE and 14 open-end schemes. Together they held roughly Rs 87 billion in shares, bank balances and bonds (Shrawan 2083). That is only about 1.5–2% of NEPSE's market value, and a tiny part of household savings.

Types of Schemes

Closed-end schemes. Most mutual funds in Nepal operate as closed-end schemes — they raise a fixed amount of capital through a public issue (similar to an IPO), list the fund units on NEPSE, and trade on the exchange like any other security. Investors who want to enter or exit the fund after the initial offering do so by buying or selling units on NEPSE, not by subscribing or redeeming directly with the fund. This means that the market price of a closed-end fund unit on NEPSE can, and frequently does, diverge from the fund's underlying NAV.

Open-end schemes. A smaller number of Nepali mutual funds operate as open-end schemes — investors can subscribe (buy units at NAV) or redeem (sell units back to the fund at NAV) on any business day. This structure ensures that the unit price always equals NAV, eliminating the discount-to-NAV problem that plagues closed-end funds. Open-end schemes in Nepal include CIT's Unit Scheme (technically the oldest and largest), and a growing number of schemes launched by private fund managers.

The Discount-to-NAV Problem

One of the most persistent anomalies in Nepal's mutual fund market is the phenomenon of closed-end funds trading at significant discounts to their NAV on NEPSE. A fund with an NAV of NPR 15 per unit may trade on NEPSE at NPR 11 or NPR 12 — a discount of about 20–27%. For a sophisticated investor, this raises an obvious question: why would you pay NPR 11 to acquire a claim on assets worth NPR 15?

The answer lies in the specific character of NEPSE's retail investor base. Retail investors, unfamiliar with NAV mechanics, often treat mutual fund units as ordinary stocks — valuing them based on momentum, recent price performance, and peer sentiment rather than on the relationship between market price and underlying asset value. This creates a structural mispricing that has persisted for years in the Nepali mutual fund market.

For the analytically literate investor, closed-end funds trading at deep discounts to NAV represent a structured opportunity: buying a diversified portfolio of NEPSE equities at a 20–30% discount to their current market value. The risk is that the discount does not close — that the fund continues to trade at a discount for the duration of the investment. But when the discount is wide enough, and when the underlying portfolio is of identifiable quality, the margin of safety is substantial.

Fund Managers and Their Limited Analytical Capacity

Nepal's mutual funds are managed by licensed Fund Management Companies (FMCs) — separate entities from the fund itself, which serve as investment managers and earn a management fee (typically 1–1.5% of NAV annually) for their services. The quality of fund management varies significantly across Nepal's FMC sector. A handful of the larger, more established fund managers have developed genuine research capabilities — analysts who track listed companies systematically, build financial models, and make investment decisions based on documented frameworks.

Most fund managers, however, run small teams — sometimes one manager and one or two junior analysts — without the tools or time to study the whole market properly. In practice, many Nepali mutual funds quietly copy the index. They hold the big bank shares that dominate NEPSE in roughly the same proportions as the index. That avoids the risk of picking stocks badly, but it also gives up the extra return that real stock picking is meant to deliver.

Mutual Funds as a Retail Investor Tool

For retail investors who lack the time, expertise, or inclination to analyse individual companies, mutual funds offer a legitimate alternative: professional management, diversification across multiple securities, and a regulated framework with daily NAV disclosure. In theory, investing in a well-managed NEPSE mutual fund is better than speculating in individual stocks based on social media tips.

In practice, the choice is harder than it looks. Management fees eat into returns over time. It is hard for ordinary investors to judge how good a fund manager is. Closed-end funds add the extra puzzle of trading above or below their NAV. And most Nepali funds were launched after 2010, so they have lived through only one or two market cycles — not enough to tell real skill from simply riding the market up. When you judge a Nepali mutual fund, look at four things: its stated investment approach; whether its holdings actually match that approach; the quality of its quarterly reports; and how its NAV has done against the NEPSE index, allowing for how concentrated it is in particular sectors. Since August 2026, SEBON has required fund managers to publish their scheme documents, daily unit sales and buy-backs, NAV and number of unit holders on their websites — so this information is now easier to find.

Mutual Fund Checklist
  • Is it closed-end or open-end? — Closed-end: check discount/premium to NAV before buying.
  • Who is the Fund Management Company? — Research their track record across market cycles.
  • What is the management fee? — 1–1.5% annually compounds significantly over long periods.
  • What is the portfolio composition? — Does it reflect the stated investment philosophy?
  • How has NAV performed vs. NEPSE Index? — Consistent underperformance vs. index questions active management value.
  • How liquid is the closed-end unit on NEPSE? — Thin trading volume creates entry/exit challenges.
  • Is the discount to NAV historically wide or narrow? — Wide discounts may represent opportunity; narrow premiums may warrant caution.

Lesson 9.4 — Licensed Brokers: The Broker Network, Commission Structure, and Limitations

The Broker's Role in NEPSE

Every transaction on NEPSE must be executed through a licensed stockbroker. An investor cannot place orders directly on the NEPSE trading system — they must instruct a licensed broker, who executes the order on their behalf through NEPSE's online trading system (NOTS, often still called NATS), usually through the broker's TMS. This intermediation requirement is universal in organised exchanges globally; what is distinctive about NEPSE's broker structure is the limited number of licensed brokers, their geographic concentration, their fee structure, and the quality and service level they provide.

The Broker Network: From 50 to 90-Plus

For most of NEPSE's history, Nepal had about 50 licensed stockbrokers — a number that stayed the same for years and was tiny for a market with so many investors and trades. That has changed. There were 90 broker firms by November 2024 (up from 82 a year earlier), and SEBON kept issuing new licences through 2026. Even so, 90-plus is small next to India's NSE, which has thousands of trading members, and smaller than Bangladesh's Dhaka Stock Exchange. So the broker network is still thin, even though the old "50 brokers" picture no longer fits.

For a long time, brokers were bunched in Kathmandu. Investors in Pokhara, Biratnagar, Butwal or Dharan had to rely on phone calls, informal agents or trips to the capital. That has eased. NEPSE has let brokers open branches outside the Valley since 2019, newer licences have included firms outside the Valley, and online trading through each broker's TMS (Trade Management System) lets anyone with a phone trade from anywhere. The network is wider than before, but most broker firms are still small.

Broker Commission Structure

SEBON sets the maximum commission rates that brokers can charge investors. The commission structure is tiered by transaction value — higher value transactions attract a lower percentage commission. The structure in force since 14 May 2024 (Jestha 1, 2081) is as follows:

Besides broker commission, investors pay a SEBON fee (currently 0.015% of the trade value), a CDSC depository fee, and capital gains tax on profits from selling shares. For individuals on listed shares, that tax has been 5% if held 365 days or less and 3.75% if held longer since 22 September 2026 (the 2026 budget had briefly raised the rates to 10% and 7.5%, from the earlier 7.5% and 5%). The government's September 2026 plan also proposes taxing only net gains — profits minus losses — worked out through the settlement system; check whether that has started before relying on it. Total trading costs are higher than in more competitive markets — India's discount brokers, such as Zerodha, have cut costs to almost nothing for many trades. Higher costs make frequent trading a little more expensive, which on cost grounds alone favours buying and holding.

Service Quality and Technology

The quality of brokerage services in Nepal varies enormously across its 90-plus licensed brokers. The largest and most established brokers — several of which are affiliates of commercial banks and financial institutions — offer functional online trading platforms, mobile apps, research reports, and client relationship services. Several smaller brokers continue to operate primarily through phone-based order execution, with basic or absent online infrastructure.

The TMS (Trading Management System), operated by NEPSE and accessible through broker interfaces, is the primary online trading interface for retail investors. While functional, TMS has historically faced capacity challenges during periods of high market activity — when every retail investor wants to trade simultaneously, the system has experienced slowdowns and outages that prevented order execution at critical moments. These technical limitations have real financial consequences for investors who rely on timely order execution. Two 2026 changes help. In April, after-market orders (AMO) were approved, so you can place an order outside trading hours and have it sent when the market opens. And in August, SEBON ordered NEPSE to run an IT audit and security test of TMS and to upgrade the trading system.

From Consolidation to Expansion — and a Warning

For years, SEBON and analysts argued for merging Nepal's brokers into fewer, stronger firms that could afford better technology, real research and branches outside Kathmandu. In 2023–24 the regulator went the other way. It licensed dozens of new brokers — for the first time including a bank — to widen access and competition. In June 2026 NEPSE had also licensed 31 brokers to offer margin trading (lending clients money to buy shares), with 24 more in line, and the government's September 2026 plan aims to have that system fully working by mid-January 2027. Later in September, SEBON issued the Securities Brokerage Business Strengthening Policy, 2083. It sorts brokers into four classes, a bit like grading hospitals by what treatment they may offer: A (stock dealers, which may also underwrite share issues and serve large institutions), B (full-service brokers), C (trading brokers, mainly carrying out clients' orders) and D (specialists, for example in bonds or institutional trades). Higher classes need more capital, and every firm must also hold capital in line with the risks it takes. Riskier services — margin lending, short selling, intraday trading and market making — each need separate SEBON approval. Client money and shares must be kept fully separate from the broker's own, checked regularly, and certified to SEBON every year. Brokers need two-step (multi-factor) login for clients, a named information-security officer and an emergency 'kill switch' to halt trading if systems fail, and licence renewal will depend on each firm's record. It is a framework only: SEBON still has to issue the detailed directives, including the rupee capital amounts, and existing brokers get a transition period whose length is not yet set.

More brokers, though, does not mean safer brokers. In April 2026, SEBON suspended Bhrikuti Stock Broking. It found that the firm had let one businessman trade large amounts without collateral, used other clients' deposits to settle those trades, and let clients buy without the required 25% advance. The firm owed investors about Rs 5.11 billion, and the trading of around 80,000 clients was frozen. Shares already sitting in clients' own demat accounts were not directly affected; the trouble was cash and unsettled trades. A few months later, SEBON ordered all brokers to keep clients' money in bank accounts separate from their own. The real question now is not 'how many brokers?' but 'how strong and how honest are they?' — a good question to ask about your own broker. Keep only the cash you need with a broker, and check your demat holdings in MeroShare regularly.

Sub-Brokers: The Informal Extension

Beyond Nepal's licensed brokers, a network of informal sub-brokers operates in Nepal — individuals who facilitate investor access to licensed brokers, often in geographic areas where licensed brokers have no presence. Sub-brokers collect orders from investors, route them through a licensed broker's account, and earn a share of the commission. Their operation exists in a regulatory grey zone: SEBON has regulations for formal sub-broker registration, but many informal participants operate without registration.

For investors using sub-broker networks — particularly in provincial areas — the risks are real: the sub-broker has no regulatory accountability for order execution quality, client fund safety, or advice accuracy. Cases of sub-broker fraud — collecting investor funds without routing them to the market, or diverting client accounts — have occurred and received limited regulatory redress. Investors should, where at all possible, establish direct relationships with licensed brokers through digital platforms rather than operating through informal sub-broker channels.

Lesson 9.5 — FII Restrictions, Allowed Limits, and Their Minimal Presence in NEPSE

The Foreign Institutional Investor Framework

Foreign institutional investors (FIIs) — overseas mutual funds, hedge funds, pension funds, sovereign wealth funds and similar bodies — are allowed in principle to invest in NEPSE. But the rules around them are so restrictive that almost none actually do. Understanding the rules, and why they produce so little foreign money, helps you judge NEPSE's future and what a real opening to foreign capital would mean.

Legal Framework for FII Investment

SEBON's rules on foreign investment in securities are the main framework for FII access. On paper, foreign institutions may invest in listed shares, government bonds and registered mutual funds, on four conditions: they must register with SEBON as foreign investors; they must trade through a licensed Nepali broker; they must keep their investments through a special non-resident account with a Nepali bank; and they are limited in how much of any one listed company they may own. (The exact regulation and its current status should be checked with SEBON, because in practice the route has barely been used.)

On paper, aggregate foreign ownership in a single listed company is capped (draft rules use figures around 30 percent of paid-up capital). In practice, as of late 2026, foreign portfolio buying on NEPSE's secondary market is still not operational, so treat this framework as the planned design rather than a working channel. For companies in specific sectors — banking and financial institutions, insurance, media, airlines — lower foreign ownership caps may apply under sector-specific regulations. Promoter-held shares typically cannot be sold to foreign investors without additional regulatory approval.

Capital Account Restrictions: The Practical Barrier

While the FII framework exists on paper, Nepal's capital account is not fully convertible — foreign investors cannot freely move capital in and out of Nepal on demand. Foreign exchange transactions related to investment must be routed through the banking system with NRB oversight, and repatriation of investment proceeds (both principal and returns) requires documentary compliance that adds friction, cost, and delay relative to markets with more open capital accounts.

These capital controls have been the main practical barrier to foreign funds — more important than ownership caps, registration or tax. A global fund manager looking at a small market asks one simple question: can I get my money in and out quickly when I need to? For Nepal, the traditional answer has been "yes, but with a lot of friction". The government's September 2026 capital-market plan aimed to open the market to Non-Resident Nepalis first, through changes to the foreign-investment and foreign-exchange laws; it set no date for foreign funds. Until foreign money can move in and out easily, most international funds — which must be able to repay their own investors on demand — will stay wary of NEPSE.

Why FII Participation Matters — or Could

Today, foreign institutions do not move NEPSE prices or liquidity in any real way. Their ownership of most NEPSE shares is well below 5%, and usually close to zero. That absence costs the market. Without foreign funds, NEPSE misses out on more sophisticated valuation methods, stronger demands for good corporate governance, and the steadying effect of investors with genuinely long-term horizons and different information.

The potential upside from meaningful FII liberalisation — a genuine opening of Nepal's capital account for institutional foreign investors, perhaps within a phased framework that starts with a pilot of registered frontier market funds — is substantial. A sustained inflow of even modest FII capital into NEPSE would improve liquidity, compress bid-ask spreads, improve price discovery, and raise market standards as listed companies sought to meet the more demanding disclosure and governance expectations of international investors. Whether such liberalisation will occur, and on what timeline, is fundamentally a question of Nepal's macroeconomic policy trajectory — specifically, the pace of current account improvement, foreign exchange reserve accumulation, and the government's appetite for financial sector reform.

FII Restrictions
  • NEPSE is effectively a closed domestic market — valuations are not anchored by global comparable analysis in real time.
  • The absence of FII arbitrageurs means that NEPSE can remain disconnected from regional and global market trends for extended periods.
  • Any regulatory development signalling capital account liberalisation would be a structural market positive — worth monitoring closely.
  • FII absence means corporate governance improvement is driven by domestic regulatory pressure alone — a slower mechanism than investor-driven pressure.
  • Nepal's equity risk premium is partly a function of capital account illiquidity — this will compress if and when the market opens to foreign capital.

Lesson 9.6 — NRN Investors: Rules, Account Types, and Repatriation Rights

Who Are NRN Investors?

Non-Resident Nepalis (NRNs) — Nepali citizens and persons of Nepali origin living and working outside Nepal — represent a distinct and growing participant category in NEPSE. The Non-Resident Nepali Act 2064 defines an NRN as a Nepali citizen who has lived outside the SAARC countries for at least two years, or a foreign citizen of Nepali origin living outside SAARC. That is narrower than 'Nepalis abroad'. The 2021 census found about 2.2 million Nepalis living away from home, most of them workers in the Gulf (Qatar, UAE, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait), Malaysia and India. Workers on short-term labour permits are treated separately from NRNs, as the IPO rules below show. The NRN diaspora proper — in the United States, the United Kingdom, Australia, Japan, Korea, Europe and elsewhere — is smaller but often wealthier.

Money sent home by Nepalis abroad — most of it from migrant workers rather than NRNs in the legal sense — has been Nepal's largest source of foreign currency for over a decade. In FY 2025/26 remittances reached Rs 2,363 billion, about 36% of GDP. The 2026 Gulf war is testing this lifeline: approvals for new Gulf jobs fell by about half between the war's start and mid-September, yet remittances were still up about 24% in the first two months of FY 2026/27 (see Chapter 6). The aggregate financial resources of the NRN community far exceed what is currently channelled into NEPSE, and expanding NRN investment participation in Nepal's capital market is a stated policy priority of both the Government of Nepal and the Non-Resident Nepali Association (NRNA).

The NRN Investment Account Framework

NRN investors can take part in NEPSE through a Non-Resident Nepali Investment Account (NRNIA) at a commercial bank in Nepal. It is a rupee account used to settle all their NEPSE activity: IPO applications, buying and selling on the market (for NRNs this is still being opened up: the government's September 2026 plan called for changes to the foreign-investment and foreign-exchange laws so NRNs can trade on the secondary market, and in October 2026 a SEBON regulation let companies issue shares in foreign currency to NRNs — check the current rules with your bank before you start), dividends and bonus shares. The account must be funded with money sent from abroad, converted at NRB's exchange rate. When the investor takes money back out, it is converted back to foreign currency at the rate of that day.

Two separate IPO channels matter here. Since November 2022, SEBON has required IPOs to reserve 10% of the public issue for Nepalis working abroad on labour permits, who apply through a remittance savings account. Some issues have also offered a separate allocation for NRNs. A reserved pool usually faces less competition than the hugely oversubscribed general pool, so the chance of getting shares is better.

Repatriation Rights

The ability to repatriate investment proceeds — to convert the Nepali rupee proceeds of NEPSE investments back to foreign currency and send the money abroad — is the most critical practical consideration for NRN investors. Under current regulations, NRN investors may repatriate: the principal amount of their initial investment (the foreign currency originally remitted to fund the NRNIA), dividends received on listed securities, and capital gains after applicable tax deductions.

Repatriation is processed through the banking system, requires documentation of the original investment and the tax payment on any gains, and is subject to NRB's foreign exchange regulations. In practice, repatriation has functioned relatively smoothly for NRN investors who have maintained clean NRNIA records — though the documentation requirements and bank processing timelines create friction that domestic investors do not face.

The exchange rate risk is an important consideration for NRN investors that is absent for domestic participants. A NRN who invests in NEPSE and earns a 20% return in Nepali rupees may find that the rupee has depreciated against their home currency (whether the US dollar, UAE dirham, or UK pound) during the same period, eroding the foreign-currency-equivalent return. Nepal's rupee is pegged to the Indian rupee (NPR 1.60 per INR), and the INR-USD rate therefore largely determines the NPR-USD rate. NRN investors should factor exchange rate risk into their expected return calculation and their risk management framework.

Tax Treatment for NRN Investors

NRN investors are subject to the same capital gains tax as domestic investors on their NEPSE gains (5% for securities held 365 days or less and 3.75% for longer holdings, since 22 September 2026). Dividends received are subject to a 5% withholding tax at source — withheld by the distributing company before the dividend is credited to the NRNIA. Double taxation treaty provisions may apply depending on the NRN's country of residence, potentially reducing the effective tax burden, but treaty applicability requires verification with a tax professional.

NRN Investment in NEPSE
  • Account type: Non-Resident Nepali Investment Account (NRNIA) with a commercial bank in Nepal.
  • Funding: Remittance from abroad — converted to NPR at prevailing NRB rate.
  • IPO access: 10% of IPOs is reserved for Nepalis on foreign-employment labour permits (since 2022); some issues also have an NRN allocation.
  • Repatriation: Principal, dividends, and post-tax capital gains may be repatriated.
  • Exchange rate risk: NPR-USD movements affect foreign-currency-equivalent returns — factor this in.
  • Tax: 5%/3.75% capital gains (short/long-term, since 22 September 2026); 5% dividend withholding at source.
  • DEMAT: Linked DEMAT account required — opened through the same commercial bank.

Lesson 9.7 — Promoter Shareholders vs. Public Shareholders: Lock-In Rules and Power Asymmetry

The Two Classes of Shareholders

Shareholders of Nepali listed companies fall into two distinct groups: promoter shareholders (the founders and their group) and public shareholders. The split is built into the Companies Act 2063 and SEBON's rules. It creates a basic imbalance of power that every public shareholder must understand, because it shapes company decisions, dividend policy, governance and the risk of promoters acting in their own interest at the expense of ordinary investors.

Promoter Shareholders: Who They Are

Promoter shareholders are the founding and controlling shareholders of a listed company — the individuals, families, or institutions who established the company and who collectively hold the majority of shares before the public offering. Under SEBON's regulations, promoter shares are defined as those held by the company's founder shareholders, institutional promoters, and strategic investors — categories that are specified in the company's founding documents and its prospectus.

In Nepali commercial banks, promoter shareholding typically constitutes 51% of paid-up capital, with the remaining 49% offered to the public through IPOs and FPOs. In other sectors, promoter proportions vary — some hydropower companies have higher promoter proportions, while some companies that have raised capital through multiple rounds of FPOs have diluted promoter stakes to lower levels.

Promoters are not a homogeneous group. In banking, prominent promoter categories include politically connected businesspeople, business families with established commercial networks, professional investors who participated in bank founding rounds, and institutional promoters (other banks, development banks, or business conglomerates). The character of the promoter group is one of the most important and least systematically analysed factors in assessing the governance risk of any specific NEPSE investment.

Lock-In Periods and Their Practical Significance

Promoter shares are subject to lock-in periods — restrictions on sale that prevent promoters from exiting their investment in the listed company for a specified period after listing. Under SEBON's regulations, the standard lock-in period for promoter shares in NEPSE-listed companies is usually three years from the IPO allotment for the initial promoter contribution, with additional restrictions applying to subsequent promoter share contributions and to shares held by related parties.

Lock-ins are meant to tie promoters to the company's long-term success. If promoters cannot sell for three years, they have more reason to build lasting value than to push up the price briefly and exit. Whether that works depends on the promoters. Some are genuinely committed to the business; others treat their shares mainly as a financial bet they plan to cash out of.

After the lock-in period expires, promoter shares become freely transferable, subject to SEBON's disclosure requirements for significant shareholding changes. Since 2026, SEBON has also required advance warning. Under its October 2026 rule, a basic (promoter) shareholder who plans to sell 15% or more of their holding must tell the company at least five trading days before, and the company must publish the notice through NEPSE. That gives public shareholders a few days' warning. But it still does not stop the sale.

The Power Asymmetry: How It Manifests

The most consequential dimension of the promoter-public shareholder divide is the governance power asymmetry it creates. Because promoters collectively hold the majority of shares in most NEPSE-listed companies, they control the Annual General Meeting — the forum at which shareholders vote on board composition, dividends, auditor appointments, capital raises, and related party transactions. A promoter group with 51% of shares can, in effect, pass any ordinary resolution — including related party transactions that may benefit promoters at public shareholders' expense — over the objection of all public shareholders combined.

Board composition. Promoters control the nomination and election of the majority of board members. While the rules require listed companies to have directors elected by public shareholders, and at least one independent director chosen for expertise rather than ownership, the selection of 'independent' directors in Nepal has not always reflected genuine independence. Some independent directors have had professional relationships with promoters that compromise their independence in practice. An investor assessing governance quality should examine the actual independence of the board's independent directors — their professional background, their other directorships, and their behaviour in board meetings (reflected in meeting minutes where available).

Dividend policy. Promoters decide dividend policy through their control of the board. In some Nepali companies, promoters have set dividends to suit their own tax position, family planning or reinvestment plans — not what public shareholders want. A promoter with big personal expenses may prefer large cash dividends. One building a wider business empire may prefer the company to keep its profits for growth or takeovers. Either choice may ignore what gives public shareholders the best return for the risk.

Related party transactions. Perhaps the most significant risk of the promoter-public shareholder divide is the potential for related party transactions — deals between the listed company and entities in which the promoters have interests — that transfer value from the public shareholders to the promoters. Nepali regulations require disclosure and audit committee approval for related party transactions, but the audit committee's independence from promoter influence varies, and SEBON's enforcement of related party transaction standards has been inconsistent. Investors should read the related party transaction disclosures in every annual report of a holding with particular scrutiny.

Promoter Share Pledging: A Hidden Risk

One practice that has caused real harm on NEPSE is promoters pledging their shares for bank loans. Promoters who need money, but cannot sell their locked-in shares, often pledge them as security instead. If a promoter then fails to repay, the bank seizes the shares and sells them. That creates sudden forced selling, often large compared with the share's normal daily trading, and the price can drop sharply — hurting public shareholders who had nothing to do with the promoter's borrowing.

SEBON requires promoters to disclose share pledges above set thresholds, and NEPSE publishes data on pledged promoter shares. Checking how much of a company's promoter holding is pledged is a simple, practical safety check. A high and rising pledge level suggests the promoter group is under financial strain, and it means forced selling could hit the share price hard if the loans go bad.

Promoter Risk Indicators
  • Promoter pledge %: Exceeding 50% of promoter shares pledged signals significant financial stress risk.
  • Promoter share sales post-lock-in: Large sales immediately after lock-in expiry signal low promoter conviction.
  • Advance sale notices: a published notice that promoters plan to sell 15% or more of their holding is worth reading before you buy.
  • Related party transaction volume: Rapidly growing RPTs relative to revenue warrant deep scrutiny.
  • Board independence: Directors with prior professional relationships with promoters are not genuinely independent.
  • AGM voting patterns: Do public shareholders ever succeed in any resolution opposed by promoters? Absence of this suggests governance capture.
  • Audit qualifications: Emphasis-of-matter paragraphs in audit reports often flag related party or accounting concerns first.

Lesson 9.8 — Market Makers: Why NEPSE Has None and the Liquidity Consequences

What Is a Market Maker?

A market maker is a firm — usually a licensed broker, bank or specialist — that promises to quote both a buying price (bid) and a selling price (ask) for a share all day, for at least a minimum quantity. Because it always stands ready to trade, it supplies liquidity: if you want to sell, it buys; if you want to buy, it sells. In return, it earns the small gap between its buying and selling prices (the bid-ask spread), as payment for the risk of holding stock. Think of the sahuji at a busy haat who always has rice to sell and will always buy more — you never have to wait for another farmer to turn up.

Market making is a foundational institutional feature of all developed equity markets. On the NYSE, designated market makers (DMMs) are assigned to specific stocks and are legally obligated to provide continuous two-sided quotes. On NASDAQ, multiple competing market makers quote simultaneously in each stock, providing competitive liquidity. Even in most mid-tier emerging markets — India's BSE and NSE, Thailand's SET, Malaysia's Bursa — some form of market making or liquidity provision exists for less-traded securities.

NEPSE Has No Market Makers

Nepal Stock Exchange has no market makers. None. Zero. Every transaction on NEPSE depends on the coincidence of a willing buyer and a willing seller at the same price, at the same time. There is no designated participant whose role and regulatory obligation is to stand ready to provide a two-sided market in any security during trading hours. This is not a minor technical gap — it is a foundational structural absence with profound consequences for NEPSE's liquidity profile, price formation quality, and the risk faced by individual investors.

Consequence 1: Bid-Ask Spreads Are Wide and Variable

Without market makers narrowing the gap between bid and ask prices through continuous quoting, NEPSE bid-ask spreads are determined solely by the coincident preferences of retail buyers and sellers. In liquid, high-volume stocks — major commercial banks on active trading days — the spread may be acceptably narrow. But in less-traded stocks — smaller hydropower developers, manufacturing companies, many microfinance institutions — the spread can be 2–5% or more of the stock's price.

A bid-ask spread of 3% means that if you buy a share and sell it straight away, you lose 3% plus trading costs. That is a real obstacle to trading that hardly exists where market makers operate. It also means the price on your screen may not be a price you can actually trade at. If a thinly traded share has not changed hands for two days, its last price is not what you can buy or sell at now; the real price could be quite different.

Consequence 2: Price Discontinuities and Gaps

In market-made securities, price discovery is continuous: there is always a market maker willing to transact, so prices move in small increments reflecting new information. In NEPSE's unmade market, information can arrive — a quarterly result, a regulatory announcement, an NRB policy change — when no natural buyer or seller is present in a specific stock. The result is a price gap: the stock's price at the next transaction can be significantly different from the last price, because there was no market maker absorbing the information flow and gradually adjusting quotes. These gaps create risk for investors whose orders are waiting in the system at prices that are no longer relevant to current conditions.

Consequence 3: The Lower-Circuit Trap

The most severe practical consequence of the absence of market makers occurs during market stress. When negative news or sentiment drives retail investors to sell, and no institutional buyer is stepping in (because there are no institutional buyers obligated to do so), a stock can hit its lower circuit limit within minutes of opening. Without a market maker willing to buy at, or near, the limit price, the stock remains locked at the lower circuit — any sell orders at prices below the circuit cannot execute, but there may be no buyers even at the circuit price.

In a market-made system, this situation would not persist: the market maker, contractually obligated to provide a buy quote, would absorb some of the selling pressure. In NEPSE, a lower-circuit lock can persist for days — each day the stock opens and hits its lower circuit, falls another 15%, and closes. An investor trapped in such a position cannot exit regardless of their willingness to accept a loss. They must wait for a natural buyer to emerge or for the circuit to stabilise prices long enough for market psychology to shift.

Consequence 4: Illiquidity Premium and Valuation Discount

The absence of market makers in NEPSE creates what finance calls an illiquidity premium: buyers want a higher expected return from shares that are hard to sell, so they will only buy them at a discount to fair value. A hydropower company with sound fundamentals and a reasonable DCF valuation (a value worked out from its expected future cash flows) of NPR 300 per share may trade at NPR 220 in NEPSE's unmade market, because potential buyers know that exiting the position will be difficult if their investment thesis changes. This structural discount — the liquidity discount — means that NEPSE stocks can be genuinely cheap relative to fundamental value for extended periods, simply because the market mechanism to close that gap is absent.

For the long-horizon investor, this liquidity discount is an opportunity: you buy fundamentally sound businesses at prices below intrinsic value, and you hold long enough that either the business's earnings growth closes the gap, or market conditions improve sufficiently that other buyers enter. The risk is that the exit remains difficult — you may have to sell at an equally large discount when you choose to exit, if market conditions have not improved. True long-horizon investing in NEPSE's unmade market requires an acceptance that position exits may be slow, costly in spread terms, and subject to significant price impact.

The Case for Market Making in NEPSE

SEBON and NEPSE accept that the lack of market makers weakens the market, and they have discussed creating a framework for them. It is not simple. Market makers need to borrow shares easily (so they can sell when buyers outnumber sellers), tools to protect themselves against price swings in their stock, and a large enough bid-ask spread to make the risk worthwhile. All of these are currently missing or underdeveloped on NEPSE. The government's September 2026 plan asks for changes to the Securities Act to allow securities lending and borrowing and short selling — two of the building blocks market makers need.

The introduction of securities lending (allowing institutional investors to lend their long-term holdings to market makers temporarily), combined with a formal market maker licensing regime under SEBON, would be transformative for NEPSE's market quality. The timeline for such a development depends on regulatory willingness, technological infrastructure investment, and the availability of sufficiently capitalised institutions willing to take on the market maker role. Investors who anticipate this development — and who understand how market quality improvements have driven re-rating of comparable frontier markets — may find it an important structural theme in their NEPSE investment thesis.

Market Structure Implications
  • Size positions in proportion to a stock's average daily volume — never build a position you cannot exit in 10–15 trading days at normal volumes.
  • Factor the bid-ask spread into return calculations for all but the most liquid banking stocks.
  • Always set your own price (a limit order) in illiquid stocks — the last price on the screen may be days old.
  • Monitor promoter pledge data — forced selling by banks on defaulted pledged shares is NEPSE's equivalent of a surprise liquidity shock.
  • In market stress, expect lower-circuit locks to persist — plan exit timelines with an allowance for illiquidity.
  • The illiquidity discount in fundamentally sound stocks is an opportunity for patient capital — NEPSE's structural imperfections are the source of its alpha opportunities.
