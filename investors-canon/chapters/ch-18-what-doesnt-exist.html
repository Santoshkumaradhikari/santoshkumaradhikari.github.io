What Does Not Yet Exist in Nepal

The tools a market offers decide which strategies are possible. In New York, Mumbai or Singapore, investors can bet on falling prices, insure a portfolio with options, buy a whole index in one trade, or own a slice of an office tower through the stock exchange. On NEPSE, in November 2026, they can do almost none of this. This chapter maps what is missing, what is actually being built, and what that means for an ordinary Nepali investor today.

Three Questions to Keep Separate

Market talk in Nepal often mixes up three different kinds of statement. Keep them apart:

Type of statementExampleHow you check it
1. A fact about the market"NEPSE has no short selling."SEBON, NEPSE and CDSC rules and registers
2. An economic mechanism"Without short selling, pessimists have one less way to act on their views."Financial reasoning
3. A claim about cause"The lack of short selling caused the 2021–22 crash."Evidence that rules out other causes

The common mistake is to jump from the first straight to the third. Part 0 showed that NEPSE's big cycles follow liquidity and credit: NRB policy, bank cash and lending limits. Missing instruments shape how those cycles play out. They are not a rival explanation for them.

From Announcement to Something You Can Actually Buy

Think of a new road to your village. First people talk about it; then it appears in the budget; then engineers draw the plan; then the road is built; then it opens; and only after the first monsoon do you know whether buses can use it all year. Financial instruments go through the same stages:

StageWhat has happenedWhat it means for you
1. Talked aboutDiscussion in articles, conferences and policy circlesIgnore for investment decisions
2. Named as a goalA budget, a SEBON roadmap or a policy lists itNote it; no timing is promised
3. Rules draftedA concept paper or draft regulation is out for commentLearn how it would work; do not assume a launch date
4. Systems builtNEPSE, CDSC, clearing and brokers set up the technologyWatch for test notices
5. ApprovedFinal rules issued; licences grantedCheck which securities and brokers qualify
6. LiveReal trades happenCheck how much actually trades, and at what cost
7. MatureMany participants, steady trading, tight pricesCan become a regular part of a portfolio

A news headline saying "SEBON plans X" usually means stage 2 or 3. It is not a product you can use.

Lesson 18.1 — Derivatives: What They Do and Why NEPSE Has None Yet

What a Derivative Is

A derivative is a contract whose value depends on something else — a share, an index, gold, a currency or an interest rate. Two kinds matter most:

FUTURES A binding agreement to buy or sell something at a fixed price on a future date. Think of a cardamom trader in Ilam agreeing in Baisakh to buy a farmer's harvest at Rs 1,800 a kilo in Kartik, whatever the market price then. Both sides must honour it. On an exchange, each side posts a deposit (margin) and gains or losses are settled every day.
OPTIONS The right, but not the duty, to buy (a "call") or sell (a "put") at a fixed price before a set date, in return for an upfront fee (the "premium"). Like paying a small advance to hold a plot of land: if prices rise you buy; if not, you lose only the advance.
HEDGING Using one position to reduce the risk of another. A shareholder who buys a put option on the index is hedging against a market fall — much like buying insurance on a house.

Derivatives do three jobs: they let people pass risk to others who are willing to carry it (hedging); they help prices react quickly to news, because a small deposit controls a large position; and they let large investors change their risk without selling the shares they own.

Nepal's Position

As of November 2026, NEPSE has no exchange-traded equity derivatives: no index futures, no single-stock futures and no listed options. Every trade is a "spot" trade — you pay for and receive the actual shares. Settlement runs on a T+2 cycle: the trade happens on day T, buyers pay in on T+1, and shares and money are paid out on T+2 (Chapter 10). Commodity derivatives have a separate law (Lesson 18.4) but no live exchange.

Derivatives are on SEBON's list. The 10-year Capital Market Development Roadmap unveiled in July 2026 names derivatives among new instruments, with new products planned for a second phase from 2028 to 2030. The 2083/84 (2026/27) budget mentioned intraday trading, short selling and derivatives "in phases". On 2 November 2026 (16 Kartik 2083), SEBON formed a seven-member committee, including NRB and NEPSE representatives, to build the legal, technical and institutional groundwork for bonds, ETFs, REITs, InvITs and, "gradually", derivatives. That puts equity derivatives at stage 2 of the ladder above.

The reasons for caution are practical. Derivatives need a clearing house that can collect margin and settle gains and losses daily, guaranteeing every trade (a "central counterparty"); a deep and reliable underlying market; market makers who quote both buying and selling prices; and investors who understand leverage. Without these, derivatives can magnify losses rather than spread risk.

International Comparisons, Stated Carefully

Options traded informally in the United States long before 1973; what began in April 1973 was the Chicago Board Options Exchange, the first exchange for standardised, centrally cleared stock options. In India, the National Stock Exchange started equity derivatives with index futures in June 2000, later adding index and stock options. India's derivatives boom came alongside electronic trading, depository reform, foreign investment and a strong clearing system — not from the contracts alone.

Two Claims to Drop

"The months before derivatives arrive are the best time to buy." There is no reliable evidence that emerging markets deliver unusual returns just before derivatives launch. Proving it would need a defined list of markets and launch dates, total returns, risk adjustment and a check for selection bias. Derivatives change how people hedge and trade; share values still depend on profits, interest rates, liquidity and governance.

"Without derivatives, NEPSE prices are purer." NEPSE avoids some derivative-related trading flows, such as hedging around monthly expiry dates. That does not make it more transparent. Transparency depends on company disclosure, visible order books, ownership reporting and surveillance against circular trading — all of which can be weak in a spot-only market.

What it means for you: you cannot insure a NEPSE portfolio against a market-wide fall on the exchange. Protection has to come from how you build the portfolio — position sizes, sector spread, and holding more cash when liquidity is tightening (Part 0).

Lesson 18.2 — ETFs: Their Absence and What Passive Investors Can Do Instead

How an ETF Works

An exchange-traded fund (ETF) holds a basket of shares, usually copying an index, and its units trade on the stock exchange all day like a share. What keeps its price close to the value of its holdings is a special arrangement with large institutions called authorised participants.

NET ASSET VALUE (NAV) The value of everything a fund owns, minus what it owes, divided by the number of units (Chapter 16).
AUTHORISED PARTICIPANT A large broker or bank allowed to swap a full basket of the underlying shares for new ETF units, or hand back units for the shares. If the ETF trades above the value of its shares, the participant buys the shares, creates units and sells them — pushing the price down. If it trades below, it does the reverse. Think of a trader who buys rice cheaply in the Terai and sells it in Kathmandu whenever the price gap is bigger than the transport cost, which keeps the two prices close.

Nepal's Position

As of November 2026, no ETF is listed on NEPSE. Nepal's closed-end mutual funds trade on NEPSE but usually below NAV, with no creation mechanism to close the gap; open-ended funds deal with their managers at NAV-linked prices (Chapter 16). ETFs have reached stage 2: the 10-year roadmap lists ETFs; the Ministry of Finance's Capital Market Strengthening and Revival Action Plan, 2083 calls for them; SEBON's Draft Collective Investment Fund Policy, 2083, released for comment on 16 October 2026 (30 Ashwin 2083), proposes developing ETFs, REITs, infrastructure funds and green funds; and the 2 November 2026 (16 Kartik 2083) committee includes ETFs in its mandate. This book has not found a published SEBON rule setting out how Nepali ETFs would work — authorised participants, market-making duties, creation baskets, intraday NAV or limits on tracking error.

Why an ETF Alone Would Not Fix Much

An ETF is only as good as the market beneath it. A NEPSE ETF could still struggle if:

  • The index is lopsided. Banks, financial institutions and hydropower dominate NEPSE, and many companies have small free floats (the shares the public can actually trade, Chapter 11).
  • The underlying shares are hard to trade. If authorised participants cannot buy or sell all the index shares quickly without moving their prices, the price-correcting mechanism breaks, and the ETF trades at a premium or discount like a closed-end fund.
  • No one makes a market. Without well-funded brokers quoting both buying and selling prices, an ETF can become one more thinly traded listed fund.

Whether ETFs would lower fees across the fund industry or attract foreign money is a possibility, not a promise.

The Do-It-Yourself Alternative

Buying "the top 20 or 30 companies by market value" is not the same as owning the index. It overweights companies whose shares are mostly locked up with promoters, and it piles your money into banks. A rules-based personal basket is better — but be honest that it is still your own concentrated portfolio, not an index fund:

  • Free-float weighting: rank companies by the value of shares the public can trade, not by total market value.
  • Liquidity filter: include only shares that trade every day in reasonable amounts (Chapter 11).
  • Sector cap: for example, no more than 30%–35% in banks and financial companies.
  • Basic health screen: exclude companies with negative net worth, repeated regulatory breaches or serious governance problems.
  • Rebalance once a year and adjust for bonus and rights issues, to keep brokerage, SEBON fees, DP charges and capital-gains tax low.

For smaller amounts, a low-cost, well-run mutual fund may be simpler (Chapter 16).

Lesson 18.3 — REITs and InvITs: Current Status and the Obstacles

What They Are

REIT Real Estate Investment Trust: a regulated fund that owns rent-earning property — offices, malls, warehouses, hotels — and sells units that trade on an exchange. It lets you own a small share of a large building, like many families jointly owning a commercial complex in New Road and splitting the rent.
InvIT Infrastructure Investment Trust: the same idea applied to working infrastructure that earns income — hydropower plants, transmission lines, toll roads.

Two cautions. First, distribution rules depend on each country's law. The United States requires qualifying REITs to pay out at least 90% of taxable income; India's SEBI rules use a similar 90% of distributable cash flow; Nepal has no rule yet. Second, a REIT is an equity-type investment in property, not a bond. Its price moves with interest rates, rents, vacancies, tenant defaults, debt costs and property values, and its payouts can be cut. A REIT dividend is not a guaranteed coupon.

YIELD For a REIT, the yearly payout as a percentage of the unit price. A unit at Rs 1,000 paying Rs 80 a year yields 8% — until the payout or the price changes.

Nepal's Position

As of November 2026, Nepal has no REITs or InvITs. They appear in the Draft Collective Investment Fund Policy, 2083 (October 2026) and in the mandate of SEBON's November 2026 instruments committee — stage 2 on the ladder. This book found no published target date for their launch.

What Stands in the Way

  • Law and tax. A listed property trust needs clear legal footing and tax rules that do not tax the same income twice — once at the trust and again in the unit-holder's hands.
  • Valuation. A REIT's NAV depends on honest property valuations. In Nepal, official government valuations of land often differ widely from actual sale prices, and independent valuation standards are still developing.
  • Clean titles. A trust can only own property with clear, undisputed ownership and proper registration.
  • Suitable buildings. REITs work best with large buildings leased to reliable companies on long, formal contracts. Much of Kathmandu's commercial property is small, fragmented and rented on informal terms.
  • Owner willingness. Owners of prime property can already borrow against it or sell privately. Listing means disclosure, outside scrutiny and paying out income.
  • Liquidity. A REIT fixes the illiquidity of property only if its units actually trade — which in Nepal would probably need buyers such as Citizen Investment Trust, the Employees Provident Fund, the Social Security Fund and insurers.

Lesson 18.4 — Commodity Exchanges: The Law, the Licences and the Reality

What a Commodity Exchange Does

A regulated commodity exchange lists standard contracts for gold, silver, farm produce or energy. Its main job is to let businesses fix prices in advance — a tea exporter in Jhapa or a gold dealer in New Road can lock in a price and protect its margin — while speculators take on that price risk in return for possible profit. It also publishes open, reliable prices.

The Law Exists; the Exchange Does Not

Parliament passed the Commodity Exchange Market Act, 2074 (2017), which made SEBON the regulator of commodity exchanges, brokers, clearing houses and warehouses. Under the 2017 regulations, as reported at the time, an exchange needed at least Rs 500 million (Rs 50 crore) of paid-up capital, a warehouse operator Rs 150 million (Rs 15 crore) and a clearing house Rs 70 million (Rs 7 crore).

But a law is not a working market. In 2017, about 15 commodity trading firms were operating under ordinary company registration, after earlier irregularities in the sector, and were told they needed SEBON licences. SEBON invited licence applications for commodity exchanges in September 2022; a court interim order halted the process days later, and it resumed only in April 2023. In July 2026, press coverage of SEBON's new roadmap noted that no commodity exchange licence had been issued in about a decade; the roadmap itself plans futures in gold, silver, tea and coffee through a commodity exchange. SEBON's 2025/26 (2082/83) plan also proposed amending the Act. This book could not verify any licence or launch date for Multi Commodity Exchange Nepal (MCXN) or any other applicant.

Before treating any commodity exchange as real, check six things:

Gold Without an Exchange

Gold is the asset most Nepali families already know — in weddings, festivals and household savings. Today it is bought mostly as jewellery or coins, with wide gaps between buying and selling prices. Gold can protect against inflation and a falling rupee, and it has sometimes held up when shares fell, but its relationship with shares varies from period to period, so do not count on it as a guaranteed hedge. Treat physical gold as a long-term store of value, and size it modestly.

Lesson 18.5 — Short Selling, Margin Trading, Securities Lending: Rules vs Reality

For each of these, ask three separate questions: Is there a rule allowing it? Are brokers ready to offer it? Can you actually open an account and use it today?

Two Kinds of Borrowing to Buy Shares

Bank share loans (margin lending by banks). Banks and financial institutions lend against shares you pledge, under NRB rules (Chapter 2). This market is large: loans against shares stood at about Rs 162.9 billion (Rs 162.9 arba) in mid-June 2026.

Broker margin trading. Here a stockbroker lends you part of the price of shares you buy. Earlier rules from 2074 — including a Rs 50 million (Rs 5 crore) broker net-worth test, a 10,000-shareholder test for eligible companies and a bonus-share record — no longer apply. SEBON's new margin trading directive, 2082 took effect on 1 Falgun 2082 (13 February 2026). As reported, its main terms are:

ItemRule
BrokerAt least Rs 200 million (Rs 20 crore) paid-up capital; clearing membership and, where relevant, depository membership; approval from NEPSE
Broker's lending limitTotal margin loans up to 5 times its certified net worth; no single client and family more than 10% of the total
Eligible sharesAt least 25 lakh public shares (excluding locked-in shares); net worth at least equal to paid-up capital; net profit in at least two of the last three years; listed for at least two years after the IPO
Your depositAt least 30% of the price (initial margin); brokers may ask for more
Keeping the account safeAt least 20% must be maintained (maintenance margin); a margin call follows, and a forced sale if not met (ShareSansar reported a 15% trigger)
OtherAccounts valued daily; separate margin trading and demat accounts; facility for up to one year, renewable
MARGIN CALL A demand from the lender for more cash or shares when your account falls below the maintenance level. If you do not pay, the lender sells your shares — often at the worst moment.
MAINTENANCE MARGIN The minimum share of the account's value that must be your own money. Below it, a margin call is triggered.

Reality check: as Chapter 9 noted, NEPSE had licensed 31 brokers for margin trading by June 2026, with 24 more in line, and the government's September 2026 plan aims to have the system fully working by mid-January 2027. Check whether your own broker actually offers funded margin trading before assuming it does. In August 2026, SEBON also published a draft Margin Lending Regulation for comment, which would let eligible investors combine their own margin with loans from brokers.

Securities Lending and Short Selling

Short selling means borrowing shares, selling them, and hoping to buy them back cheaper later. It needs a way to borrow shares, called securities lending and borrowing (SLB). As of November 2026, neither exists on NEPSE.

But the rules have moved from talk to drafting. Around 31 July 2026 (15 Shrawan 2083), SEBON published for comment a Concept Paper on Policy, Legal, Structural and Technical Arrangements for Margin Lending, Securities Lending and Borrowing, and Covered Short Selling, 2083. It proposes only covered short selling — selling shares you have actually borrowed — not "naked" short selling. As reported, the order would be: securities financing (margin lending) first, securities lending and borrowing second, and covered short selling third, each with its own regulation published for comment before being finalised. A committee of SEBON, NEPSE, CDS and Clearing and the brokers' association is coordinating these secondary-market reforms. This is stage 3: proposals, not permission. Nobody can short a NEPSE share today.

The order matters. You cannot sell a borrowed share until there is a working system to lend shares, hold collateral, call shares back, and pass on dividends, bonus shares and rights to the lender.

Lending income is not guaranteed. In mature markets, long-term holders earn fees by lending shares. How much depends on demand to borrow each share (hard-to-borrow shares earn more; easy-to-borrow large shares can earn almost nothing), how much is actually lent, and how fees are split with brokers or custodians. This book has no basis for estimating what Nepali lenders might earn.

Does the Lack of Short Selling Cause NEPSE's Booms and Busts?

Not as a proven fact. Here the three kinds of statement from the start of this chapter matter most.

The mechanism: without short selling, investors who think a share is overpriced can only stay away or sell what they own. They cannot borrow and sell. This removes one way for bad news to reach prices, and may let overpricing last longer.

The evidence for NEPSE: the rise to 3,198.60 in August 2021 and the fall to about 1,815 by late September 2022 fit the liquidity and credit story of Part 0 much better: NRB tightening, banks hitting lending limits, deposit and loan rates jumping, NRB's cap on share loans (Rs 4 crore per institution and Rs 12 crore across all, in 2021–22), and forced selling by borrowers. A missing instrument cannot be shown to have caused a cycle that other forces explain well.

The other side: short selling does not prevent crashes. In a panic, crowded short selling and margin calls can push prices down faster, which is why markets that allow it often add price rules and circuit breakers.

Leverage does work only in one direction in Nepal today: people can borrow to buy, but not to sell. That can feed rallies when credit is easy and force selling when it tightens — another reason to watch the credit cycle closely.

Rule 1: IPOs Are Not Free Money

Many Nepali investors believe that applying for an IPO at Rs 100 is a near-certain profit. It is not. Listing gains have been common when liquidity was plentiful, but Chapter 14 showed that IPO shares often give back much of their early rise. The risks are real:

  • Listing below the issue price, especially in a weak market or for a weak company — and not all IPOs are at Rs 100; book-built issues are priced higher (Chapter 14).
  • Fading after listing, as early buyers sell and locked-in shares are released.
  • No buyers for small, thinly traded companies.
  • Tiny allotments. With over 8 million demat accounts applying, most winners get a small lot, often 10 shares, so the rupee gain is small compared with your savings.
  • Rule-breaking. Applying through other people's accounts to increase your chances breaks the issue rules and can get accounts frozen.

Treat IPO applications as a small side activity. Decide whether to keep the shares after listing on value, not on the excitement of the allotment.

Rule 2: Dividends Are Not Bonds, and Bonus Shares Are Not Income

Without REITs or a deep bond market, income-seeking investors turn to dividend-paying shares. But a company can cut or stop its dividend at any time. And bonus shares are not cash. If a company gives a 20% bonus, you own 20% more shares, but NEPSE cuts the reference price by the same proportion: adjusted price = price before the book-close ÷ 1.20 (Chapter 15). You are no richer on the day; you gain only if the company later earns more on its larger capital.

Judge a dividend payer with four measures, not the headline yield alone:

  • Cash dividend yield = cash dividend per share ÷ share price. A very high yield can mean the price has collapsed for a good reason.
  • Payout ratio = dividends ÷ profit. For banks, use distributable profit after the reserves NRB requires.
  • Cash-flow coverage: is the dividend paid from real cash generated, not new borrowing?
  • Track record: did the company keep paying through both easy-money and tight-money years?

Retired investors cannot simply swap to bond funds and REITs as they could abroad. That makes debentures and government bonds (Chapter 17) and bank deposits more important parts of an income plan, alongside carefully chosen dividend shares.

Rule 3: Less Research Coverage Is Not Automatically Your Advantage

Few analysts cover most NEPSE companies. It is tempting to compare Nepal with the United States of the 1950s, when Warren Buffett found bargains that others ignored. But a gap in information is not the same as an edge. In Nepal, thin coverage often goes with weak disclosure, hidden related-party dealings, poor protection for minority shareholders and shares that are hard to sell. You have a real edge only when your own checking — annual reports, NRB directives, power purchase agreements, SEBON filings — gives a more accurate value than the market's, by enough to cover trading costs and the risk of being unable to sell.

Rule 4: Be Your Own Risk Manager

With no hedges and no short-side price signals, your safety net is your own discipline:

  • Do not use borrowed money — bank share loans or broker margin — to buy volatile, thinly traded shares, least of all late in a credit boom.
  • Keep a reserve in deposits, treasury bills or other safe, short-term savings, larger when valuations are stretched, so that a falling market becomes a chance to buy rather than a forced sale.
  • Set limits for any single share and sector, and stick to them.

Chapter recap

  • Separate fact, mechanism and cause. An instrument's absence is a fact; how that changes incentives is reasoning; what it caused needs evidence.
  • A plan is not a product. Use the seven stages to judge any announcement.
  • Derivatives, ETFs and REITs are at stage 2. They are named in SEBON's roadmap, a draft fund policy and a new committee, with no launch dates.
  • Commodity exchanges have a law but no licensed exchange. Avoid any unlicensed platform.
  • Broker margin trading is in force but still rolling out under the 2082 directive. Securities lending and covered short selling are proposals.
  • NEPSE's cycles are explained by liquidity and credit (Part 0), not by missing instruments.
  • Your strategy: no "free" IPO money, no confusing bonus shares with income, no assuming that less research coverage means easy bargains — and your own risk controls in place of the hedges Nepal does not yet have.
