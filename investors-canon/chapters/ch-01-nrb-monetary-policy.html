Lesson 1.1 — Why the Central Bank Sits Upstream of Every NEPSE Trade

Every serious NEPSE investor learns, sooner or later, that the index does not move on company profits alone. It also moves on the price and supply of money. In Nepal, both are set directly and deliberately by Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB), the central bank. This is not economics trivia. It is the most important fact to understand before you open a TMS trading account.

Here is why. Banks, development banks, finance companies, microfinance institutions and insurers together made up about 52 percent of NEPSE's market value in March 2026. Hydropower, the other big force on the exchange, is the most indebted non-financial sector in the country, built mostly on long-term bank loans. So when NRB makes money cheaper or dearer, more or less plentiful, it is not turning some distant dial. It is directly changing the value of the companies that make up most of the index — and how much money investors have available to put into shares.

This chapter is the toolbox for the rest of the book. Later chapters ask you to judge a bank's interest income, decide whether a hydropower company can carry its debt, and tell whether a NEPSE rally rests on real profits or just on cheap money. For all of that, you need a working picture of NRB's machinery. The chapter covers:

• how the yearly monetary policy statement is put together;

• how the policy rate and the interest rate corridor work;

• how the cash reserve ratio and the statutory liquidity ratio limit what banks can lend;

• how NRB manages the day-to-day supply of cash in the banking system; and

• how all of this feeds, with a delay, into deposit and loan rates, bank profits and, finally, the prices investors pay for Nepali shares.

KEY CONCEPT NRB is not just a referee standing outside the banking system. Through the policy rate, the cash reserve ratio, the statutory liquidity ratio and its daily cash operations, it largely decides how much money banks have available to lend at any time. That, in turn, decides how much money can flow into NEPSE, into hydropower projects, and into loans for households and businesses.

Lesson 1.2 — How the Annual Monetary Policy Statement Is Built

The Nepal Rastra Bank Act, 2058 (2002) requires NRB to set and announce a monetary policy for every fiscal year. Nepal's fiscal year runs from Shrawan to Asar, roughly mid-July to mid-July, so the policy is announced in July, just before the new year begins. (This book writes fiscal years both ways: FY 2082/83 in the Nepali calendar is FY 2025/26 in the international one.)

The governor presents the statement, but the work behind it is done by NRB's economists and its Monetary Policy Committee. They look at five main inputs:

• Foreign exchange reserves and the balance of payments. Reserves are usually measured in months of import cover — how many months of imports the reserves could pay for.

• Inflation, compared with India's. Because the Nepali rupee is pegged to the Indian rupee (Rs 1.60 to IRs 1), Nepal tends to import India's money conditions.

• How fast remittances are coming in. They fund much of the growth in bank deposits.

• Private-sector credit growth, compared with the growth of the economy.

• The health of the banks, shown by their bad-loan (NPL) ratios and capital adequacy (how much of their own capital they hold against risks).

The statement opens with a few headline numbers: assumed economic growth, an inflation ceiling, and projections for money supply and private-sector credit. Then comes a much longer list of rules: cheap refinancing for chosen sectors, required lending to priority sectors, provisioning rules for particular loan types, margin lending limits for share-backed loans, and limits on lending to single borrowers.

For FY 2025/26 (FY 2082/83), NRB assumed 6 percent growth and a 5 percent inflation ceiling, with money supply projected to grow about 13 percent and private-sector credit about 12 percent. That was a deliberate easing compared with FY 2022/23. Back then, inflation had jumped from about 4.4 percent to 8.6 percent, and a balance-of-payments crisis had pushed import cover dangerously low. So NRB slashed its credit growth ceiling to 12.6 percent, from 19 percent the year before. For FY 2026/27 (FY 2083/84), announced in July 2026, NRB kept all its key rates and reserve ratios unchanged. It backed the government's 7 percent growth target and expected inflation around 5.5 percent. It also eased several lending rules. Housing loan limits went up. Banks may now lend up to 80 percent of value on margin loans for qualifying companies (set out in a directive of 15 July 2026). And margin limits are now linked to each institution's strength.

This history matters for how you read any one year's statement. Since FY 2022/23, NRB's policy has followed a clear arc. Rates were raised sharply in the 2022 external crisis to defend the rupee peg and rebuild reserves. Easing began cautiously in FY 2023/24, with the policy rate cut from 7.0 to 6.5 percent while inflation came down from above 8 percent. Cuts then became bigger through FY 2024/25 and FY 2025/26, as reserves recovered (helped by strong remittances and weak imports) and inflation fell below target. In FY 2026/27 NRB paused.

If you read one July statement on its own, you may mistake "less tight than last year" for "loose". Always compare the new statement with what actually happened in the previous year, not just with last year's targets. NRB also changes course during the year through formal reviews. The first-quarter review usually arrives around Mangsir (November–December) and the mid-term review around Falgun (February–March). For example, the first-quarter review for FY 2025/26 (December 2025) cut the policy rate from 4.50 to 4.25 percent and the bank rate from 6.00 to 5.75 percent.

REGULATORY DETAIL The monetary policy statement is a legal document under the NRB Act. But its headline numbers — growth, inflation, money supply, credit growth — are planning assumptions, not promises NRB must meet. Treat the targets as a signal of which way NRB wants to lean. Track the actual settings — the policy rate, CRR, SLR and refinancing limits — because those are the decisions that move money.

Lesson 1.3 — The Interest Rate Corridor: Policy Rate, Bank Rate, and Deposit Collection Rate

Since 2016, NRB has run an interest rate corridor, with a formal policy rate at its centre since FY 2019/20. It rests on three rates that every NEPSE investor should know by heart. Think of a road with a centre line and two kerbs.

• The centre line is the policy rate (also called the repo rate). It is the benchmark for NRB's main short-term lending operations, and the rate around which short-term market rates should gather.

• The upper kerb is the bank rate — the rate on the Standing Liquidity Facility (SLF). Any bank short of cash can borrow overnight from NRB at this rate against approved security. So no bank should ever need to pay more than this to borrow from another bank.

• The lower kerb is the deposit collection rate — the rate on the Standing Deposit Facility (SDF). Any bank with spare cash can park it overnight with NRB at this rate. So no sensible bank should lend to another for less.

The interbank rate — what banks charge each other for short-term loans — should stay between the kerbs. Where it sits between them is one of the quickest live signals of how much spare cash the banking system has.

The corridor has moved down a long way in the recent easing cycle, as the table shows.

This path is not a technical footnote. It is the clearest number-based picture of NRB's shift from crisis tightening to deliberately encouraging credit, and it lines up with NEPSE's own path. The 2021/22 tightening came with a deep fall in the index, as banks' ability to lend collapsed and margin borrowers were forced to sell. The easing from FY 2023/24 onward brought back spare cash and lower fixed-deposit rates, which made shares more attractive to savers hunting for returns. A broad recovery followed, taking NEPSE just above 3,000 in August 2024 and again in July 2025.

Chapter 0.1 tested this link against NRB data from 2015 to 2026. Of all the readings tested, the interbank rate gave the most useful early warning: a rising interbank rate tended to come about two quarters before market falls. An investor who tracks only company profits and ignores the corridor will keep getting the timing of NEPSE cycles wrong.

CASE IN POINT NRB's quarterly figures show how violently the interbank rate swung in the last cycle. In mid-January 2021, when the system was flooded with cash, it was about 0.1 percent. By mid-April 2022 it was about 7 percent, and by mid-October 2022 it reached 8.5 percent — the very top of that year's corridor. Banks scrambled for overnight money, credit-to-deposit ratios pressed against the ceiling, banks froze new lending including margin loans, and NEPSE fell about 43 percent from its 2021 peak. It is the clearest lesson in this book for why a NEPSE investor must watch interbank conditions, not just company results.

It helps to be precise about what the policy rate actually controls. NRB's main operation is usually a 14-day repo auction (sometimes 7 or 28 days), held at or near this rate, to add cash when the system is short. When the system has too much cash, NRB runs reverse repo or deposit collection auctions to soak it up. So the policy rate is not a price NRB imposes on the whole economy. It is the price at which NRB itself lends to, or borrows from, banks in its routine operations. Everything else — the ceiling, the floor, and through them the interbank rate, treasury bill yields and, in time, banks' base rates and loan rates — is set in relation to it.

Lesson 1.4 — CRR, SLR, and the Plumbing That Constrains Lending Capacity

If the corridor sets the price of money, the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) and Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) decide how much of their deposits banks may lend at all. For a NEPSE investor reading a bank's accounts, these two ratios matter as much as any line in the balance sheet.

The CRR requires every "A", "B" and "C" class bank and financial institution to keep a set share of its deposits at NRB, earning no interest. NRB raised the CRR from 3 to 4 percent going into FY 2022/23 and has kept it at 4 percent since; the July 2026 statement left it unchanged again. Because this money earns nothing, the CRR works like a tax on taking deposits. Every rupee a bank must lock away at NRB is a rupee it cannot lend at interest. A higher CRR shrinks the money available to lend across the whole system, even before interest rates change.

The SLR requires banks to keep a further minimum share of their deposits in safe, easy-to-sell assets — mainly government treasury bills, development bonds and NRB instruments. Unlike the CRR balance, these usually earn a small return. The SLR is 12 percent for commercial ("A" class) banks and 10 percent for development banks and finance companies ("B" and "C" class).

Together, the CRR and SLR keep about 16 percent of a commercial bank's deposits (4 percent plus 12 percent) away from its loan book. And that is before you count capital buffers, provisions or the bank's own wish to keep spare cash. Think of a farmer who must keep part of every harvest as seed and part as a reserve before he can sell the rest. So when you study a bank's earning assets, interest margin or room to grow loans, start here. The money it can lend is never simply "total deposits".

PRACTICAL TOOL To estimate how fast a listed bank can grow its loans next year, do not start from deposit growth alone. First take away the CRR (4 percent) and SLR (12 percent for commercial banks) from expected deposit growth. Then check the bank's credit-to-deposit (CD) ratio against NRB's 90 percent ceiling. A bank already near the ceiling cannot lend much more, however many new deposits arrive.

The credit-to-deposit (CD) ratio ceiling deserves special attention, because in practice it is often the limit that actually bites. NRB says a bank's loans may not exceed 90 percent of its deposits, with some adjustments set out in its directives. The aim is to stop banks lending too much of money that depositors could withdraw. In the 2021/22 credit boom, CD ratios across the system pressed hard against this ceiling. That is why the crisis showed up as a lending freeze, not just higher rates. Many banks were not merely unwilling to lend — they were not allowed to lend more at any rate. By mid-July 2026, commercial banks' CD ratio had fallen to about 73 percent, well below the ceiling. That leaves plenty of room for lending to grow, and helps explain the surplus cash and falling deposit rates of FY 2024/25–2025/26.

WATCH FOR A CD ratio climbing toward the ceiling, even while NRB is cutting rates, is a warning sign. It means rate cuts may not turn into more lending, because the limit has shifted from the price of money (interest rates) to the amount banks are allowed to lend. Always check the CD ratio alongside the policy rate before concluding that "easing" means more credit will flow.

Lesson 1.5 — Open Market Operations, the Standing Liquidity Facility, and Day-to-Day Liquidity Management

The corridor and reserve ratios set the structure. Open market operations are how NRB manages the amount of cash in the banking system from day to day, to keep the interbank rate near the policy rate instead of drifting to either kerb. NRB's tools include:

• repo auctions, where NRB lends cash to banks against government securities to add cash, usually for 14 days;

• reverse repo and deposit collection auctions, where NRB borrows cash from banks to soak up a surplus;

• buying and selling government securities outright; and

• issuing its own bonds, as it did in Poush 2082 (December 2025–January 2026), when it issued Rs 200 billion of one-year NRB bonds purely to absorb spare cash.

Whether NRB is adding or absorbing cash in a given week is a direct, visible signal of money conditions. These operations are reported regularly and publicly. Use them, rather than relying only on policy announcements made a few times a year.

Standing facilities exist because scheduled auctions cannot meet every bank's needs at every moment. A bank caught short today cannot wait for next week's auction. The Standing Liquidity Facility lets any eligible bank borrow overnight from NRB, against approved security, at the bank rate (the ceiling), more or less on demand. It is Nepal's routine back-up lender for everyday cash needs — much less dramatic than a rescue in a real crisis. Its mirror image, the Standing Deposit Facility, lets banks with spare cash park it overnight at the floor rate. In practice the deposit facility has sometimes run only on a limited weekly schedule rather than every business day, which has occasionally made the interbank rate jumpier even inside a working corridor.

Why this matters to you: the interbank rate, published daily, tells you in real time where the system's cash sits within the corridor. It shows whether banks are short of money or flush with it, well before that appears in quarterly bank results. Through FY 2025/26 the interbank rate stayed near 2.7 percent — below the 4.25 percent policy rate and just under the 2.75 percent floor. That is a clear signal. An interbank rate stuck at or below the floor means the system has so much spare cash that NRB is busy soaking it up. It fits the wider picture: plenty of liquid assets in banks and a CD ratio well below its ceiling.

WARNING A low interbank rate sitting near the floor, together with a falling CD ratio, means banks have plenty of cash but not enough good borrowers. Bankers sum it up as "liquidity is not the problem; bankable projects are." In this state, further NRB rate cuts may do little to speed up lending or company profits. They can also squeeze bank margins once loan rates catch up with cheaper deposits (Chapter 0.2 explains the timing). So do not assume every rate cut is good news for bank profits. It depends on whether the limit on lending is the price of money or the demand for loans.

Refinancing facilities also belong in this lesson, because they are one of NRB's most Nepal-specific tools. NRB runs targeted windows that give banks cheap money specifically for re-lending to chosen sectors — agriculture, cottage and small industries, tourism recovery, disaster-hit borrowers and exporters among them. Because these carry below-market rates and limits that NRB changes nearly every year, they let monetary policy steer credit toward particular sectors, not just change its total amount and price. This is one reason why some development banks or finance companies with large priority-sector loan books grow at a different pace from the commercial bank average.

Lesson 1.6 — From NRB's Desk to NEPSE's Ticker: The Transmission Mechanism

Knowing each tool on its own is not enough. The real task is to follow the chain from an NRB decision to a NEPSE price move — and to remember that there is a delay at every link. Think of water released from a dam upstream: it reaches the nearest fields within hours, but the farthest fields only days later.

Link 1: the money market. A policy rate cut, backed by NRB operations that add cash, pulls the interbank rate down toward the new, lower policy rate. It also pulls down short-term yields, most visibly treasury bill rates, which are traded and repriced all the time. This link is fast — usually days to a few weeks.

Link 2: banks' funding costs. As money market rates fall, banks no longer need to offer high fixed-deposit rates to attract savers, and deposit rates across the system start to fall. NRB's easing clearly did this. Average deposit rates were recently reported near 3.5 percent, far below the double-digit rates banks had to offer in the 2022 cash squeeze. This link takes roughly one to two quarters, because existing fixed deposits change rate only when they mature and are renewed.

Link 3: loan rates. Most Nepali bank loans are variable-rate, priced as the bank's base rate plus a premium for the borrower's risk. The base rate is built mainly from the bank's cost of funds — largely its deposit rates — and is updated regularly, usually every quarter. As deposit costs fall, base rates fall, and loan rates follow. Average loan rates were recently reported around 7 percent, down from 12–13 percent or more at the peak of the 2022 tightening. This link runs a step behind deposit rates.

Link 4: company results — the link NEPSE investors care about most.

• Banks: falling rates squeeze a bank's margin if loan rates fall faster than deposit rates, and widen it if deposit rates fall faster. NRB also caps the average gap between lending and deposit rates (around 4 percentage points for commercial banks), so margins cannot swing too far either way. In FY 2025/26, cheaper deposits helped commercial banks' combined net profit rise about 32 percent, to about Rs 69.8 arba.

• Hydropower: most hydropower companies carry debt levels that would look reckless in another sector but are normal for projects that cost a lot and take years to pay back. Falling rates cut their interest costs directly. Profit can rise noticeably with no change in electricity generation or tariffs, which makes hydropower among the most rate-sensitive shares on the exchange.

• Insurers: the picture is mixed. Falling rates lower the return on new investments in government securities and fixed deposits, which are a large part of what insurers hold. But lower rates also lift the wider share market, where insurers hold sizeable portfolios too.

Link 5: money flowing straight into shares. This runs alongside link 4, not after it. As deposit rates fall, savers who parked money in fixed deposits during the high-rate years of 2022–23 have less reason to renew them. Some of that money moves elsewhere — for Nepali households, NEPSE is a leading choice, along with land. Margin lending — loans against pledged shares, within NRB's limits — grows when banks have spare cash and low rates make borrowing to buy shares look cheap. That magnifies whatever price move better profits or good news starts. This is exactly why the fall in interest rates in FY 2024/25–2025/26 came with a broad NEPSE recovery. It is a loop that feeds itself. Recognise it for what it is, rather than mistaking it for a rally driven purely by profits.

KEY CONCEPT Monetary policy reaches NEPSE through two channels that run at different speeds. The slow channel works through profits: over two to four quarters, falling rates can improve bank margins (depending on timing), ease hydropower interest costs and lift company profits generally. The fast channel works through money moving: within weeks, falling deposit rates and cheaper margin loans pull household savings straight into shares. Rallies driven mainly by the fast channel, without support from the slow one, are more fragile and more likely to reverse sharply if money tightens again. NEPSE's falls from just above 3,000 in 2024–25 are a recent example.

Remittances are the background to all of this, and they deserve a direct look, because Nepal's money and banking system depends on them far more than most countries'. Remittances fund a large share of deposit growth. They build the foreign exchange reserves that give NRB room to cut rates — or force it to raise them. And they decide, indirectly, how much new money flows into banks whatever happens to domestic lending. Remittances reached a record of roughly Rs 2,363 billion in FY 2025/26 and kept growing in the first two months of FY 2026/27. By mid-September 2026, reserves covered about 17.8 months of imports of goods and services — more than double the roughly 7 months NRB treats as adequate. That has given NRB the room to ease. Watch NRB's monthly remittance and reserve figures as an early guide to how long that room will last, or how soon NRB may have to reverse course, as it was forced to entering FY 2022/23.

CAUTION — AND IT IS LIVE NOW Because the rupee is pegged to the Indian rupee, NRB cannot set interest rates purely for Nepal's needs. If the Reserve Bank of India tightens while conditions in Nepal would favour easing, NRB comes under pressure to follow, to protect the peg and its reserves. This is no longer a textbook risk. In October 2026 the Reserve Bank of India raised its repo rate from 5.25 to 5.50 percent and said rate cuts were off the table for now. In Nepal, year-on-year inflation had already climbed to 6.3 percent by mid-September 2026, from 1.9 percent a year earlier. NRB's huge reserves give it more room than in 2022, but the direction of pressure has turned. Watch NRB's first-quarter review of FY 2026/27, due around Mangsir, for any change in tone.

Chapter recap

NRB's monetary policy is not background scenery for a NEPSE investor. It is the machinery that decides how much money the banking system has, what it costs and how easily it can flow into shares. The yearly statement, announced each July and revisited in quarterly and mid-term reviews, sets headline assumptions. But the numbers to track are the settings themselves: the policy rate; the bank rate and deposit collection rate that form the corridor; and the CRR and SLR that decide how much of each deposit a bank may lend.

The corridor has moved sharply lower — from a 7.00 percent policy rate in FY 2022/23 to 4.25 percent by December 2025, where NRB held it in July 2026. That path tracks NEPSE's journey from the 2021/22 crash to the recovery since, and the interbank rate inside the corridor is one of the best free early-warning signals a Nepali investor has.

The CRR (4 percent) and SLR (12 percent for commercial banks, 10 percent for development banks and finance companies) keep about a sixth of deposits away from loans. The CD ratio ceiling of 90 percent is often the limit that bites in practice; in mid-July 2026 commercial banks were at about 73 percent, with plenty of room. So check both the price of money (the policy rate) and the amount banks may lend (the CD ratio) before assuming that easing will speed up lending and profits.

Monetary policy reaches NEPSE through a slow channel (profits) and a fast channel (money moving into shares). Rallies built mainly on the fast channel are the most likely to reverse. And because the rupee is pegged to the Indian rupee and Nepal depends on remittances, NRB's room to ease never depends on Nepal alone. With India's central bank now raising rates and Nepal's inflation above 6 percent, the next turn in the rate cycle is worth watching closely.
