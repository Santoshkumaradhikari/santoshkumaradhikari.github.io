Lesson 1.1 — Why the Central Bank Sits Upstream of Every NEPSE Trade

Every serious NEPSE investor learns, sooner or later, that the index does not move on company profits alone. It moves on the price and supply of money — and in Nepal, both are set directly and deliberately by Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB), the central bank. This is not a piece of economics trivia. It is the most important fact to understand before you open a TMS trading account. NEPSE is dominated by banks and financial institutions: commercial banks, development banks, finance companies, microfinance institutions and insurers together make up most of the market's value and daily trading. Hydropower, the other big force on the exchange, is the most indebted non-financial sector in the country, built mostly on long-term bank loans. So when NRB makes credit cheaper or dearer, more or less plentiful, it is not turning some abstract dial. It is directly changing the value of the companies that make up most of the index — and how much money investors have to put into shares.

This chapter lays the groundwork for the rest of the book. Before you can judge a bank's interest income, decide whether a hydropower company can carry its debt, or tell whether a NEPSE rally is driven by real profits or just by cheap money, you need a working picture of how NRB's policy machinery works. This chapter covers: how the yearly monetary policy statement is put together; how the policy rate and the interest rate corridor work; how the cash reserve ratio and statutory liquidity ratio limit what banks can lend; how open market operations and the standing liquidity facility manage day-to-day cash; and how all of this feeds, with a delay, into deposit and loan rates, bank profits and, finally, the price-to-earnings (P/E) multiple the market will pay for Nepali shares.

KEY CONCEPT NRB does not merely regulate the banking system from the outside — through the policy rate, CRR, SLR, and open market operations, it mechanically determines how much lendable liquidity exists in the banking system at any given time, which in turn governs how much capital is available to flow into NEPSE, into hydropower project financing, and into consumer and corporate credit generally.

Lesson 1.2 — How the Annual Monetary Policy Statement Is Built

The Nepal Rastra Bank Act, 2058 (2002) requires NRB to set and announce a monetary policy for every fiscal year. By long habit, it is announced in July, around the start of the Nepali fiscal year in Shrawan — recent statements have come in early-to-mid July, days before the new year begins. The governor presents it, but the work behind it is done by NRB's Monetary Policy and Research departments with the Monetary Policy Committee. They look at a handful of core inputs: the balance of payments and foreign exchange reserves (usually measured in months of import cover — how many months of imports the reserves could pay for); inflation compared with India's (because the rupee is pegged to the Indian rupee, Nepal tends to import India's monetary conditions); how fast remittances are coming in (they fund much of the growth in bank deposits); private-sector credit growth compared with the growth of the economy; and the health of the banks, shown by bad-loan (NPL) ratios and capital adequacy.

The statement opens with a few headline targets: an assumed real GDP growth rate, an inflation ceiling, a projection for broad money supply (M2) and a projection for private-sector credit growth. Then comes a much longer list of rules: limits on NRB's refinancing for productive sectors, required lending to priority sectors, provisioning rules for particular loan types, margin lending limits for share-backed loans, and changes to lending limits for single borrowers. For FY 2025/26, NRB assumed 6 percent growth and a 5 percent inflation ceiling, with money supply projected to grow about 13 percent and private-sector credit about 12 percent. That was a deliberate easing compared with FY 2022/23. Back then, the growth target was 8 percent but the credit growth ceiling had been slashed to 12.6 percent (from 19 percent the year before), because inflation had jumped from about 4.4 percent to 8.6 percent and a balance-of-payments crisis had pushed import cover dangerously low.

This history matters for how you read any one year's statement. Since FY 2022/23, NRB's policy has followed a clear arc. Rates were raised sharply in the 2022 external crisis to defend the rupee peg and rebuild reserves. They were held high through FY 2023/24 while inflation came down from above 8 percent toward the mid-single digits. Then they were cut step by step through FY 2024/25 and FY 2025/26, as reserves recovered (helped by strong remittances and weak imports) and inflation fell toward target. If you read one July statement on its own, without this background, you may mistake "less tight than last year" for "loose". Always compare the new statement with what actually happened in the previous year, not just with last year's targets. Remember too that NRB often changes course during the year through formal reviews: the first-quarter review usually arrives around Mangsir (November–December) and the half-yearly review around Magh–Falgun (January–February). For example, NRB's first-quarter review for FY 2082/83 (December 2025) cut the policy rate from 4.50 to 4.25 percent and the standing liquidity facility rate from 6.00 to 5.75 percent.

REGULATORY DETAIL The monetary policy statement is a legal instrument under the NRB Act, but its numerical targets — GDP growth, inflation ceiling, M2 growth, credit growth — are policy assumptions used to calibrate instruments, not binding commitments NRB is obligated to hit. Investors should treat the targets as a signal of intended stance, and the instrument settings (policy rate, CRR, SLR, refinancing ceilings) as the actual operative decisions to track.

Lesson 1.3 — The Interest Rate Corridor: Policy Rate, Bank Rate, and Deposit Collection Rate

Since 2016, NRB has run an Interest Rate Corridor (IRC) system (with a formal policy rate at its centre since FY 2019/20). It replaced a looser older framework with three clear rates that every NEPSE investor should know by heart. Think of it as a road with a centre line and two kerbs. The centre line is the policy rate (also called the repo rate): the benchmark for NRB's main 14-day lending operations, around which short-term market rates should gather. The upper kerb is the bank rate — the rate on the Standing Liquidity Facility (SLF). Any bank short of cash can borrow overnight from NRB at this rate against approved security, so no bank should ever need to pay more than this to borrow from another bank. The lower kerb is the deposit collection rate — the rate on the Standing Deposit Facility (SDF). Any bank with spare cash can park it overnight with NRB at this rate, so no sensible bank should lend to another for less. The interbank rate — what banks charge each other for short-term loans — should stay between the kerbs, and where it sits within them is one of the quickest live signals of how much spare cash the banking system has.

The corridor has moved down a long way in the recent easing cycle. In FY 2022/23 the policy rate was 7.00 percent, with the bank rate at 8.50 percent and the deposit collection rate at 5.50 percent — a wide corridor, reflecting both tight policy and a system still learning to manage cash within a narrow band. By FY 2023/24 the policy rate had eased to 6.50 percent, the bank rate to 7.50 percent and the deposit rate to 4.50 percent. FY 2024/25 brought them down to 5.00, 6.50 and 3.00 percent. The FY 2025/26 statement cut further, to 4.50, 6.00 and 2.75 percent. Then the first-quarter review (December 2025) narrowed the corridor again: policy rate 4.25 percent, bank rate (SLF) 5.75 percent, with the deposit floor held at 2.75 percent.

This path is not a technical footnote. It is the clearest number-based picture of NRB's shift from post-crisis tightening to deliberately stimulating credit — and it lines up closely with NEPSE's own multi-year path. The 2021/22 tightening came with a deep fall in the index, as banks' ability to lend collapsed and margin borrowers were forced to sell. The easing from FY 2023/24 onward came with a gradual return of liquidity, falling fixed deposit rates (which made shares more attractive to savers hunting for returns), and a broad recovery in bank, hydropower and insurance shares. An investor who tracks only earnings and ignores the corridor will keep getting the timing of NEPSE cycles wrong.

CASE IN POINT During the 2021/22 liquidity crisis, the interbank rate spiked well outside any orderly band — reported at points to have swung between roughly 0.2 percent and 8.5 percent before the corridor system was fully implemented — as banks scrambled for overnight funds. Credit-to-deposit ratios pushed against regulatory ceilings, banks froze new lending including margin loans against shares, and NEPSE fell sharply from its 2021 highs. The episode is the clearest object lesson in this book for why a NEPSE investor must monitor interbank liquidity conditions, not just corporate results.

It helps to be precise about what the policy rate actually controls. NRB's main open market operation is usually a 14-day repo auction (sometimes 7 or 28 days), held at or near this rate to add cash when the system is short. When the system has too much cash, NRB runs reverse repo or deposit collection auctions to soak it up. So the policy rate is not a price NRB imposes on the whole economy. It is the price at which NRB itself will lend to, or borrow from, banks in its routine operations. Everything else — the SLF ceiling, the SDF floor, and through them the interbank rate, treasury bill yields and in time banks' base rates and loan rates — is set in relation to it.

Lesson 1.4 — CRR, SLR, and the Plumbing That Constrains Lending Capacity

If the corridor sets the price of money, the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) and Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) decide how much of their deposits banks may lend at all. For a NEPSE investor reading a bank's accounts, these two ratios matter as much as any line in the balance sheet.

The CRR requires every "A", "B" and "C" class bank and financial institution to keep a set percentage of its deposits at NRB, earning no interest. NRB raised the CRR from 3 to 4 percent as part of its tightening going into FY 2022/23, and has kept it at 4 percent through the easing since — the FY 2025/26 statement and its first-quarter review both kept "existing arrangements" for the CRR unchanged. Because this money earns nothing, the CRR works like a tax on taking deposits. Every rupee a bank must lock away at NRB is a rupee it cannot lend at interest. A higher CRR shrinks the money available to lend across the whole system, even before interest rates change.

The SLR requires banks to keep a further minimum share of their deposits and borrowings in safe, easy-to-sell assets — mainly government treasury bills, development bonds and NRB instruments. Unlike the CRR balance, these usually earn a small return. The SLR has been 12 percent for commercial (Class A) banks and 10 percent for development banks and finance companies in recent years. NRB judges that smaller lenders need a slightly lighter mandatory cushion. In practice, it also means commercial banks — which hold most of the system's deposits — keep a bigger share of their balance sheets in low-yield government paper.

Together, the CRR and SLR keep about 16 percent of a commercial bank's deposits (4 percent CRR plus 12 percent SLR) away from its loan book, before you even count capital buffers, provisions or the bank's own appetite for spare cash. Think of a farmer who must keep part of every harvest as seed and part as a reserve before he can sell the rest. So when you model a bank's earning assets, interest margin or room to grow loans, start here: the money it can lend is never simply "total deposits". It is deposits minus the CRR, minus the SLR, minus whatever extra cushion the bank chooses to keep.

PRACTICAL TOOL When estimating a listed bank's realistic loan growth capacity for the year ahead, do not start from deposit growth alone. Subtract the CRR (currently 4 percent) and SLR (12 percent for commercial banks) from projected deposit growth to estimate the maximum loanable increment, then cross-check against the bank's credit-to-deposit (CD) ratio relative to NRB's regulatory ceiling — commonly cited at 90 percent — since a bank already near the ceiling cannot expand lending materially even with ample fresh deposits.

The credit-to-deposit (CD) ratio ceiling deserves special attention, because in practice it is often the limit that actually binds, rather than the CRR or SLR. NRB caps the share of core deposits (plus certain qualifying borrowings) that a bank may lend, at about 90 percent. The aim is to stop banks over-lending against deposits that could leave, and to keep a cushion for withdrawals. In the 2021/22 credit boom, CD ratios across the system pressed hard against this ceiling. That is why the crisis showed up as a lending freeze, not just higher rates: many banks were not merely reluctant to lend — they were legally unable to lend more at any rate. In recent reports, the system-wide CD ratio has eased into the low-to-mid 70s percent, well below the 90 percent ceiling. That leaves plenty of room for lending to grow, and helps explain the surplus cash and falling deposit rates of FY 2024/25–2025/26.

WATCH FOR A rising CD ratio approaching the regulatory ceiling, even amid falling policy rates, is a warning sign that headline rate cuts may not translate into actual credit growth — the binding constraint has simply shifted from price (interest rates) to quantity (regulatory lending capacity). Always check the CD ratio alongside the policy rate before concluding that "easing" automatically means more credit will flow.

Lesson 1.5 — Open Market Operations, the Standing Liquidity Facility, and Day-to-Day Liquidity Management

The corridor and reserve ratios set the structure. Open market operations (OMOs) are how NRB manages the amount of cash in the banking system from day to day, to keep the interbank rate near the policy rate rather than drifting to either kerb. NRB's OMO tools include: repo auctions, where NRB lends cash to banks against government securities to add liquidity, usually for 14 days; reverse repo and deposit collection auctions, where NRB borrows cash from banks to soak up a surplus; outright buying and selling of government securities; and issuing its own instruments. Whether NRB is adding or absorbing cash in a given week is a direct, visible signal of liquidity conditions. These operations are reported regularly and publicly. Investors should use them, rather than relying only on policy rate announcements made once or twice a year.

Standing facilities exist because scheduled auctions cannot meet every bank's needs at every moment — a bank caught short today cannot wait for next week's auction. The Standing Liquidity Facility lets any eligible bank borrow overnight from NRB, against approved security, at the bank rate (the corridor ceiling), more or less on demand. It is Nepal's routine lender-of-last-resort for everyday cash needs (different from, and much less dramatic than, a rescue in a real solvency crisis). Its mirror image, the Standing Deposit Facility, lets banks with spare cash park it overnight at the deposit collection rate (the floor). In practice, though, the SDF has sometimes run only on a limited weekly schedule (reported as about three days a week) rather than every business day — a quirk that has occasionally made the interbank rate jumpier even inside a working corridor.

Why this matters to you: the interbank rate, published daily, tells you in real time where the system's cash sits within the corridor. It shows whether banks are short of money or flush with it — well before that appears in quarterly bank results or NRB's periodic reports. In recent data, the interbank rate has been near 2.70 percent: below the 4.25 percent policy rate, and just under the 2.75 percent SDF floor. That is a clear signal. An interbank rate stuck at or below the floor, instead of near the policy rate, means the system has so much spare cash that NRB is actively soaking it up through bond sales and other operations. It fits the wider picture: ample liquidity (banks' net liquid assets have recently been reported in the high 30s as a percentage of deposits) and a CD ratio well below its ceiling.

WARNING A persistently low interbank rate sitting near the corridor floor, alongside a falling CD ratio, signals that banks are liquidity-rich but loan-demand-constrained — a condition sometimes described locally as "liquidity is not the problem, bankable projects are." In this state, further policy rate cuts by NRB may do little to accelerate credit growth or corporate earnings, even though they mechanically compress bank net interest margins by narrowing the spread between lending and deposit rates. Investors should not assume every rate cut is unambiguously bullish for bank earnings — it depends on whether the constraint is price or volume.

Refinancing facilities also belong in this lesson, because they are one of NRB's most Nepal-specific tools, and they matter for NEPSE. Beyond the corridor and OMOs, NRB runs targeted refinancing windows: cheap loans to banks, specifically for re-lending to chosen priority sectors — agriculture, cottage and small industries, tourism recovery, earthquake- and disaster-hit borrowers, and exporters among them. Because these carry below-market rates and limits that NRB changes nearly every year, they let monetary policy steer credit toward particular sectors, not just change its total amount and price. This explains, for example, why some development banks or finance companies with large priority-sector books show loan growth that differs from the commercial bank average.

Lesson 1.6 — From NRB's Desk to NEPSE's Ticker: The Transmission Mechanism

Knowing each tool on its own is not enough. The real task is to follow the chain from an NRB decision to a NEPSE price move — and to remember that there is a delay at every link.

The first link is the money market. A policy rate cut, reinforced by liquidity-injecting OMOs, pulls the interbank rate down toward the new, lower policy rate, and correspondingly pulls down short-term instrument yields — treasury bill rates most visibly, since T-bills are actively traded and repriced continuously. This link is fast, typically showing up within days to a few weeks.

The second link is banks' funding costs. As money market rates fall and NRB's deposit collection (SDF) rate comes down, banks no longer need to offer high fixed deposit rates to attract savers, and deposit rates across the system start to fall. NRB's easing has clearly done this: average deposit rates were recently reported near 3.5 percent, far below the double-digit rates banks had to offer in the 2022 cash squeeze to keep depositors. This link takes roughly one to two quarters, because existing fixed deposits change rate only when they mature and are renewed.

The third link is loan rates. Nepali banks price loans using a base rate method set by NRB. The base rate is built mainly from the bank's cost of funds — largely its deposit rates — plus a margin. As deposit costs fall, base rates fall, and average loan rates follow. They were recently reported around 7.0 percent across the system, down sharply from 12–13 percent or more at the peak of the 2022 tightening. This link takes another one to two quarters after deposit rates, because floating-rate loans usually reset on a fixed schedule — often every six months — not continuously.

The fourth link — the one NEPSE investors care about most — is where all this shows up in company results. For banks, falling rates squeeze their margin if loan rates fall faster than deposit rates, and widen it if deposit rates fall faster. Getting that order right, quarter by quarter, is a key skill this book returns to in the chapters on bank accounts. For hydropower companies, most of which carry debt levels that would look reckless in any other sector but are normal given how costly and slow to pay back these projects are, falling rates cut interest costs directly. Profit can rise noticeably even with no change in generation or power-purchase tariffs, which makes hydropower among the most rate-sensitive shares on the exchange. For insurers, especially life insurers with long-term promises to policyholders, the picture is mixed. Falling rates lower the return on new investments in government securities and fixed deposits — a large part of what insurers hold — which can squeeze investment income. But lower rates also lift the wider share market, where insurers hold sizeable portfolios too.

The fifth and last link is money flowing straight into the share market. It runs alongside the earnings link above, not strictly after it. As deposit rates fall, savers who parked money in fixed deposits during the high-rate years of 2022–23 have less reason to keep renewing them. Some of that money moves to other places — for Nepali households, NEPSE is a leading choice, along with land. Margin lending — loans against pledged shares, subject to NRB's loan-to-value and borrower limits, which it changes almost every year — grows when banks have spare cash and low rates make borrowing to buy shares look cheap. That amplifies whatever price move good earnings or economic news starts. This is exactly why the fall in interest rates in FY 2024/25–2025/26 came with a broad NEPSE recovery. Lower deposit rates pushed savers toward shares at the same time as cheap, plentiful bank money made margin loans easier to get. It is a self-reinforcing loop. Recognise it for what it is, rather than mistaking it for a rally driven purely by profits.

KEY CONCEPT Monetary transmission into NEPSE runs through two parallel channels operating on different timelines: a slower fundamentals channel (falling rates improving bank margins conditionally, hydropower debt servicing, and corporate earnings generally, over two to four quarters) and a faster liquidity-rotation channel (falling deposit rates and cheaper margin financing pulling household savings directly into equities, often within weeks of a rate cut). NEPSE rallies driven predominantly by the second channel, without confirmation from the first, are structurally more fragile and prone to sharp reversal if liquidity conditions tighten again.

Remittances are the background to all of this, and they deserve a direct look, because Nepal's money and banking system depends on them far more than most countries. Remittances fund a large share of deposit growth. They support the foreign exchange reserves that give NRB room to cut rates — or force it to raise them. And they decide, indirectly, how much new money flows into banks regardless of domestic lending. Recent years have seen strong remittance growth and comfortable reserves (reported above USD 23 billion through 2026, comfortably above the roughly 7 months of import cover NRB itself treats as adequate). That has given NRB the room to keep easing. So watch NRB's monthly remittance and reserve figures — not as background trivia, but as an early guide to how long NRB can keep easing, or how soon it may have to reverse, as it was forced to entering FY 2022/23.

CAUTION Nepal's currency peg to the Indian rupee means NRB's monetary policy independence is structurally limited: if the Reserve Bank of India tightens meaningfully while NRB's own domestic conditions would otherwise argue for continued easing, NRB faces pressure to follow India's rate direction to defend the peg and prevent reserve drawdowns, regardless of what domestic credit growth or NEPSE conditions might prefer. Any investor building a multi-quarter view of Nepali interest rates should track Indian monetary policy alongside NRB's own statements, not in isolation.

Chapter recap

NRB's monetary policy is not background scenery for a NEPSE investor. It is the machinery that decides how much money the banking system has, what it costs and how easily it can flow into shares. Skip this layer and you are trading with half a picture of what drives the market. The yearly statement, announced each July and revisited in quarterly and half-yearly reviews, sets headline assumptions for growth, inflation, money supply and credit. But the numbers to track quarter by quarter are the settings themselves: the policy rate; the bank rate and deposit collection rate that form the corridor; and the CRR and SLR that decide how much of each deposit a bank may lend.

The interest rate corridor, in place since 2016, has moved sharply lower through the recent easing cycle — from a 7.00 percent policy rate and an 8.50/5.50 percent corridor in FY 2022/23, down to a 4.25 percent policy rate and a 5.75/2.75 percent corridor by the FY 2025/26 first-quarter review (December 2025). That path tracks NEPSE's own journey from the 2021/22 correction to the recovery since, which makes the corridor one of the most useful pieces of free public data a Nepali investor has.

The CRR (held at 4 percent) and SLR (12 percent for commercial banks, 10 percent for development banks and finance companies) keep about a sixth of deposits away from loans, whatever the interest rate. The CD ratio ceiling, near 90 percent, is often the limit that bites in practice. So check both the price signal (the policy rate) and the quantity signals (the CD ratio and the liquidity-to-deposit ratio) before assuming that a given policy stance will actually speed up lending and profits.

Open market operations and the two standing facilities are the daily plumbing that keeps the interbank rate inside the corridor. The interbank rate itself — published daily and recently sitting near the corridor floor, around 2.75 percent — is one of the fastest and most under-used early signals of how much cash the banking system has, well ahead of quarterly bank results.

Monetary policy reaches NEPSE through two channels that run at different speeds. The slow one works through fundamentals: over several quarters, it can improve bank margins, ease hydropower interest burdens and lift company profits generally. The fast one works through money moving: within weeks, lower deposit rates and cheaper margin loans pull household savings straight into shares. Rallies built mainly on the fast channel, without support from the slow one, tend to be the most fragile and the most likely to reverse.

Because the rupee is pegged to the Indian rupee, and Nepal depends heavily on remittances for deposit growth and reserves, NRB's room to ease — or need to tighten — never depends on Nepal alone. A disciplined investor watches Indian monetary policy, Nepal's remittance and reserve data, and NRB's own statements together, because a change in any one of them can force a turn in the rate cycle that domestic profits alone would not have predicted.
