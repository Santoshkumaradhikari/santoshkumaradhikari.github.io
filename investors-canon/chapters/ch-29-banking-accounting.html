WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Net interest margin (NIM) — net interest income divided by the loans and investments that earn interest; a bank's core earning power. Non-performing loan (NPL) — a loan whose repayments are more than three months overdue. Interest suspense — interest due on a bad loan that the bank may not count as income until it is actually paid. Loan loss provision — money set aside against loans that may not be repaid, like keeping some grain aside in case of a poor harvest. Capital adequacy ratio (CAR) — a bank's own capital as a share of its risk-weighted loans; its cushion against losses. Tier 1 capital — the strongest capital: paid-up shares and retained profits.

On the hill road to Surkhet, the branch manager of a small development bank once told visiting NRB inspectors that his loan book was "clean" — every account current, every file complete. Nineteen months later, 40.85 percent of that institution's loans were non-performing, its capital had gone, and Nepal Rastra Bank used Section 86(B) of the NRB Act to take management away from its own board.

Nothing about that collapse was sudden. It was written, quarter after quarter, in provisioning tables, interest suspense accounts and a capital ratio sliding towards a floor nobody wanted to look at. This chapter teaches you to read those tables before the inspectors have to.

Lesson 29.1 — The Architecture of a Nepali Bank's Financial Statements

A bank buys and sells money

A bank's annual report is not built like a factory's. There is no cost of goods sold, no stock turnover, no gross margin in the usual sense. A bank buys money (deposits) and sells money (loans). Its income comes from the spread between the two, on top of a balance sheet made almost entirely of other people's money — like a grain trader who earns the difference between the price paid to farmers and the price charged in town.

The balance sheet

Liabilities, in the format set by NRB's Directive on Format of Financial Statements:

  • Deposits — current, savings, call and fixed deposits — by far the largest line, broken down by type, maturity and currency;
  • Borrowings — NRB refinance, interbank loans and sometimes bonds or debentures;
  • Other liabilities;
  • Equity — share capital, share premium, retained earnings, and reserves that exist because NRB requires them.

Assets:

  • Loans and advances to customers — the largest and most important line, always shown after deducting impairment (the provision this chapter teaches you to question);
  • Investments — government securities, NRB bonds, corporate debentures, and increasingly mutual fund units;
  • Cash and balances with banks, and fixed and other assets.

The income statement

It follows the same logic, from top to bottom:

  1. interest income (from loans, investments and interbank lending);
  2. minus interest expense (on deposits and borrowings) = net interest income — the most important line of all;
  3. fee and commission income (loan fees, letter-of-credit and guarantee commissions, remittance fees — "non-funded" income);
  4. trading and other operating income;
  5. the impairment charge for loans and other assets;
  6. staff and operating costs;
  7. profit before and after tax.
KEY CONCEPT Net interest margin (NIM) — net interest income divided by average interest-earning assets — best sums up a bank's core spread business. A bank can look profitable at the bottom line while its NIM quietly shrinks, if fees or one-off trading gains prop up the total. Judge NIM separately from non-funded income before deciding how good the earnings are.

Every later lesson — provisions, capital, recognising income — is really about how honestly these two statements show reality. A bank's profit is, more than in almost any other sector, a policy choice as well as a fact: how strictly management classified loans, how much interest it counted on stressed accounts, how cautiously it provided under NFRS 9 compared with the regulatory floor. Reading a bank means seeing the choices behind the numbers.

The deposit mix

Banks disclose how much of their deposits are fixed deposits and how much are current and savings accounts (CASA). Fixed deposits cost more and leave more easily. Across the sector, fixed deposits were about 48 percent of total funding in mid-August 2025.

A bank that depends heavily on fixed deposits pays more for its money and is exposed to the rate wars that break out when liquidity tightens, as banks outbid each other on FD rates. A bank with a strong CASA base has cheaper, stickier money and, all else equal, a higher NIM — like a shop with loyal regular customers rather than one that must offer discounts every week.

Lesson 29.2 — Interest Income Recognition and the Interest Suspense Account

When to stop counting interest

The most important accounting judgement a Nepali bank makes each quarter is whether to keep counting interest on a loan as income, or to stop and park it in an interest suspense account. Get this wrong — on purpose or through weak systems — and a bank can report profit that does not exist.

  • NRB's Guideline on Recognition of Interest Income, 2019 told banks to stop counting interest as income once a loan became non-performing (Substandard, Doubtful or Loss — overdue more than three months). The interest due is parked in suspense and counted only when cash actually arrives.
  • A Guidance Note on Interest Income Recognition, issued in July 2025 and applied from FY 2081/82, replaced those rule-based tests with an account-by-account system tied to each loan's NFRS 9 expected-credit-loss "stage" at the end of the previous quarter. Stage 1 and Stage 2 loans (broadly Pass, Watchlist, and restructured loans still being repaid) keep counting interest at the loan rate. Stage 3 loans (non-performing) count only interest actually received in cash. From FY 2083/84, Stage 3 interest moves to an "effective interest" method on the loan's value after expected losses.
REGULATORY DETAIL Once a loan is non-performing, interest already booked on it must be reversed out of interest receivable and profit, moved to interest suspense, and counted as income only when received in cash. This is a cash rule laid on top of an otherwise accrual-based income statement — one of the clearest cases in NEPSE accounting of the regulator overriding normal reporting practice.

A practical cross-check

Compare three growth rates from the notes: reported interest income, the interest suspense balance, and the non-performing loan book. If bad loans are rising but suspense is flat or falling while interest income still grows briskly, that is a red flag. Loans that should have become non-performing are probably being kept "current" through evergreening or restructuring. The notes, not the headline income statement, settle this.

Evergreening and the Watchlist

Evergreening means rolling over or refinancing a struggling borrower's loan just before it would cross a classification line, resetting the overdue clock. NRB's Watchlist class was added partly to catch this. Banks must flag loans where the borrower has had negative cash flow for three years in a row, or where quality is worsening even without a formal overdue event. That closes some of the gap, though not all.

Staff bonus

One more item: the staff bonus under the Bonus Act is a percentage of profit before bonus and tax, expensed above the tax line. It is small but easy to miss when comparing bank margins with other companies.

Lesson 29.3 — Loan Classification and Provisioning Mechanics

The load-bearing wall

Loan classification is the load-bearing wall of Nepali bank accounting. NRB's Unified Directives — the directive on loan classification and loss provisioning, usually Directive No. 2 in the yearly reissued set — sort loans into five classes, mainly by how many days a payment is overdue, plus some qualitative warning signs.

KEY CONCEPT In Nepal, classifying a loan is mainly a matter of counting overdue days, not management's judgement. That is deliberate: it stops management keeping a worsening loan "current" with optimistic labels. So the overdue bucket a loan sits in is the most important number you can take from a bank's loan disclosures.
ClassOverdue period or triggerMinimum provision
PassCurrent, or overdue up to 1 month1%
WatchlistOverdue 1–3 months; or negative cash flow for 3 years in a row; or other signs of stress5%
Substandard (non-performing)Overdue 3–6 months25%
Doubtful (non-performing)Overdue 6–12 months50%
Loss (non-performing)Overdue more than 12 months100%

Think of it as a farmer keeping back grain: a little for every field, more for fields that look dry, and the whole harvest's worth for a field that has failed.

Restructured loans

Restructured and rescheduled loans sit in a special group, provided for at rates that depend on why they were restructured and what class they came from — from about 12.5 percent up to the full rate of the class they would otherwise fall into.

This matters a great deal. A bank under pressure has every reason to restructure a weak loan rather than let it slide to Substandard or Doubtful, because restructuring can — if nobody checks — reset both the overdue clock and the provision.

WARNING A rising share of "restructured and rescheduled" loans — especially growing faster than the whole loan book — is one of the most reliable early signs of a hidden loan-quality problem. Restructuring is a fair tool for a sound borrower in temporary trouble. It is also the easiest lever for a bank that does not want to report a loss yet.

Two features to remember

  1. Provisions are set by bucket, not loan by loan. The rate depends on the class, not on a detailed estimate of what each loan will recover. That is why it is called a "regulatory" or "prudential" system, not an economic-loss system.
  2. Even good loans carry a provision. Every Pass loan needs a general provision — now 1 percent, after NRB raised it to 1.25 percent and then cut it in steps to 1.20, 1.10 and 1.0 percent by March 2025. So provision coverage in Nepal always has a floor that has nothing to do with credit quality.

What to do with it

Take the classification breakdown from the notes (every NRB-format report has it), work out the share of the book in each class, and follow the movement between classes quarter by quarter. A bank whose Watchlist is swelling while Pass shrinks — even if the non-performing classes look steady — is showing you where next year's bad loans will come from.

PRACTICAL TOOL Build a simple migration table from a bank's quarterly disclosures: the rupee amount in each class over four to eight quarters. A bank managing its book honestly shows gradual, explainable shifts. A bank managing its story shows sudden, unexplained jumps in Pass or Watchlist just before a reporting date, often reversing soon after — a sign of restructuring or evergreening timed to the calendar.

Lesson 29.4 — NFRS 9 Expected Credit Loss and the Regulatory Reserve Bridge

A second, forward-looking system

Since adopting Nepal Financial Reporting Standards, banks must also work out loan impairment under NFRS 9's expected credit loss (ECL) model in their audited accounts. It runs alongside the classification system, not instead of it, and uses three stages:

  • Stage 1: no significant rise in credit risk since the loan was made. Provide for the losses expected in the next 12 months.
  • Stage 2: a significant rise in credit risk, even if the loan is still technically performing. Provide for the losses expected over the loan's whole remaining life.
  • Stage 3: credit-impaired loans (broadly, though not exactly, the non-performing classes). Also lifetime losses, on a loan the bank accepts is impaired.

ECL looks forward and weighs probabilities: economic scenarios, past loss experience and each borrower's risk. NRB's NFRS 9 Expected Credit Loss Related Guidelines (2024, later amended) set standard methods for the probability of default, loss given default and economic adjustments, so that each bank's estimate is not just its own unchecked judgement.

KEY CONCEPT Nepal runs two provisioning systems on the same loan book: NRB's classification provisions (Lesson 29.3) — mechanical, cautious and required for regulatory reporting — and NFRS 9 ECL — model-based, forward-looking and used in the audited accounts. They almost never give the same number, so NRB built a bridge between them.

The bridge: the regulatory reserve

If a bank's NFRS 9 impairment is lower than NRB's classification provision, the shortfall must be moved each year out of distributable retained earnings into a regulatory reserve inside equity. That reserve cannot be paid as dividends. It stops a bank using a gentler model to report and distribute profit that NRB says has not really been earned. If NFRS 9 gives the higher figure, no transfer is needed — the more cautious number stands.

REGULATORY DETAIL The regulatory reserve transfer goes through the statement of changes in equity, not the profit and loss account. So it does not lower reported profit, but it does reduce the retained earnings available for dividends. A bank can report healthy profit while a large part of it is locked in the regulatory reserve. Check the reserve movement before assuming strong profit means strong dividend capacity.

The transition relief

Moving fully to NFRS 9 ECL could have given banks with under-provided books a sudden capital shock. So NRB allowed a four-year transition, from FY 2081/82 to FY 2084/85. During it, banks get relief on their core equity (CET1) capital against the first-day impact of ECL — about 80 percent relief in the first year, falling to 20 percent by the last — before the full effect hits capital.

This relief is shown in the capital notes. A bank that looks well capitalised today, with the relief, may look much tighter when it runs out.

CAUTION When comparing a bank's capital ratio over recent years, look in the capital notes for NFRS 9 transition relief. A ratio that seems steady while the relief percentage is falling is not really steady — it is being held up by a shrinking concession. The true trend appears only when you strip the concession out.

Lesson 29.5 — Capital Adequacy: The Ratio That Decides Whether a Bank Survives

Shareholders' money at risk first

Capital adequacy makes sure a bank has enough of its shareholders' own money at risk, compared with how risky its assets are, to absorb losses before depositors or the deposit-guarantee system are touched. Think of it as the down payment on a house: the bigger it is, the more the price can fall before the lender loses anything.

It is governed by NRB's New Capital Adequacy Framework, broadly based on Basel III as adapted for Nepal, and it is the ratio NRB watches most closely as a trigger for stepping in.

The minimums

  • Total capital adequacy ratio (CAR) — total qualifying capital divided by risk-weighted assets — of at least 11 percent.
  • Core (Tier 1) capital — mainly paid-up capital, share premium and retained earnings, after set deductions — of at least 8.5 percent.
  • The gap between 8.5 and 11 percent can be filled with supplementary (Tier 2) capital — general loan-loss provisions up to a limit, subordinated debt and revaluation reserves, among others.

Both headline figures include a 2.5-point capital conservation buffer. Underneath it, the base minimums are 6 percent core capital and 8.5 percent total capital. A bank that dips into the buffer is not in outright breach, but it faces limits on dividends (Case Study 64A). Banks treated as systemically important must hold extra on top.

REGULATORY DETAIL Sector figures: the commercial banking sector averaged a CAR of about 13.14 percent in mid-August 2025; 12.85 percent across the 20 commercial banks by mid-July 2026 (fourth quarter of FY 2082/83, unaudited); and 12.48 percent by mid-October 2026 (Sharesansar). All above the floor — but averages hide wide differences, and some banks run much closer to the minimum.

Compliant is not the same as comfortable

Individual banks show the spread. In an NRB compliance snapshot at Chaitra-end 2078 (2022):

  • Standard Chartered Bank Nepal had the highest CAR, 15.90 percent;
  • Himalayan Bank had the lowest compliant figure, 11.61 percent — just above 11;
  • NIC Asia and Prabhu Bank had Tier 1 ratios of 8.54–8.70 percent — just above 8.5.

All 27 commercial banks then operating were technically compliant. But by mid-July 2026, NIC Asia's Tier 1 ratio had fallen to 6.27 percent — below the 8.5 percent level that includes the buffer, though above the 6 percent base — so the consequence is limits on dividends rather than a breach.

"Technically compliant" and "comfortably capitalised" are different claims. The useful question is how much room a bank has before the next credit cycle pushes it to the floor.

CASE IN POINT A bank at 11.6 percent CAR against an 11 percent floor has almost no room. One bad quarter of loan downgrades — each loan moving to a riskier class pulls capital down — can take it from compliant to non-compliant within two or three reporting periods. A bank at 15–16 percent has real shock-absorbing capacity. Always read CAR as distance from the floor, not just pass or fail.

What happens when capital runs short

Falling below the capital floor is a main trigger for NRB's Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) — step-by-step restrictions on dividends, branch expansion and senior management, and eventually direct intervention — for banks that fall below capital, loan-quality or governance thresholds.

Karnali Development Bank was placed under PCA in November 2024 after failing to keep its required capital. That was not enough. By then 40.85 percent of its loans were non-performing, governance was weak and it could not repay depositors. In December 2024 NRB took direct control under Section 86(B) of the Nepal Rastra Bank Act, 2002, appointing an NRB-led team to run it, protect deposits, recover loans, audit its books and pursue those responsible.

CASE IN POINT Karnali Development Bank (Class B) is the clearest recent example of this chapter's mechanics leading to real failure: too little capital (Lesson 29.5) and worsening loans (Lesson 29.3) fed each other until cash ran out and governance collapsed. NRB climbed the exact ladder the rules provide — PCA first, then takeover. The lesson applies to Class A, B and C alike, because the classification, provisioning and capital rules are common to all of them.

Sector loan quality

Sector-wide non-performing loans were about 4.62 percent in mid-August 2025, averaged 5.44 percent across the 20 commercial banks by mid-July 2026, and 5.57 percent by mid-October 2026. Provisions were about 5.09 percent of total loans in mid-2025. These look moderate. But NRB and commentators have flagged them as rising, against high credit-to-GDP (above 91 percent) and heavy reliance on fixed deposits. None of this alone signals a crisis. It shows why numbers must be read in context, over time and against the floor.

Lesson 29.6 — Reading a Bank's Numbers Like an Examiner: A Practical Checklist

Read a bank's statements the way an NRB supervisor does, not the way a casual investor glances at EPS. Use this order every time.

  1. Capital. Find the CAR and Tier 1 ratio and measure the distance to 11 and 8.5 percent, not just whether they pass. Check for NFRS 9 transition relief, and whether today's ratio depends on it.
  2. Loan quality. Take the full breakdown — Pass, Watchlist, Substandard, Doubtful, Loss and Restructured — in rupees, not just the NPL ratio. Update your migration table over at least four quarters. A growing Watchlist or Restructured bucket, even with a flat NPL ratio, is your earliest warning.
  3. Provisioning discipline. Compare NRB-based provisions with NFRS 9 impairment in the notes, and follow the regulatory reserve in the statement of changes in equity. A growing reserve alongside strong profit means much of that profit is not yet available to shareholders.
  4. Income quality. Separate net interest income (and NIM) from fees and any trading or one-off gains. Compare the growth of interest income, interest suspense and bad loans. A divergence is worth chasing before it shows in the headlines.
PRACTICAL TOOL A five-line bank scorecard for every NEPSE bank you follow: (1) CAR and its distance from 11 percent; (2) NPL ratio and its yearly trend; (3) NIM and its trend; (4) regulatory reserve as a share of total reserves; (5) restructured and rescheduled loans as a share of all loans. Tracked over eight quarters, these five tell you more about a bank's real direction than its EPS.

Finally, compare the bank with the sector: about 12.5 to 13 percent CAR and an NPL ratio of about 5.4 to 5.7 percent in mid-to-late 2026, depending on the source. A bank well below the sector's CAR or well above its NPL ratio deserves closer study than one near the average.

WARNING No single ratio is enough. A bank can pass every test — adequate CAR, moderate NPLs, positive NIM — while hiding stress in the way restructuring, the timing of income recognition and provisioning choices interact. The discipline here is checking one disclosure against another, not memorising a pass mark.

Chapter recap

  • A bank earns a spread between interest on loans and interest on deposits. Net interest income and NIM are its core; judge fees and one-off gains separately. Cheap CASA deposits support a higher NIM.
  • Once a loan is non-performing, its interest goes to interest suspense and counts only when paid (NRB guideline 2019; since July 2025, a stage-based guidance note). Compare interest income, suspense and NPL growth.
  • NRB's five loan classes are set mainly by overdue days: Pass 1% (since March 2025), Watchlist 5%, Substandard 25%, Doubtful 50%, Loss 100%. A rising restructured or Watchlist share is an early warning; build a migration table.
  • NFRS 9 ECL runs in parallel. Where it is lower than NRB's provision, the gap goes to a non-distributable regulatory reserve. Transition relief on capital runs from FY 2081/82 to FY 2084/85 and shrinks each year.
  • Banks need 11% total capital and 8.5% Tier 1, both including a 2.5-point buffer (base 6% Tier 1). Sector CAR fell from about 13.1% (mid-2025) to 12.48% (mid-October 2026); NIC Asia's Tier 1 was 6.27% at mid-July 2026. Read capital as distance from the floor.
  • Karnali Development Bank shows the ladder: PCA in November 2024, NRB takeover in December 2024.
  • Use the five-line scorecard and cross-check disclosures against each other.
