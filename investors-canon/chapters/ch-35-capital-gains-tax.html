WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Capital gain — the profit when you sell shares for more than you paid. Withholding — tax taken at source, before you are paid. Final tax — a deduction that settles the tax completely, like a bus fare paid to the conductor. WACC (in tax) — your weighted average cost per share across all purchases, including fees — not the discount rate of Chapter 46. Settlement (T+2) — when money and shares actually change hands, two working days after the trade. EDIS — your Meroshare approval to transfer sold shares, due by 9 PM on T+1.

When you sell shares on NEPSE for more than you paid, the profit is called a capital gain. The government takes a small slice of it as tax. You never queue at a tax office for this. The slice is cut out automatically before the money reaches your bank account.

To understand that slice, you need three separate ideas. Most investors mix them up, so this chapter takes them one at a time:

  • The rate — what percentage of the profit is taken.
  • The cost basis — what the law says you paid, which is the starting point for measuring profit.
  • The collection — who takes the tax, and when.

Lesson 35.1 — The Legal Architecture: Why Share Gains Are Taxed Differently

A separate room in the tax house

Think of the Income Tax Act, 2058 (2002) as a house with many rooms. Salary sits in one room. A shopkeeper's trading income sits in another. Rent sits in a third. Gains on NEPSE shares have their own room, set mainly by Section 95Ka of the Act (often written "95A" in English).

In most rooms, you add up your income at the end of the year and file a return. In the share-gains room, the tax is withheld — cut out at the moment of sale by the market system itself. Your broker and CDS and Clearing Limited (CDSC), the company that keeps everyone's electronic shares, do this together.

It works like the toll booth on the Nagdhunga road. You do not pay the toll at the end of the month. You pay it as you pass, and then you are done.

Who pays what

The rate depends on two things. First, who you are: a resident individual, a resident company or institution, or a non-resident. Second, for individuals only, how long you held the shares: more than 365 days, or 365 days or less.

Companies get no reward for patience. A bank's trading desk pays the same rate whether it held a share for three days or three years. For a company, share profits are treated as ordinary business income.

These are the rates in force today. The rates for individuals on listed shares were cut by a Cabinet decision published in the Nepal Gazette on 22 September 2026. The other rows were set by the Finance Act 2083, from Shrawan 1, 2083 (mid-July 2026).

REGULATORY DETAIL Section 95Ka makes "any entity conducting securities exchange market business" — in practice the broker and CDSC together — the withholding agent: the party legally responsible for cutting out the tax and sending it to the Inland Revenue Department (IRD). Because the tax is taken at settlement, most retail investors never file a capital gains return at all.

The 2026 budget: higher rates and a "final" tax

The FY 2083/84 budget was presented on Jestha 15, 2083 (29 May 2026) and took effect on Shrawan 1, 2083. It did two things.

  1. It raised the rates for individuals. The long-term rate went from 5% to 7.5%. The short-term rate went from 7.5% to 10%.
  2. It declared the tax on listed-share gains a final tax.

The second change sounds like a technicality, but it matters. Before it, the broker's deduction was only an advance payment — like a deposit on a booking. A person with a high total income might still have to settle up later on an annual return. Under a final tax, the deduction closes the matter. There is nothing more to pay on that gain, and nothing to settle later.

KEY CONCEPT A final tax is like a bus fare paid to the conductor: once paid, the trip is settled. The amount your broker withholds is the whole tax on that gain. This makes life simple. The flip side is that you cannot claim any of it back, even if your income is low.

Two months later, the rates were cut again, to 3.75% and 5% (Lesson 35.2). Sources differ on whether the "final" status still applies to every seller after that cut. One tax-advisory table, for example, treats it as final only for people who do not file a return. Confirm with your broker or the IRD if this matters to you.

Unlisted shares are different

Unlisted shares are shares that do not trade on NEPSE — for example, shares in a private company, or shares in a company before it lists. They do not get the simple treatment. They are taxed at 10% for resident individuals and 15% for resident companies, and they still go through the annual return.

So if you own a NEPSE portfolio and also a stake in a friend's private trekking company, do not assume both are taxed the same way. Only the NEPSE portfolio gets the "sell and forget" treatment.

Lesson 35.2 — The 365-Day Line: Long-Term, Short-Term, and a Decade of Rate Changes

How the days are counted

The 365-day line counts calendar days, not trading days. Saturdays, Dashain and Tihar holidays and market closures all count. The clock starts when your purchase settles and stops when your sale settles.

Example: you buy on Falgun 10 and sell on Falgun 11 of the next year. You have crossed the line, even though NEPSE traded on only about 240 of those days.

Which shares did you sell?

Most investors do not buy a share just once. They build a position bit by bit — an IPO allotment, a few market purchases, some bonus shares. So when you sell part of it, which purchase date decides the rate?

In Meroshare's "My Purchase Source" tool, the seller confirms which purchases are being sold. In normal practice, the oldest purchases are used first. Think of a shopkeeper selling the oldest stock of rice first: the bags at the back of the shop go out before the new delivery.

So the holding period is judged by the oldest purchases still in your account. But the cost is still averaged across everything you hold (Lesson 35.3). Before selling part of a holding, check inside Meroshare which purchases the system has matched to the sale.

Four rate changes in seven years

The rate has changed four times since 2019. The pattern shows what the government wanted at each step: first to help a weak market, then to discourage quick trading, then to raise money, and finally to support the market again.

CASE IN POINT In May 2019 the government cut the individual rate from a flat 7.5% to a flat 5%, whatever the holding period. The market had been weak for years, and this was relief. Two years later, the Finance Act 2078 brought back a difference by holding period from Shrawan 1, 2078. Long-term holders kept 5%. Anyone selling within 365 days paid 7.5% — commentators called it a 50% higher tax on short-term traders. That two-rate design is still in place today. Only the numbers inside it have moved: up in July 2026, then sharply down in September 2026.
UPDATE · 22 SEPTEMBER 2026 Two months after the budget increase, the government reversed course. A Cabinet decision published in the Nepal Gazette on 22 September 2026 cut the rates for individuals on listed shares to 3.75% (held more than 365 days) and 5% (held 365 days or less), from the day of publication. Some early news reports said 3.5%; the gazetted long-term rate is 3.75%.

What the gap is worth now

Every change since 2021 has kept the reward for patience: long-term holders always pay less than quick traders. The goal — reward patient money, discourage rapid buying and selling — has outlasted several finance ministers.

What changes is the size of the reward. Under the July 2026 budget, holding past one year saved you 2.5 percentage points (7.5% instead of 10%). After the September cut, it saves 1.25 points (3.75% instead of 5%).

In rupees: on a Rs 1 lakh gain, waiting past the one-year mark now saves Rs 1,250. That is still worth having, but it is a small reason to hold a share you would otherwise sell.

WARNING Do not trust a rate you learned two or three years ago. The Finance Act is passed every year with the budget — usually presented in Jestha (May) and effective from Shrawan 1 (mid-July) — and the share-gains rate has changed four times since 2019. Always check the rate in force on the day your sale settles, not the day you bought.

Lesson 35.3 — WACC: How Nepal Computes Your Cost Basis

One average price for everything

To measure your profit, the system needs to know what you paid. Some countries track each purchase separately and sell them in order — "first in, first out" (FIFO) or "last in, first out" (LIFO). Nepal does not.

Nepal uses one average price per share, called WACC in Nepali market talk ("weighted average cost"). Note: in company finance, WACC means something else — a discount rate (Chapter 46). Here it simply means your average cost per share.

Think of a milk collection centre in Chitwan. Farmers pour milk from many cans into one big tank. Once it is in the tank, nobody can say which litre came from which farmer. The centre just knows the total litres and the total price paid. Your shares work the same way: every purchase of one company's shares goes into one pool, and the pool has one average cost.

Why pool instead of tracking each purchase

Electronic shares in your demat account have no serial numbers. One Nabil Bank share is exactly the same as any other. So the tax system treats cost the same way: one average, updated every time new shares arrive — whether bought, given as bonus, or taken up in a rights issue.

Pooling is also easier to run at scale. The broker's software needs only one number per company per account, not your full purchase history at every sale.

The formula, and every fee that goes into it

WACC = (Total money spent on all purchases, including charges) ÷ (Total shares acquired)

"Money spent" is more than the share price. It includes three charges on each purchase:

  • the broker commission;
  • the SEBON fee of 0.015% of the trade value (SEBON is the Securities Board of Nepal, the market regulator);
  • the DP charge of about Rs 25 per transaction, paid to your depository participant (the firm that runs your demat account).

The same three charges apply when you sell. They are taken off the sale money, and what is left is the "adjusted selling price" used to measure the gain.

Broker commission falls as the trade gets bigger. These slabs have applied since Jestha 1, 2081 (mid-May 2024), after SEBON ordered a 10% reduction:

KEY CONCEPT Your cost is never just "the price on the screen". It is the price plus commission plus the SEBON fee plus the DP charge, averaged across every purchase of that company you have made. If you leave out the fees, your cost looks lower, your profit looks higher, and you will think you owe more tax than you do.

A worked example

An investor buys Standard Chartered Bank (SCB) shares three times:

That Rs 620.11 is the cost the broker's system uses for any share from this holding. It is not Rs 600, not Rs 620, and not a simple average of the three prices. The big third purchase pulls the average towards Rs 620, and the fees push it slightly above.

If the investor later sells 500 shares at Rs 750, the profit per share is Rs 750, less selling fees, less Rs 620.11 — not Rs 750 minus Rs 600.

PRACTICAL TOOL Check your WACC before you sell. Log into Meroshare, open "My Purchase Source", and choose the company. You will see every purchase on record. You can check or correct the records there, and Meroshare then works out the average that will be used for your tax. Do this before the sale, while a wrong record can still be fixed.

Lesson 35.4 — Bonus Shares and Rights Shares: The Cost-Basis Traps

Two kinds of share arrive without a normal market purchase: bonus shares and rights shares. Both change your average cost in ways that surprise people.

Bonus shares enter at Rs 100

Bonus shares are free extra shares a company gives existing owners, made by turning its saved profits into share capital. Banks and hydropower companies give them often. You pay no cash. But 5% tax was already taken on their Rs 100 face value when they were given (Lesson 36.3).

So bonus shares enter your pool at Rs 100 each. For most holdings, that is far below what you paid for your other shares. The result: your average cost falls for every share you hold.

Back to the milk tank. Pour in a few litres that cost almost nothing, and the average price per litre in the whole tank goes down.

Example: you hold 1,000 shares at an average of Rs 500. Your pool is Rs 500,000. The company gives a 10% bonus. You receive 100 new shares, entered at Rs 100 each — Rs 10,000 in total. The pool becomes Rs 510,000 and 1,100 shares.

New WACC = Rs 510,000 ÷ 1,100 = Rs 463.64 per share

Every share now carries this lower cost — the old 1,000 and the new 100 alike. If you later sell at Rs 700, your taxable profit is Rs 236.36 per share instead of Rs 200.

The bonus did not create tax-free wealth. It moved part of the tax to later, and spread it across more shares. It is collected when you sell, at whatever rate applies then.

WARNING Bonus shares are not tax-free just because you did not pay for them. 5% was taken on their face value when you received them. When you sell, everything above that Rs 100 is taxable profit. And by lowering your average cost, they quietly raise the taxable profit on every other share in the same holding.

Bonus shares start their own clock

There is a second trap. A bonus share's holding period usually starts on the day it reaches your demat account — not on the day you bought the original shares.

Suppose you have held your original shares for two years, and the bonus arrived three months ago. If the bonus shares are matched to a sale today, that part may be taxed at the short-term rate, even though your original holding is long-term.

So WACC mixes the cost of bonus and original shares together. It does not mix their clocks. Each batch keeps its own start date.

Rights shares: real money and a fresh date

Rights shares are new shares offered to existing owners, who must pay for them — usually at or near the Rs 100 face value, though some companies charge more. The price you pay, plus fees, enters the pool as a real purchase, just like buying on the market.

The catch is timing. Rights shares are often allotted months after you apply and pay. Their clock starts on the allotment date, not the payment date. If you applied in Baisakh and the shares arrived in Ashoj, they count as held from Ashoj.

CAUTION Bonus shares lower your average cost without you spending anything. Rights shares add real money to the pool at the price you paid. Mixing them up — treating rights shares as free, or giving bonus shares your original purchase date — is the most common cost mistake Nepali investors make. It changes both the rate you pay and the amount it is charged on.

Lesson 35.5 — How the Deduction Actually Happens: Broker, CDSC, and the Settlement Cycle

What happens after you click "sell"

Everything so far comes together in one automatic step. A NEPSE sale usually settles — money and shares actually change hands — on T+2, two working days after the trade day ("T"). At that point, the broker's Trading Management System (TMS), working through CDSC, does the tax arithmetic for you:

  1. It takes your WACC for that company from your Meroshare records.
  2. It takes the selling fees off the sale money to get the adjusted selling price.
  3. It subtracts WACC to find the gain.
  4. It checks the holding period and picks the long-term or short-term rate (or the flat 10% rate for an institution).
  5. It withholds the tax and sends it to the IRD through CDSC's central system.

What reaches your bank account is already after tax. You do not write a cheque to the IRD. You only calculate anything if you want to check the broker's work.

REGULATORY DETAIL Before the FY 2083/84 "final tax" rule, individuals whose total annual income was above Rs 40 lakh also had to file a D-4 statement and get tax clearance from the IRD by the end of Ashoj — even though tax had already been withheld at sale. The final-tax rule was meant to remove that second step for listed-share gains. Gains on unlisted shares and other assets still follow the normal filing rules.

The EDIS deadline: the bigger danger

New investors are caught out far more often by a settlement deadline than by the tax rate. After a sale, you must approve the electronic transfer of the sold shares from your demat account to your broker. This approval is called EDIS (Electronic Delivery Instruction Slip), and you give it in Meroshare.

The deadline is 9:00 PM on T+1 — one working day after the trade. Miss it, and you face a penalty of 20% of the sale amount for failing to deliver shares you sold. This is not a tax. It is a fine for breaking settlement rules. But it sits in the same Meroshare session as the WACC confirmation, so do both together.

CAUTION The 20% EDIS penalty can be many times larger than the tax on the same sale. Example: you sell Rs 2 lakh of shares at a Rs 20,000 profit. The short-term tax at 5% is Rs 1,000. A missed EDIS deadline costs Rs 40,000. Set a phone reminder for the evening after every sale.

Institutions: same deduction, different ending

For banks, insurers, mutual funds and brokers' own trading desks, the deduction at sale works the same way: 10% is withheld through the same CDSC system. What happens afterwards is different.

For most institutions, share profits are ordinary business income in the yearly company tax return. The 10% withheld at sale is treated as tax paid in advance, credited against the company's total tax bill. It is not a final, closed tax as it now is for individuals. The finance team, not the trading desk, settles the final figure.

PRACTICAL TOOL Meroshare and most brokers' TMS portals let you download a yearly statement of capital gains and tax deducted. Download it at least once a year. Check three things against your own records: the WACC used, the holding period applied to each sale, and the rate charged.

Lesson 35.6 — Putting It Together: A Full Worked Portfolio Example

In real life, these rules rarely appear one at a time. One sale usually brings them all together. Let us follow an investor, Sunita, who built a position in a commercial bank:

EventDateSharesPrice per shareNotes
Market purchaseKartik 2081800Rs 480First purchase; clock starts here
Bonus allotment (10%)Ashoj 208280Rs 100 (face value)Enters pool at face value; own clock starts
Rights allotmentMagh 2082200Rs 100Real new money; own clock starts
Market purchaseBhadra 2083300Rs 560Recent top-up; newest clock

Step 1: her average cost

By Bhadra 2083, Sunita holds 1,380 shares. Her pool adds up like this:

  • first purchase, with fees: about Rs 3.85 lakh;
  • bonus shares at face value: Rs 8,000;
  • rights shares, with fees: about Rs 20,000;
  • recent top-up, with fees: about Rs 1.68 lakh.

The total is about Rs 5.82 lakh. Divided by 1,380 shares, her WACC is roughly Rs 421 per share. The cheap bonus and rights shares pulled it well below her first price of Rs 480. The expensive top-up pushed it back up a little.

Step 2: which shares she sold, and at what rate

In late Ashwin 2083 — after the 22 September 2026 rate cut — Sunita sells 600 shares at Rs 650. The system answers two questions separately.

Which shares? Oldest first, so all 600 come from her Kartik 2081 purchase of 800, held about 23 months. That is well past 365 days, so the long-term rate of 3.75% applies. Her recent Bhadra 2083 shares would not have qualified on their own.

What cost? The pooled WACC of Rs 421, not the Rs 480 she actually paid for those particular shares.

Gain per share ≈ Rs 650 (less selling fees) − Rs 421 = about Rs 227
Gain on 600 shares ≈ Rs 136,000
Tax withheld at 3.75% ≈ Rs 5,100, taken automatically at settlement

If the system had matched the sale to her newest shares instead, the rate would have been the short-term 5%. But the cost would still have been the same Rs 421, because cost is pooled even when clocks are not.

Sunita did not "choose" the lower rate. The oldest-first rule chose it for her. An investor who does not know this rule cannot predict which rate a sale will trigger.

CASE IN POINT In Sunita's case, four events — a purchase, a bonus, a rights allotment and a second purchase — became one average cost. At the same time, they stayed four separate clocks for choosing the rate. Both rules work at once. If you use the average cost to judge the holding period, or use the oldest date for the whole pool, you get the wrong answer.

Chapter recap

  • Tax on NEPSE share gains is withheld at sale by the broker and CDSC under Section 95Ka of the Income Tax Act, 2058. Most retail investors never file a return for it.
  • Since 22 September 2026, resident individuals pay 3.75% on listed shares held more than 365 days and 5% on shares held 365 days or less. Resident institutions pay 10% on listed shares; unlisted shares are taxed at 10% (individuals) or 15% (institutions) through the normal return; non-residents pay 25%.
  • The rate has changed four times since 2019: a flat 5% from FY 2076/77, 5%/7.5% from FY 2078/79, 7.5%/10% from Shrawan 2083, and 3.75%/5% since 22 September 2026. Always use the rate in force when your sale settles.
  • The Finance Act 2083 made the tax on listed-share gains a final tax — the deduction closes the matter. Sources differ on whether this still applies to every seller after the September cut, so check with your broker or the IRD.
  • Your cost is the WACC: one average across every purchase of a company's shares, including commission, the 0.015% SEBON fee and the DP charge.
  • Bonus shares enter the pool at Rs 100 and lower your average cost; rights shares enter at the price paid. Both start their own holding-period clock on the day they are allotted.
  • Cost is pooled, but clocks are not. The oldest shares are normally matched to a sale first, and that decides the rate.
  • Approve EDIS in Meroshare by 9:00 PM on T+1. Missing it costs 20% of the sale amount — often far more than the tax.
