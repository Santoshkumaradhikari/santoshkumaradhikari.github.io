Rights Issues and FPOs

"When a company asks its shareholders for more money, the question is never simply whether to give it. It is whether the terms are fair, whether the capital will be used wisely, and whether you are being treated as an owner or as a source of funds."

Capital formation is the lifeblood of any company's growth, and in Nepal's equity market, the methods through which listed companies raise additional capital follow patterns that are distinct from the practices of more mature exchanges. For the investor navigating the Nepal Stock Exchange (NEPSE), two instruments dominate the secondary capital-raising landscape: the rights issue and the Further Public Offering, or FPO. Both mechanisms dilute existing ownership, and both carry consequences that are almost never fully appreciated by the retail shareholders who participate in them — or, worse, who ignore them entirely.

This chapter provides a complete analytical framework for understanding these two instruments. We will work through the arithmetic of dilution and pricing with the care such decisions need, and build a framework for choosing whether to subscribe. We will look at the rules that govern FPOs in Nepal. And finally we will face an uncomfortable question: when does raising more equity help shareholders, and when does it quietly hurt them? The answer this chapter builds towards is simple to state. A company does not destroy value just by issuing more shares. It destroys value when the new money cannot earn enough, or when the terms move value unfairly from one group of shareholders to another.

Lesson 15.1 — Why Rights Issues Are the Primary Capital-Raising Tool for NEPSE Companies

To understand why rights issues dominate Nepali corporate finance, one must first appreciate the institutional and regulatory environment from which they have emerged. Nepal's capital market is relatively shallow. The bond market remains underdeveloped, institutional investors are few, and access to long-term bank credit is both expensive and collateral-intensive. When a company needs growth capital — whether to expand its branch network, fund new infrastructure, or meet regulatory minimum paid-up capital requirements — the rights issue to existing shareholders is, in most cases, the path of least resistance.

A rights issue is a mechanism through which a listed company invites its existing shareholders to subscribe to new shares in proportion to their current holdings, typically at a price below the prevailing market price. The word 'rights' is precise: each shareholder receives a legally recognised entitlement to new shares, proportional to their existing stake. They are not obligated to exercise this right, but the right itself has intrinsic value — value that, as we shall see, is frequently misunderstood or abandoned by retail investors.

The Regulatory Mandate and the Minimum Paid-Up Capital Problem

Perhaps the single most powerful driver of rights issues in Nepal is regulatory compulsion. Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB), the central bank, has over the past decade issued a series of directives requiring commercial banks and financial institutions to maintain progressively higher levels of paid-up capital. The biggest step came in July 2015. NRB's monetary policy quadrupled the minimum paid-up capital of commercial banks from Rs 2 billion (Rs 2 arba) to Rs 8 billion (Rs 8 arba) within two years, and development banks and finance companies faced similar jumps. This was a requirement that pushed banks that could not meet it through retained earnings to raise capital through rights issues, bonus shares, FPOs and mergers — in large amounts and in a short time. The number of commercial banks fell from 30 in 2015 to 20 licensed in 2026.

These rising rules produced an odd result. Investors who had bought bank shares as a stable, income-earning asset were asked, year after year, to put more money into the same bank just to keep the regulator satisfied. In the short run the new capital often could not be lent out profitably fast enough, so returns on equity fell. Lesson 15.5 looks at how much of the later fall in bank returns came from this extra capital, and how much from other causes. The regulatory aim — safer banks — was sound; the cost to shareholders who could not keep subscribing was real.

Beyond banks, development banks (Class B) and finance companies (Class C) faced similar trajectories. Hydropower companies, having secured project licences, have historically used rights issues to fund construction phases — a more defensible use of the instrument, though one that introduces its own timeline and execution risks.

Why Promoters Prefer Rights Issues Over Other Routes

From the promoter's perspective, a rights issue appears to have several advantages over other ways of raising money. (This is this book's reading of promoters' incentives, not a proven rule for every company.) It is a little like a family that owns a shop asking only its own members for money to expand, rather than taking in an outside partner who would want a say. A private placement to new strategic investors would dilute existing promoter control. A public offering to entirely new shareholders would invite scrutiny, require more extensive regulatory compliance, and potentially introduce activist or institutional voices into the shareholder register. A rights issue, by contrast, is offered first to existing shareholders — including the promoters themselves — who, if they have the financial means, can maintain their ownership percentage simply by subscribing in full.

Rights issues may also be cheaper and quicker to arrange than a public offering, because they go to a known group of shareholders — though this book has not compared actual costs. The Securities Board of Nepal (SEBON) has built a rights issue approval framework that, while carrying its own procedural requirements, is broadly familiar to corporate secretaries and legal advisors across the listed universe.

MARKET CONTEXT Rights issues are far more common on NEPSE than FPOs, especially among banks, development banks and insurers, which have repeatedly raised capital over the past fifteen years. (This is an observation; this book has not totalled the capital raised by each route. SEBON publishes a list of approved rights issues for anyone who wants to check.)

The Shareholder Who Does Nothing

Understanding rights issues as the primary capital-raising tool requires confronting an asymmetry that is rarely discussed in Nepali investor education: a shareholder who does nothing usually loses something. When new shares are sold cheaply, the price of each existing share adjusts down (Lesson 15.2). A shareholder who subscribes ends up with more shares, and roughly the same wealth. A shareholder who does nothing keeps the same number of shares, each worth less, and owns a smaller slice of the company. The value of the unused entitlement is lost to them.

This is not a theoretical concern. Nepal's market is mostly retail. Many shareholders are small investors who hold shares in demat accounts but lack the money or the awareness to act on rights issues. For them, round after round of dilution without taking part can steadily erode wealth over the years. An investor who bought 100 shares in a commercial bank in 2015 and never subscribed to any of its later rights issues now owns a much smaller percentage of the bank, and gave up the value of every entitlement. Whether that investor is poorer overall also depends on how well the bank used the new money — the question Lesson 15.5 takes up.

This is the foundational problem that the rest of this chapter will address: the mechanics and the decisions involved, approached with the analytical rigour that the stakes demand.

Lesson 15.2 — Rights Ratio, Rights Price, and Theoretical Ex-Rights Price (TERP)

The arithmetic of a rights issue is not complicated, but it is frequently misunderstood — with real financial consequences. Getting comfortable with three numbers — the rights ratio, the rights price, and the Theoretical Ex-Rights Price — is the analytical foundation upon which every rights issue decision should rest.

The Rights Ratio: How Many New Shares for How Many Old

The rights ratio expresses the entitlement offered to existing shareholders. A 1:5 rights issue, for example, means that for every five shares currently held, the shareholder is entitled to subscribe to one new share. A 1:1 rights issue — sometimes called a 'one-for-one' or a 100% rights issue — means the company is doubling its share count, with each existing shareholder entitled to one new share for each they already hold.

In Nepal, rights ratios have historically been aggressive by international standards. Ratios of 1:1 (100%), 1:2 (50%), and even 2:1 (200%) are not uncommon, particularly in the banking sector during capital enhancement drives. A 1:1 ratio is a significant event: it doubles the total paid-up capital of the company and, if fully subscribed, brings in an amount of fresh capital equal to the rights price multiplied by the entire existing share count.

The Rights Price: Discount as Incentive, Not as Gift

The rights price is the price at which new shares are offered to existing shareholders. It is almost always set below the prevailing market price — this discount is the incentive for shareholders to subscribe. In Nepal, rights prices are usually set at par value (Rs 100 per share), however high the market price, though some issues are priced above par. This has an important implication: the lower the rights price relative to market price, the greater the value of the rights entitlement itself, and the steeper the post-issue dilution in the market price.

A rights price set at par for a company whose shares trade at Rs 800 is a very different economic event from a rights price set at Rs 600 for a company whose shares trade at Rs 800. In the first case, the rights entitlement carries enormous intrinsic value; in the second, it carries moderate value. Both dilute the share price post-issue, but the magnitude differs significantly.

The Theoretical Ex-Rights Price: The Adjusted Value After the Issue

The Theoretical Ex-Rights Price, universally abbreviated as TERP, answers a deceptively simple question: if a rights issue is fully subscribed, what should the market price of the share be immediately after the issue? TERP is not a prediction — markets do not always behave efficiently, and the actual trading price will diverge from TERP based on sentiment, liquidity, and news flow. But TERP is the rational anchor around which ex-rights pricing should cluster, and any investor making a rights issue decision should calculate it.

The formula is:

TERP = (N x Market Price + R x Rights Price) / (N + R)

Where N is the number of existing shares, R is the number of new rights shares, and the market price is the prevailing price immediately before the ex-rights date. Let us work through a concrete example.

A Worked Example

Imagine a commercial bank — call it Bank X. It has 100 million shares outstanding and a market price of Rs 400 per share. It proposes a 1:2 rights issue (one new share for every two held) at a rights price of Rs 100 per share.

Using our formula: N = 100 million (existing shares), R = 50 million (new shares, at 1:2 ratio), Market Price = Rs 400, Rights Price = Rs 100.

TERP = (100 x 400 + 50 x 100) / (100 + 50) = (40,000 + 5,000) / 150 = 45,000 / 150 = Rs 300

Think of mixing milk at a dairy collection centre. If 100 litres worth Rs 400 a litre are mixed with 50 litres bought at Rs 100 a litre, the whole can of 150 litres is worth Rs 300 a litre. Adding cheaper milk does not make the can richer; it lowers the average. Rights shares work the same way.

The TERP is Rs 300. Now follow one investor who owns two shares:

StepSubscribesLets the entitlement lapse
Before: two shares (2 × Rs 400) plus Rs 100 cashRs 800 + Rs 100 = Rs 900Rs 800 + Rs 100 = Rs 900
Pays for one new shareRs 100 cash paidRs 100 cash kept
After: shares valued at TERP Rs 3003 shares = Rs 9002 shares = Rs 600
Total wealth afterRs 900Rs 600 + Rs 100 = Rs 700

The investor who subscribes keeps the same wealth, Rs 900 (before costs and market moves). The investor who lets the entitlement lapse ends up Rs 200 poorer. Note the two different numbers: the entitlement is worth Rs 100 per old share, so Rs 200 for the two old shares that together give the right to one new share.

TERP rests on assumptions. It assumes the whole issue is taken up and the rights price is known. It assumes nothing else, such as a bonus issue, happens at the same time. And it assumes the last price before the issue is a fair starting point, and that the business itself does not change in value meanwhile. It is a theoretical benchmark, not a guaranteed market price.

A second example, priced above par. The formula uses the actual rights price, not an assumed Rs 100. If a share trades at Rs 500 and a 1:2 rights issue is priced at Rs 250, TERP = (500 + 0.5 × 250) ÷ 1.5 = Rs 416.67. The entitlement is then worth Rs 83.33 per old share — much less, because the rights price is closer to the market price.

The Value of the Rights Entitlement

This brings us to the value of the rights entitlement itself — the 'rights' that a shareholder can exercise by subscribing. (In some markets, such as India, rights entitlements are traded on the stock exchange; in Nepal they are not yet, as explained below.) Theoretically, the value of the rights attached to each existing share is:

Value of Right = (Market Price - Rights Price) / (N/R + 1) OR = Market Price - TERP

In our Bank X example, the rights value attached to each existing share is Rs 400 - Rs 300 (the TERP) = Rs 100. Or, expressed differently: since the 1:2 ratio means you need two shares to get one right, the right to subscribe to one new share is worth Rs 200 in total rights value across two existing shares.

This is the number that matters in practice. Four different things can happen to an entitlement, and the words are often mixed up:

TermWhat it means
SubscribeUse the entitlement: pay the rights price and receive the new shares
Renounce (transfer)Pass the entitlement to someone else, where the issue's rules allow it
Sell the entitlementTransfer it in return for money
Let it lapseDo nothing; the entitlement expires unused

Under the system in place in November 2026, rights entitlements are not traded separately on NEPSE. The law does allow an entitlement to be transferred privately where the issue permits it, but in practice it is slow, and many holders cannot find a buyer (Chapter 87 describes the process). Shares left unsubscribed are auctioned by the company after the window closes, and the auction money goes to the company. So for most shareholders today, an entitlement is either used or lost. This may change: on 19 November 2026 (3 Mangsir 2083), SEBON chairman Gopal Bhatta said the regulator plans to bring in, within the current fiscal year, a policy letting entitlements be traded separately on NEPSE during the subscription period. That is a proposal, not yet a working facility — check its status before relying on it.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION Many retail investors in Nepal believe that not subscribing to a rights issue costs nothing — that they simply "don't take up new shares." It does cost something. Your shares are adjusted down to TERP, your percentage of the company shrinks, and the entitlement's value (Rs 100 per old share in the example) is forfeited. It is not handed to the company as a gift. Part of it comes back to the company through the auction premium, shared by all remaining shareholders, and part goes to auction buyers if they pay less than the shares are worth — but none of it comes back to you. The cost is easy to miss, but it is real.

TERP and Price Discovery on NEPSE

In practice, NEPSE adjusts the share's reference price when the rights book closes, using the same idea as TERP: adjusted price = (last traded price before book closure + rights price × rights ratio) ÷ (1 + rights ratio). For example, when National Hydropower (NHPC) closed its books for a 50% rights issue in June 2023, its last traded price was Rs 223.10. With a Rs 100 rights price, the formula gives (223.10 + 0.5 × 100) ÷ 1.5 = Rs 182.07; ShareSansar reported the adjusted price as Rs 182.7, which appears to be a small typing slip. After the adjustment, the market is free to trade above or below that level, depending on how investors see the company, the amount being raised and the wider market.

Investors who misread an ex-rights price drop as a buying opportunity — simply because "the price fell" — without understanding that the drop is mechanical and not informational are making a category error. The TERP is the new baseline from which any further price movement should be judged.

Lesson 15.3 — Subscribe, Transfer, or Let Lapse: A Decision Framework

Every rights issue confronts the Nepali shareholder with three choices: subscribe in full, subscribe partially, or let the entitlement lapse. (A fourth choice, transferring the rights privately to someone else, exists on paper but is rarely practical in Nepal.) These are not only financial choices. They reflect your whole judgement about the company, its management, how it will use the capital, and your own financial position. A disciplined framework for navigating this decision will serve investors across every rights issue they encounter throughout their investing lives.

The Four Questions Before You Decide

The decision to subscribe or let the entitlement lapse should flow from four questions, answered in sequence. Skipping any of them is a shortcut that costs money.

The first question is: Do I still want to own this company? A rights issue is an opportunity to re-examine your investment thesis. The company may have got worse since you first bought in: management has changed, the business model has weakened, or competition has grown. If so, a rights issue is the moment to ask whether you would buy this share at TERP if you did not already own it. If the honest answer is no, the rights issue does not change that calculus. In that case, let the entitlement lapse and consider whether your existing holding still makes sense — remembering that the value of the lapsed rights is lost to you.

The second question is: Will this capital be productively deployed? The purpose of the capital raise matters enormously. Compare two cases. A hydropower company raises rights capital to build a profitable project with a signed Power Purchase Agreement. A commercial bank raises rights capital for the seventh time in a decade, mainly to meet regulatory minimums, while its return on equity keeps falling. The difference is huge. Capital raised to fund real economic value creation can produce returns that compensate for dilution. Capital raised to maintain regulatory compliance, or to fund acquisitions at inflated prices, or to expand into low-return segments, will likely deliver substandard returns on the incremental equity deployed.

The third question is: What is the post-rights valuation, and is it attractive? Using TERP as your base price, calculate the Price-to-Book and Price-to-Earnings ratios at which you would effectively be acquiring the new shares. Suppose you subscribe to a bank's rights issue at a rights price of Rs 100 (par). The TERP may represent a Price-to-Book of 2.5x. You would then be paying well above book value for a business whose return on equity might not justify that multiple. The act of subscribing is equivalent to making a fresh investment decision; price it accordingly.

The fourth question is: Do I have the capital to subscribe without compromising my overall portfolio? Rights issues create a liquidity demand that is frequently underestimated by retail investors, particularly when they hold shares in multiple companies and several rights issues coincide — a common occurrence in Nepal's periodic waves of capital enhancement drives. Borrowing to subscribe is almost never warranted unless the rights issue presents an exceptionally clear opportunity; borrowing to take part in capital raises adds risk that most retail investors are not equipped to manage.

Behind all four questions sits a fifth: what else could this money do? The Rs 10,000 needed to subscribe could instead go into another NEPSE company, a government bond, a fixed deposit or paying off a loan. Subscribing is right only if putting more money into this company offers a better return for its risk than those alternatives.

The Maths of Partial Subscription

If financial constraints prevent full subscription, a partial subscription — subscribing to some but not all of your entitlement — is a rational middle path, as long as you understand its cost. Because unexercised rights cannot be sold on NEPSE, the part you do not take up is usually lost to you; the company auctions it. So calculate your full entitlement, work out how many new shares you can afford at the rights price, and weigh the value you give up on the rest. If that lost value is large, one option is to raise the money by selling some existing shares before the window closes. That is not automatically the best move: compare the price you would get for the old shares with the value you would receive in new ones, and remember the selling costs and tax.

The Subscription Window and the Auction

SEBON rules set a fixed subscription window for every rights issue, typically a few weeks. Unlike India, Nepal has no market for trading rights entitlements during this window. After it closes, the unsubscribed shares are auctioned by the company to other investors, and the proceeds belong to the company. For you, this means the only practical way to capture the value of your rights today is to subscribe. If you cannot fund the full entitlement, you will need to raise the cash some other way (Lesson 15.2 notes the planned reform).

The practical implication is that the subscribe-or-not decision should be made early in the subscription window, leaving time to arrange the money — or to sell some existing shares to raise it — before the window closes.

A Note on Oversubscription

In Nepal, shareholders cannot apply for more than their entitlement. Shares left unsubscribed are offered through a company auction after the window closes, open to investors generally. Bidding in such an auction is a fresh investment decision at the auction price — usually well above the rights price — and should be judged like any other purchase.

Lesson 15.4 — FPOs in Nepal: Rules, Pricing, and When Companies Use Them

The Further Public Offering — colloquially known as the FPO — is a less common but equally important secondary capital-raising mechanism in Nepal. Unlike a rights issue, which is offered exclusively to existing shareholders, an FPO is offered to the general public, including institutional investors and the broader retail market. It is, in essence, a second Initial Public Offering: the same company that once listed its shares for the first time through an IPO returns to the public market to raise additional capital.

The Regulatory Framework Governing FPOs

FPOs in Nepal are governed by the Securities Registration and Issue Regulation, 2073, issued under the Securities Act 2063. That regulation has been amended several times; SEBON's website lists the seventh amendment and an eighth amendment of 2083 (2026), which among other changes created the new route for selling shares to Non-Resident Nepalis in foreign currency (Chapter 9). Version note: this book has not yet checked the full amended text clause by clause, so treat the conditions below as a guide and confirm them in the current regulation and the issue's prospectus. Under the older 2065 rules, a company needed, among other things, profits in at least two of the last five years and a net worth per share above its paid-up value per share. The FPO had to be approved at its general meeting, and a premium price had to be justified to SEBON with its calculation. Whether each condition still applies, and in what form, should be checked in the current text.

FPO prices are set in one of three broad ways, and they should not be confused. Some FPOs are sold at par, Rs 100. Most recent bank FPOs have been sold at a fixed premium justified in the prospectus — for example Rs 333 for NMB Bank in 2018 and Rs 157 for Himalayan Bank in 2025. And book building, used so far mainly for IPOs (Chapter 14), is possible for some issuers. The rules for each route, and the limits on premiums, are set in the regulation and SEBON's guidelines and can differ by type of company.

Why Companies Choose FPOs Over Rights Issues

Given that rights issues are simpler and faster, why would a company choose an FPO? The answer lies in the objectives of the capital raise and the composition of the desired shareholder base. A company that wants to broaden its ownership base — reducing promoter concentration, admitting institutional shareholders, increasing free float, or improving market visibility and analyst coverage — will find an FPO more suitable than a rights issue. An FPO, by inviting public participation, can significantly diversify the shareholder register in a way that a rights issue, which tends to preserve the existing ownership structure, cannot.

An FPO also requires a full prospectus and public scrutiny. More disclosure is useful, but it is not the same as good governance: a company can publish a thick prospectus and still be badly run. Treat an FPO as a chance to read more, not as a quality stamp.

A third consideration is the role of promoter dilution. Some companies must bring their promoter and public holdings to a required ratio. An FPO does this arithmetically without promoters selling: new public shares enlarge the total, so the promoters' percentage falls while their number of shares stays the same. NMB Bank's 2018 FPO was approved for exactly this purpose. Whether a particular FPO fully meets a company's ownership rules still has to be checked against the regulator's limits.

FPO Pricing: What the Investor Must Evaluate

The critical question for an investor evaluating an FPO is whether the offered price represents a fair value relative to the company's intrinsic worth and the prevailing market price of the existing shares. This sounds simple, but it requires disentangling several layers.

First, the FPO price is typically set at or below the prevailing market price — if the FPO were priced above market, rational investors would simply buy on the exchange rather than subscribe to the FPO. The discount, if any, represents an immediate gain for FPO subscribers, but this must be weighed against the allotment probability (in oversubscribed FPOs, which are common, individual allotments may be very small) and the post-listing price trajectory.

Second, the investor must independently assess whether the market price itself is justified. An FPO from a company trading at an unjustifiably high market price — perhaps inflated by promotional commentary or speculative interest — is not automatically attractive simply because the FPO price is set below that inflated level. The correct reference point is intrinsic value: what is this business worth, based on its sustainable earnings, asset quality, growth prospects, and competitive position?

Third, the use of FPO proceeds must be scrutinised in the prospectus. FPO prospectuses in Nepal are required to disclose the objects of the issue — how the company intends to use the capital raised. The quality of this disclosure varies, and investors should read it with a sceptical eye. Vague statements about 'business expansion' or 'working capital requirements' are less informative than specific, quantified capital allocation plans with identifiable return profiles.

Key Points DUE DILIGENCE CHECKLIST FOR FPO APPLICANTS Before subscribing to an FPO: (1) Read the prospectus in full, especially the objects of issue, risk factors, and related party transactions. (2) Compare the FPO price to the company's Price-to-Book and Price-to-Earnings on both historical and forward earnings. (3) Assess the allotment probability — heavily oversubscribed FPOs may yield very small allotments, reducing the practical significance of the price discount. (4) Examine the promoter track record and any history of capital misallocation. (5) Check whether the company has pending rights issues; subscribing to an FPO and then facing an immediate rights issue creates a double capital demand.

Post-FPO Price Behaviour: Patterns on NEPSE

FPO shares do not get their own listing day like an IPO. They join a share that is already trading. So the 'gain' for a successful applicant is simply the gap between the FPO price and the market price when the new shares can be sold. That gap can shrink or disappear, as Nepal Bank's 2018 issue showed. This book has not compiled a full record of FPO outcomes, so it makes no claim about how FPOs perform on average. Decide on the relationship between the FPO price and what the business is worth, not on a hoped-for gap.

Lesson 15.5 — The Equity Dilution Problem: Why Frequent Rights Issues Hurt Minority Shareholders

"Repeated rights issues that are not matched by proportionate growth in earnings are a form of silent taxation on minority shareholders — one that compounds invisibly and damages wealth with no single moment of visible harm."

The final lesson of this chapter turns to what may be the most important, and least understood, structural risk in Nepal's share market. It is the harm done to minority shareholders by frequent rights issues that are not matched by growth in earnings. This is not a peripheral concern. For long-term holders of NEPSE bank shares, the combination of repeated capital raising and falling returns has been one of the biggest drags on wealth — though, as the case study below shows, it was not the only cause.

Return on Equity: The Number That Tells the Story

The central diagnostic for evaluating the impact of repeated rights issues is Return on Equity, or ROE — net profit divided by shareholders' equity. ROE is the measure of what the company earns, proportionally, on the capital shareholders have entrusted to it. If a company consistently earns Rs 100 on every Rs 1,000 of equity, its ROE is 10%. When a rights issue adds another Rs 500 to the equity base, the company must now earn Rs 150 in total net profit just to maintain that 10% ROE. If it cannot — if it earns Rs 115, Rs 120, perhaps Rs 130 — ROE falls. Earnings per share falls. And unless the market blindly re-rates the stock upward on other grounds, the price falls too, or at best stagnates.

Nepal's commercial banks show what this looks like in real numbers — and why the cause needs care.

Return on equity fell by more than half. Three forces were at work. First, the forced jump in capital after 2015 raised equity faster than banks could lend it out profitably. Second, the gap between lending and deposit rates narrowed from about 5.2 to about 3.4 percentage points, partly because of NRB's spread cap (Chapter 0.2), so each rupee of loans earned less. Third, bad loans rose, and provisions for them ate into profit. The falling core-capital ratio is a clue. In recent years banks' capital has not been sitting idle, because loans have grown faster than capital. So much of the later fall in returns came from lower earnings per rupee of assets, not from new shares alone. The honest conclusion is that dilution was a major cause of the poor shareholder returns, especially around 2015–2018, but not the only one.

Book Value Growth Without EPS Growth: The Trap

Company managements and market commentators in Nepal often say: 'Our book value per share is growing; the company is becoming stronger.' This is technically true but economically misleading (Chapter 13 explains how book value per share is calculated). Book value per share grows when retained earnings are added to equity. But when rights issues continuously add capital at or near par value to the equity base — while earnings grow more slowly — book value per share can grow while simultaneously EPS stagnates or declines. The investor who bought shares at 3x book value for a 15% ROE business now holds shares at 3x a higher book value, but the ROE has fallen to 10%, making the 3x multiple unjustifiable. This is book value growth that can coexist with falling shareholder returns.

The correct framing: what matters is not whether book value per share is higher than last year, but whether the company is earning an adequate return on that growing equity base. Growth in capital without adequate returns is not strength; it is dilution wearing the costume of prosperity.

The Promoter's Perspective vs. The Minority Shareholder's Reality

There is a deep conflict of interest at the heart of Nepal's rights issue culture that investors must understand. Promoters who control large blocks of shares and take up every rights issue keep their ownership percentage and their control. A larger company can also bring benefits that are not shared equally — bigger related-party contracts, for example. That is a potential conflict to look for in each company's disclosures, not something this book has shown to be common.

Minority shareholders, however, face a different calculation. The retail investor who cannot participate in every rights issue either faces dilution of their economic claim or is forced to keep allocating fresh capital to the same company year after year — capital that might earn better returns elsewhere. The option to exit — sell the shares — is always there, but if the company keeps raising money without lifting its earnings per share, the share price may stay weak, making exit at a good price harder.

This is not an abstract critique of any individual company or promoter group. It is a structural feature of some markets. There, capital rules, weak earning power and weak protection for minority shareholders combine. The result is a pattern of raising capital that steadily favours those with the money and information to take part over those without.

Screening for the Dilution Problem Before You Invest

The practical implication for investors is to build dilution history into pre-investment analysis. But measure it properly. Paid-up capital is only part of shareholders' equity — equity also includes retained earnings, share premium and reserves — and comparing paid-up capital growth with profit growth can mislead in either direction. A company can raise its paid-up capital fast and still lift earnings per share; another can grow profit while EPS falls because the share count grew even faster. Use six separate measures:

MeasureHow to calculateWhat it tells you
1. Ownership dilutionYour shares ÷ total shares, before and after (if you do not subscribe)How much smaller your slice of the company becomes
2. EPS dilutionProfit for ordinary shareholders ÷ weighted average shares (Nepal Accounting Standard 26 explains how rights issues are counted)Whether profit per share keeps up with the new shares
3. Book-value dilutionShareholders' equity ÷ shares outstandingAccounting equity behind each share
4. ROE dilutionNet profit ÷ average shareholders' equity (use the average, not year-end)Whether returns on the bigger equity base hold up
5. Economic dilutionYour total wealth before and after: shares, entitlement value, cash paidWhether you actually lost money, not just percentage ownership
6. Return on the new capitalExtra profit ÷ extra average equity over the following yearsWhether the new money earned enough — the question that matters most

Read the six together over five to ten years. Look at the trend in ROE: is it stable, rising, or declining? Look at the trend in EPS: is the company earning more per share today than five years ago, despite all the capital injections? Consider a company whose share count has grown sharply while EPS is flat or lower than five years ago, and whose return on the new capital is below what shareholders require. It has been destroying shareholder value rather than creating it, whatever its share price or dividends suggest.

When Rights Issues Are Value-Creating

It would be unfair and misleading to characterise all rights issues as destructive. Some capital-hungry businesses can create real shareholder value through rights issues at certain stages: a hydropower project under construction, a bank expanding into underserved areas with genuine loan demand, or an infrastructure company investing in productive assets. The condition is that the new capital earns more than the cost of equity. The framework for evaluating any specific rights issue is precisely the TERP arithmetic and the business analysis discussed in earlier lessons of this chapter.

The warning is not against rights issues per se but against the pattern of reflexive, repeated rights issuance as a solution to every capital requirement, executed regardless of the company's capacity to deploy that capital productively. The discipline an investor must bring to rights issue decisions is the same discipline a sound capital allocator brings to every deployment of capital: is this the best available use of this money, at this price, in this company?

THE INVESTOR'S CANON ON RIGHTS ISSUES Subscribe to rights issues only when you would make the same investment at TERP if you had no prior position. Let the rights lapse only when you would not. When you cannot afford the full entitlement, weigh selling some existing shares to fund what you can — entitlements are not yet traded on NEPSE, so lapsed rights are almost always a cost. Screen companies before you buy them for chronic dilution — it is the silent destroyer of NEPSE returns. And remember that the most important number in assessing any capital raise is not the amount raised, but the return on equity that the company can be expected to earn on the incremental capital for years to come.

Chapter recap

This chapter began with where Nepal's rights-issue culture came from, then worked through the arithmetic of TERP and dilution. It built a framework for the subscribe-or-not decision and looked at FPOs under Nepal's rules. Finally, it faced the dilution problem that has marked Nepal's banking sector. The analytical tools — TERP calculation, the six dilution measures, and the return earned on new capital — are portable: they apply to any rights issue or FPO you will encounter in this market or any other. The principle is equally portable: issuing shares is not the problem in itself. Value is destroyed when the new money cannot earn enough, or when the terms move value unfairly between shareholders — and that cost falls hardest on those least equipped to see it coming.
