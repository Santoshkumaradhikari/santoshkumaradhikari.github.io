WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE P/E ratio — share price divided by earnings per share: how many years of today's profit you are paying for. P/B ratio — share price divided by book value (net worth) per share. Dividend yield — cash dividend per share divided by the share price — not by the Rs 100 face value. Book value per share — equity divided by the number of shares. Weighted average shares — the share count averaged over the year, adjusted for bonus issues. Market P/E — the P/E of the whole NEPSE market, a rough thermometer of mood.

Lesson 39.1 — The P/E Ratio: How NEPSE Actually Reports It

Price divided by earnings

Open any company's page on Sharesansar or Merolagani and the first number people quote is the P/E ratio: share price divided by earnings per share (EPS). It tells you how many rupees you pay for each rupee of yearly profit — or, put another way, how many years of today's profit you are paying for. Think of buying a tea shop that earns Rs 1 lakh a year for Rs 15 lakh: you are paying 15 times its earnings.

The formula never changes. But the way Nepal's market works out each half has local twists.

  • Price is simple: the last traded price (LTP) on NEPSE at the close.
  • Earnings is where the twist lies. Platforms usually use the latest audited yearly EPS. But between annual reports, many switch to an "annualised EPS" — the last four quarters added up or, for young companies, the latest quarter multiplied by four. That is convenient but dangerous for any business with seasonal profits (Lesson 39.2).

A worked example

Take second-quarter (Poush-end) results for FY 2082/83 across a range of commercial banks:

Two things stand out:

  1. Even within commercial banking — NEPSE's most uniform, most regulated sector — P/E runs from under 8 to over 300.
  2. The outlier is not a growth story. It is an accounting quirk. NIC Asia's EPS of Rs 1.76 was squeezed by a large capital base and a weak profit quarter. Divide an ordinary price by a near-zero EPS and you get 343 times — a number that says nothing about value and everything about a tiny denominator. NIC Asia at Rs 604 is not "343 times dearer" than Nepal Bank at Rs 136 on 7.67 times; the ratio has simply broken.
KEY CONCEPT P/E = market price per share ÷ earnings per share. In Nepal, "price" is the NEPSE last traded price; "earnings" is either the latest audited yearly EPS or an annualised figure, depending on the platform and the time of year. Always check which EPS a quoted P/E uses before comparing two companies.

A single-digit P/E does not automatically mean cheap, and a triple-digit P/E does not automatically mean expensive. It means: look at the denominator first.

PRACTICAL TOOL Before trusting any P/E from a broker app or Sharesansar, click through to the EPS itself. If EPS is below about Rs 5, or the company made a loss in any of the last four quarters, treat the P/E as unreliable and switch to P/B or price-to-sales instead.

Lesson 39.2 — Why Sector P/E Differs Structurally

Thirteen markets under one index

Do not treat NEPSE as one market with one "fair" P/E. It is really thirteen sub-markets under one index, and each prices profit differently for structural reasons, not because of mistakes. It is like a bazaar where gold, vegetables and cloth all sell in the same street but at very different mark-ups.

A Sharesansar survey of every listed company shows the pattern. The data are from an earlier period, but the order of sectors — which trade rich and which cheap — has held up well since:

The levels drift. In a late-2025 survey, commercial banks averaged nearer 15.8 times, with P/B near 1.51 — still the most rationally valued part of the market even as NEPSE's overall P/E neared record levels. But the order — insurance and microfinance rich, hydropower volatile, banks and manufacturing more grounded — has stayed stable across cycles. That order is the useful lesson, more than any year's numbers.

Why banks sit at the cheap end, and hydropower sprawls

Banks: NRB's capital rules, its credit-to-deposit ceiling and its single-borrower limits (the fixed Rs 25 crore ceilings were removed in October 2025) cap how fast a bank's balance sheet — and profit — can grow. Investors know this, so they will not pay insurance-style multiples for bank-style growth.

Hydropower: a run-of-river plant's output swings with the monsoon, and a newly opened plant reports a tiny, almost accidental profit in its first full year before reaching normal output. Divide a normal price by that accidental first-year profit and you get a huge, meaningless P/E.

A live example of the hydropower trap

Upper Tamakoshi has traded at a P/E near 114 in recent periods — not because anyone expects its profit to grow 114-fold, but because a particular reporting period caught unusually thin profit relative to its size. Compare Chilime Hydropower, which has paid dividends since FY 2060/61, with an average payout around 29 percent and a peak near 70 percent. Its profits have settled into a steadier, more readable pattern. Same sector, very different reliability of the P/E — purely because of age and where each sits in the monsoon cycle.

WARNING Never annualise one quarter's EPS for a hydropower company without checking which months it covers. A plant earns most of its yearly revenue in the monsoon (roughly Asar to Ashwin). Multiplying a dry-season quarter by four understates the true yearly EPS and overstates the P/E — sometimes two or three times over.

Lesson 39.3 — P/B Ratio and Why It Matters Most for Banks

Price against net worth

Price-to-book (P/B) compares the share price with book value per share — shareholders' equity (net worth) divided by the number of shares. For most factories and traders, book value says little about real worth, because plant, stock and goodwill are recorded at old cost and say little about earning power.

Banks are the exception. A bank's assets — loans, investments, deposits — are financial items that NRB's provisioning, capital and disclosure rules force it to carry reasonably close to reality. That is why P/B means more for a bank than for a hydropower company or a trading house: you can trust the denominator.

BankP/B (×)NPL (%)Reading
Everest Bank (EBL)5.620.68Premium multiple, justified by very clean loans
Kumari Bank (KBL)2.376.92Middle multiple; a weaker loan book explains the discount
Nepal Bank (NBL)1.845.34Older state-linked bank; has at times traded below book value

This is not random. A bank with bad loans under 1 percent (Everest) earns a much higher P/B than one with about ten times as many (Kumari), because the market rightly suspects Kumari's book value overstates what its loans will really collect. That is P/B's most useful job on NEPSE: checking whether a bank's book value can be trusted, read alongside its NPL ratio, capital ratio and provision coverage in the same quarterly report.

KEY CONCEPT P/B = market price per share ÷ book value per share, where book value per share = (total shareholders' equity − preference capital) ÷ ordinary shares outstanding. Below 1.0 means the market values the company below its accounting net worth — worth investigating, not automatically buying. Above about 3.0 without a clear, lasting growth or ROE story is a sign to check whether you are paying for hype rather than earnings.

Working it out

A bank has total equity — paid-up capital plus reserves and retained profit — of Rs 18 arba, and 12 crore shares. Book value per share is Rs 18,00,00,00,000 ÷ 12,00,00,000 = Rs 150. At a share price of Rs 270, P/B is 1.8.

Now compare its return on equity (ROE). A bank earning 16–18 percent on equity arguably deserves to trade above book, because it grows shareholders' money faster than one earning 8–10 percent. P/B without ROE is an incomplete comparison: read the two together. It is like valuing two buffaloes: the one that gives more milk deserves a higher price, even if both cost the same to buy.

CASE IN POINT In banking, P/B below 1.0 together with an NPL ratio above 4–5 percent and capital near the regulatory floor is a classic value trap: the share looks cheap on book value, but the book value itself is damaged by loan problems the ratio cannot see.

Lesson 39.4 — Dividend Yield, Nepal-Style: The Face-Value Trap

Two different "dividend percentages"

Everywhere else, dividend yield means the yearly dividend per share divided by the current market price. In Nepal, the number that circulates — in AGM notices, newspaper tables and WhatsApp groups — is something else: the dividend as a percentage of face value, which for almost every NEPSE company is Rs 100.

This is not a technicality. It is the most common confusion for new NEPSE investors, and it leads straight to overpaying for "high-yield" shares that are nothing of the sort.

CompanyDividend declaredType
Unilever Nepal (UNL)1,842%Cash
Nepal Telecom (NTC)30%Cash
Nepal Life Insurance (NLIC)21.05%5% bonus + 16.05% cash
Standard Chartered Bank Nepal (SCB)19%Cash
Agricultural Development Bank (ADBL)13%3.25% bonus + 9.75% cash
Nabil Bank (NABIL)12.5%Cash

Unilever Nepal's "1,842%" looks absurd until you remember it is 1,842 percent of Rs 100 — that is, Rs 1,842 cash per share — on a share that trades in the tens of thousands of rupees because it has very few shares and has issued almost no bonus shares in decades. The face-value percentage and the real yield on market price can differ ten times or more.

Turning the headline into a real yield

Nabil Bank declared 12.5 percent cash — Rs 12.50 per share. At a price of about Rs 546:

Rs 12.50 ÷ Rs 546 = 2.29%

Standard Chartered Nepal declared 19 percent cash — Rs 19 per share — at a price of about Rs 628:

Rs 19 ÷ Rs 628 = 3.03%

Both are unremarkable once converted — in the 2–3 percent range typical of Nepal's larger banks, and in line with NEPSE's market-wide yield of only 1–2 percent when the index is expensive. That is a world away from the "12.5%" or "19%" skimmed off a newspaper table. It is like a shop advertising "50% profit on cost price": what matters to you is the return on what you pay.

CAUTION A dividend percentage in an AGM notice, newspaper or brokerage app is almost always on the Rs 100 face value, not on the price you would pay. Before treating it as income, divide the rupee amount (percentage × Rs 100 ÷ 100) by the current market price to get the true yield.

Bonus is not income

The distortion cuts both ways. A thinly traded, high-priced share can declare a modest-looking percentage and still give a decent true yield, while a low-priced, bonus-heavy share can declare a big-looking percentage and yield under 2 percent.

Bonus shares add more confusion. When ADBL declares "13% (3.25% bonus + 9.75% cash)", only the cash part is income. The bonus part is more shares, which dilute future EPS and tend to pull the price down about proportionally on the ex-date. Treating a bonus percentage as cash yield is a second, related mistake.

Lesson 39.5 — EPS Mechanics: Weighted Average Shares and the Bonus Share Adjustment

Get the share count right

Every ratio in this chapter depends on the number of shares. Get it wrong, and everything built on it is wrong. Nepal's heavy, repeated use of bonus shares makes this the most important mechanical detail in the chapter.

Under NFRS (following international rules), EPS uses the weighted average number of shares during the year — not just the count on the last day — because rights issues, bonus issues and mergers change the share count mid-year. Rights and bonus shares are treated completely differently, and mixing them up is the most common error.

Rights shares are time-weighted; bonus shares go back in time

  • Rights issue: new cash comes in for new shares on a specific date. Because real money entered only from that date, the new shares count only for the part of the year after it. A rights issue completed with three months left adds only a quarter-year of dilution.
  • Bonus issue: existing reserves become new shares; no new money comes in. Because nothing real changed, the rules treat bonus shares as if they had always existed — restating last year's EPS on the same enlarged share count, so growth comparisons stay fair.

A worked example

A company earns Rs 40 crore this year and starts with 1 crore shares. During the year it issues a 10 percent bonus (10 lakh new shares). Last year it earned Rs 35 crore.

Wrong way: this year Rs 40 crore ÷ 1.1 crore shares, but last year left at Rs 35 crore ÷ 1 crore shares. Comparing an unadjusted small old share count with a bigger new one distorts the growth rate.

Right way (NFRS): both years on the post-bonus 1.1 crore shares.

Last year (restated): Rs 35 crore ÷ 1.1 crore = Rs 31.82
This year: Rs 40 crore ÷ 1.1 crore = Rs 36.36
Real growth: about 14.3 percent

REGULATORY DETAIL NRB's 2015 directive raising commercial banks' minimum paid-up capital to Rs 8 arba set off a wave of bonus issues across banking between about 2015 and 2018, as banks turned reserves into capital instead of raising new cash. Any historical EPS or P/E comparison across that period must use EPS correctly restated for the bonus shares. Many casual comparisons online do not, and show a false "profit collapse" that is only a change in the denominator.

Three questions for a strange EPS

Back to NIC Asia's 343 times P/E on Rs 1.76 EPS. Whenever EPS looks unusually low against a company's history and peers, ask first:

  1. Was there a recent bonus or rights issue that grew the share count faster than profit?
  2. Was there a merger or acquisition that reset the share count?
  3. Is the reporting period annualised correctly?

Only after answering these should you conclude that the business itself has weakened.

CASE IN POINT A P/E of 343, as briefly appeared for NIC Asia, does not mean the market expects extraordinary growth. It almost certainly means the EPS was temporarily and mechanically depressed. Check any triple-digit P/E against the raw EPS before drawing any conclusion about value.

Lesson 39.6 — NEPSE's Market-Wide P/E as a Sentiment Barometer

The market's own thermometer

A company's P/E tells you about that company. NEPSE's market-wide P/E — the weighted average across all listed companies, published by NEPSE and tracked by Sharesansar — tells you where investor mood sits in the market's own history, and how Nepal compares with its neighbours. Think of it as a thermometer for the whole market's fever.

  • At the speculative peak of 2021, NEPSE's P/E reached about 42.28 — in hindsight an unsustainable extreme, followed by a multi-year fall and later recovery.
  • In early 2026 it was back to about 38, with market P/B near 2.78, dividend yield of just 1–2 percent and market value about 72.6 percent of GDP — all near the top of NEPSE's own historical range.

The pattern is striking. At about 38, NEPSE trades at nearly three times the frontier-market average it belongs to, and two to five times Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and Pakistan. Its dividend yield of 1–2 percent is below every one of them; several pay 3–7 percent. A market can be expensive on earnings and stingy on income at the same time. That combination signals a market carried more by hopes of price rises and by spare cash than by the income investors are actually paid today.

Why NEPSE stays so expensive

The reasons are structural, not about better businesses:

  • few tradable shares compared with demand from a large domestic retail base;
  • few other places to put savings — property is slow and costly to trade, capital controls block investing abroad, and fixed-deposit rates fall when liquidity is plentiful;
  • retail trading driven by price momentum and rumour as much as by earnings.

None of this means the multiple must correct tomorrow; Nepal has sustained rich multiples for long periods. But a market P/E near 38 — close to the level that came before the 2021 fall — is information a careful investor should not ignore, even while still buying individual shares that look cheap on the sector and company tools in this chapter.

CASE IN POINT Read NEPSE's P/E against its own history (about 38 now, against the 2021 peak of 42.28) and against neighbours (Bangladesh 10.1, Sri Lanka about 11, Pakistan 8.7) at the same time. The first shows where Nepal stands against its own past excess; the second shows how much of today's valuation is Nepal-specific enthusiasm rather than a shared regional re-rating.

The practical lesson is not to sell everything when the market P/E crosses some magic line — no precise trigger exists. It is to demand a bigger margin of safety on new purchases as the market multiple rises, to lean on the sector and company tools in Lessons 39.1 to 39.5 rather than market momentum, and to remember that the last time NEPSE's P/E was this high, those who bought at the peak on faith rather than earnings waited years to recover.

Chapter recap

  • P/E = price ÷ EPS. Check whether the EPS is audited yearly or annualised. A tiny EPS breaks the ratio (NIC Asia's 343 times); if EPS is under about Rs 5 or there was a recent loss, use P/B or price-to-sales.
  • Sectors price profit differently for structural reasons: insurance and microfinance rich, hydropower volatile, banks and manufacturing grounded. Never annualise a dry-season hydropower quarter.
  • P/B matters most for banks, whose book value is close to reality. Read it with the NPL ratio, capital and ROE; P/B below 1 with high NPLs is a value trap.
  • NEPSE "dividend %" is on the Rs 100 face value. True yield = rupee dividend ÷ market price (Nabil 2.29%, SCB 3.03%). Bonus shares are not income.
  • EPS uses the weighted average share count: rights shares count from their date; bonus shares are treated as always existing, and last year's EPS is restated.
  • NEPSE's market P/E (about 38 in early 2026, against 42.28 at the 2021 peak) is far above regional peers, with a yield of only 1–2%. Treat a high market multiple as a reason for a bigger margin of safety.
