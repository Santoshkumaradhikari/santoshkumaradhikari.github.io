WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Project finance — lending against one project's own cash flows and assets, usually through a separate company (an SPV). Non-recourse — lenders cannot chase the sponsors' other assets if the project fails. Debt-equity ratio — the share of project cost funded by loans versus owners' money, such as 70:30. Syndication (consortium) — several banks lending together, led by one bank, like villagers pooling money for one big irrigation canal. Single obligor limit — a cap on how much one bank may lend to one borrower group; NRB removed it in October 2025. DSRA — debt service reserve account: cash kept aside to cover loan repayments in a bad season.

Lesson 42.1 — What Project Finance Actually Means

Two ways to borrow for a flat

Picture a Kathmandu family that owns the house it lives in, plus a small flat bought to rent out. It wants to buy a second rental flat, and has two ways to borrow.

Way one: borrow from a bank against everything the family owns — home, savings, gold, the existing flat. If the new flat is a bad investment and the rent does not cover the loan, the bank can still come after the family home. This is corporate finance (balance-sheet lending): all the borrower's assets and income stand behind the loan.

Way two: set up a separate small company that owns only the new flat. That company borrows in its own name, using only the flat and its future rent as security. If the rent cannot repay the loan, the bank's claim stops at the flat. The family home, the parents' pension and the children's school fund are untouched. The lender agreed from day one to be repaid only from what this one asset earns.

Way two is project finance: financing a long-term infrastructure or industrial project mainly on the project's own expected cash flows, rather than on its owners' balance sheets, with the project's assets, rights and revenue contracts as collateral.

The company that owns the project is a special purpose vehicle (SPV): a separate company created to build, own and run one project and nothing else. It has its own board, bank accounts and contracts — and its own limited liability, so its creditors cannot reach into the pockets of the shareholders who set it up.

KEY CONCEPT Non-recourse financing means that if the project fails, the lender can claim only the project's own assets and cash — nothing from the sponsor's other businesses or personal wealth. Limited-recourse financing, the more common real-world form, gives the lender no general claim on the sponsor, but narrow claims for set events — usually cost overruns during construction, fraud, or a shortfall if the project is abandoned before completion. Almost every hydropower financing in Nepal is limited-recourse, because banks insist on some sponsor support during the risky construction period.

Why lenders accept it

Why would a lender agree to be repaid from one asset's earnings rather than everything the borrower owns? Because in return it gets something a corporate loan rarely offers: close control over the asset itself.

Before a single rupee is lent, the lender's lawyers and engineers examine every contract the project depends on — construction, sale of electricity, insurance, land. Then the lender wraps the project in a security package — legal rights over its assets, contracts and cash — so tight that, if the project stumbles, the lender can step in and run it, sell it or hand it to someone who can make it pay, without ever touching the sponsor's other assets.

Why hydropower uses it

That is why project finance dominates hydropower in Nepal, and large infrastructure worldwide. A single 100 MW plant can cost tens of arba rupees. No promoter group, however rich, wants to stake its whole fortune on one river's flow, one government's tariff policy and one buyer's ability to pay. And no bank wants to lend that much against a promoter's general promise; it wants a contractual claim on the one thing that will actually produce cash — the plant and its electricity sales.

WARNING "No recourse to the sponsor" does not mean "no risk to the sponsor". Sponsors still lose all their equity — often 25–30 percent of project cost — if the project fails. Project finance limits the downside to the equity invested; it does not remove it.

Lesson 42.2 — The Anatomy of a Nepali Hydropower Deal

Step 1: the SPV and its licences

It starts with the SPV. A group of promoters — perhaps local entrepreneurs, a construction house and sometimes a foreign strategic investor — registers a company under the Companies Act whose only purpose is to develop, own and run one hydropower project on one stretch of river. The company applies to the Department of Electricity Development (DoED) for a survey licence and then a generation licence under the Electricity Act. Every later contract, loan and approval attaches to this one company.

Step 2: the project contracts

The SPV then assembles the "project contracts" — the web of agreements that turns a hole in a riverbed into a bankable business:

  1. Power purchase agreement (PPA): a long-term contract (usually 20–35 years in Nepal, sometimes on build-own-operate-transfer, "BOOT", terms) under which the Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA) agrees to buy all the power at an agreed tariff — usually different rates for wet and dry seasons, and sometimes for peak and off-peak hours. It is the most important document in the whole financing, because it turns falling water into predictable cash that banks can lend against.
  2. Engineering, procurement and construction (EPC) contract: a contractor — often a Chinese, Indian or Nepali construction house — agrees to build the powerhouse, dam or weir, headrace tunnel or canal, and penstock for a fixed price by a fixed date, usually with liquidated damages (pre-agreed cash penalties) for delay.
  3. Land and government agreements: the survey and generation licences, and often a Project Development Agreement with the Government of Nepal covering royalty, local benefit-sharing and, for larger projects, tax and foreign-exchange terms.
  4. Insurance: cover for construction risks (fire, flood, contractor default) and operating risks (business interruption, machinery breakdown), assigned to the lenders as part of their security.

Step 3: how much is borrowed

With these contracts nearly final, the SPV goes to the banks, and the split between borrowed money and owners' money is fixed.

KEY CONCEPT The debt-equity ratio in project finance is the share of project cost paid by loans against the share paid by owners. 70:30 means 70 percent from bank loans and 30 percent from the promoters' own money. Nepali hydropower usually runs 70:30 to 75:25 — so promoters need find only a quarter to a third of the cost.

Why do lenders accept so much debt for hydropower? Because an operating plant with a PPA has an unusually predictable, long-lived income: the water keeps flowing, the NEA keeps needing power, and the price is fixed by contract for decades. That predictability lets debt be stacked heavily on modest equity — the opposite of a restaurant or trading business, where income swings and banks rarely go beyond 50:50.

Typical terms, from recent IPO prospectuses and credit-rating reports of NEPSE hydropower companies:

CASE IN POINT Chilime Hydropower, a 22.1 MW plant on the Chilime Khola in Rasuwa and one of the NEA's earliest subsidiaries, is often cited as Nepal's pioneering project-company model: an NEA-controlled SPV that later opened its shares to the public and specifically to local Rasuwa residents, long before hydropower IPOs became routine. Many later hydropower SPVs have followed its template of a fixed local-resident quota alongside public and promoter shares.

Lesson 42.3 — Financing the Deal: Syndication and the Single-Obligor Constraint

Projects bigger than any bank

A number worth sitting with: by recent industry estimates, the combined core capital of all Nepal's commercial banks is about Rs 800 arba (roughly USD 5.5–6 billion), judging by their own reported net worth. Core capital — paid-up capital plus reserves — is the base regulators use to size how much one bank can safely lend to one borrower.

Meanwhile, Nepal has issued generation licences for well over 10,000 MW, with more than 3,000 MW already installed. One large storage or peaking project of 400–900 MW can cost well over Rs 100 arba. No single Nepali bank — often not even a handful together — could write that cheque alone.

Syndication: many banks, one loan

So syndication is not optional in Nepali hydropower; it is a structural necessity. Several banks jointly provide one loan to one borrower, sharing the amount, the collateral and the risk under one set of documents — like villagers pooling money to dig one large irrigation canal that no single family could afford.

A lead arranger structures the deal and invites others to take a share on the same terms. Nepal mainly uses the consortium model: each bank lends directly and holds its own claim on the SPV for its share, with a lead bank coordinating the security documents and cash monitoring for all.

REGULATORY DETAIL NRB long enforced a single obligor limit (SOL) — a ceiling on how much one bank could lend to one borrower or group of related borrowers without NRB's specific approval. It was usually described as a share of the bank's core capital (25 percent for fund-based lending); news reports of the 2025 change also describe an NPR 25 crore threshold above which lending to one borrower group needed NRB's approval. Either way, it was small next to a large hydropower project's needs — which is exactly why syndication became the default for anything beyond a small run-of-river scheme.

The 2025 change

In October 2025 (FY 2082/83), NRB removed the fixed Rs 25 crore single-obligor ceiling, above which a loan to one borrower or group had needed NRB's approval. Each bank now sets its own borrower limits under its credit-risk framework and board-approved policies. Whether the separate limit tied to each bank's core capital still applies is not settled in the reports; check NRB's current Unified Directives. The stated aim was to unlock financing for "mega projects" — large hydropower, transmission and infrastructure — that the old ceiling held back, while banks stay accountable to their own risk governance and to NRB's wider supervision. Capital rules, sector concentration limits and loan classification rules all remain.

WARNING Removing the single-obligor limit does not remove NRB's other, arguably more binding, sector limits. Banks still face a cap on their total exposure to hydropower — commonly cited at about 50 percent of core capital. A bank with plenty of room under the new single-borrower rule may still be limited by how much of its whole book it may put into hydropower. Always check both limits.

Why syndication will continue anyway

Even without the ceiling, syndication remains the norm for big projects, for a reason unrelated to regulation: spreading risk. No bank's board wants all of a Rs 50–100 arba exposure riding on one river, one contractor and one buyer. Spread across six, eight or a dozen banks, an underperforming project threatens no single bank, and any workout — restructuring a troubled loan — becomes a shared negotiation rather than a life-or-death event for one lender.

Two Nepali features

  1. The lenders are mostly Nepali. Lenders are almost always Nepali commercial banks and, increasingly, provident and pension funds such as the Employees Provident Fund (EPF) and Citizen Investment Trust (CIT). Because the NEA's PPA is in rupees, foreign banks will not take open rupee risk on a 15-year loan without a hedge — and Nepal's shallow currency markets offer none.
  2. Big national projects bring in governments and development banks. For the largest national-priority projects, the Government of Nepal and multilateral development banks (World Bank, Asian Development Bank) — and sometimes bilateral lenders such as India's Exim Bank or Indian public-sector lenders — join as direct lenders, guarantors or co-financiers, because the domestic system alone cannot carry the exposure.
CASE IN POINT For Tamakoshi V, planned as a follow-on to the 456 MW Upper Tamakoshi plant, the NEA, the Employees Provident Fund and the Tamakoshi hydropower company signed a three-way loan agreement in 2023. It shows Nepal's biggest pools of long-term savings — provident and pension funds — being drawn directly into hydropower lending alongside, or even instead of, commercial banks.

Lesson 42.4 — The Lender's Security Package

Once the banks agree to lend, what exactly do they hold if the SPV cannot repay? In ordinary corporate lending, the answer is often a mortgage on land and buildings plus personal guarantees. In project finance, the lenders rely almost entirely on the project's future cash, so they build layers of legal control that let them step into the project's shoes if things go wrong — well before the SPV is formally insolvent.

1. Assignment of the PPA

The NEA's payments under the PPA are the project's only real income. So lenders require the SPV to assign its PPA rights to them (or to a security agent for the group). Lenders do not sell electricity day to day; but if the SPV defaults, they gain the right to step into the PPA, receive payments directly, or even take over running the plant (a "step-in right") to keep the PPA alive rather than let the NEA end it. The NEA's formal consent to this assignment is a standard condition before the loan is released.

2. The escrow account and the cash waterfall

An escrow account is a special bank account, usually with the lead lender or security agent, into which all the project's income (the NEA's payments) is paid directly. Cash leaves it only in a set order — the cash waterfall, like water filling one pot before overflowing into the next:

  1. operating expenses;
  2. scheduled loan repayments (principal and interest);
  3. topping up the debt service reserve account if it has been used;
  4. other reserves, such as a major-maintenance reserve;
  5. only then, dividends to shareholders.

Management never has free access to incoming cash. The bank sees every rupee arrive and controls the order it leaves — the single most powerful tool lenders have to be paid before shareholders.

PRACTICAL TOOL When reading a NEPSE hydropower company's bond prospectus, annual report or rating report, look for the details of its escrow and waterfall. A company that releases dividends only after fully funding its DSRA and maintenance reserve is meaningfully safer for lenders — and for minority shareholders relying on steady dividends — than one with a thin or loosely enforced waterfall.

3. The debt service reserve account (DSRA)

A DSRA is a ring-fenced cash reserve — funded from the loan up front or built from early cash — big enough to cover a set number of months of loan payments, commonly three to six. If the plant has a bad month — a landslide damages the intake, the river runs low, a transformer fails — the SPV can draw on it to keep paying on time instead of defaulting at once. It must be refilled before any dividend, which is why it sits ahead of shareholders in the waterfall.

KEY CONCEPT Think of the DSRA as the project's emergency fund — kept not for the shareholders' comfort but purely for the lenders' protection. It is like a landlord who insists a tenant keep two months' rent in a locked account before moving in, so one bad month does not break the lease.

4. Everything else

A full Nepali hydropower security package usually also includes:

  • a first-ranking mortgage or charge over all project land, the powerhouse, penstock and generating equipment;
  • a pledge of the promoters' shares in the SPV, so lenders can take control of the company itself, not just chase physical assets;
  • assignment of all insurance policies, so payouts after a loss go to repairs or the lenders, not to the sponsor's other businesses;
  • assignment of the EPC contract and any performance bonds or delay penalties, so lenders can pursue the contractor directly for defects or delay.
CAUTION A security package is only as strong as its weakest link, and in Nepal the weak link is the PPA. Domestic-investment hydropower PPAs usually give the developer no compensation if the NEA ends the agreement — only the right to "wheel" (transmit) power through the NEA's network — whereas PPAs for foreign-invested projects usually include termination compensation. Assigning a weak contract to lenders does not make it strong.

Lesson 42.5 — Construction Risk vs Operational Risk: Why Financing Terms Change Over the Project's Life

The hinge: commercial operation

Every project financing has a hinge: the commercial operation date (COD), when the plant is finished, tested and earning. The loan terms, the security and the sponsor's role all shift at that moment.

  • Before COD — construction risk: Will the contractor finish on time and on budget? Will unexpected rock in the tunnel cause months of delay? Will a monsoon flood wash away a temporary dam? The project earns nothing, so there is no cash to repay lenders and no track record to judge. This is the riskiest period of the project's life.
  • After COD — operational risk: Will the river behave as the feasibility study predicted? Will the machines run reliably? Will the NEA pay on time? These risks are far more predictable and better insured, because there is now a record of generating power and receiving PPA payments.
KEY CONCEPT During construction, the lender's collateral is an unfinished hole in a hillside with no income — worth little to anyone if the sponsor walks away. After COD, it is a working, earning asset with a long-term contracted buyer. Lenders will accept almost pure non-recourse terms for the operating phase, but almost never for construction. That is why nearly every Nepali hydropower loan is in practice "limited recourse": sponsor support is required exactly when the asset alone is not yet bankable.

How lenders bridge the gap

  • Completion guarantees and cost-overrun undertakings. Sponsors usually promise to pay any cost overrun themselves, up to a cap, and sometimes to guarantee completion — if the SPV cannot finish, the sponsor must fund completion or repay the loan. This is the "limited" in limited recourse: limited in time (construction only) and in scope (overruns and completion, not general underperformance).
  • Standby equity or letters of credit. Instead of putting in extra cash at once, sponsors often arrange a standby facility drawn only if an overrun actually happens, so their money can work elsewhere until needed.
  • An independent engineer. Lenders appoint their own technical consultant — paid by the borrower but reporting to the lenders — to check progress, certify milestones before each loan instalment is released, and flag delays or overruns early.
  • Payments tied to progress. Loan instalments are released against verified milestones (foundations done, penstock installed, turbines delivered), not as a lump sum, so lenders never fund far ahead of what has actually been built.

After COD: the DSCR

Once COD is reached and output has been stable for an agreed test period, the loan "converts" to its long-term repayment schedule, and the main covenant becomes the debt service coverage ratio (DSCR): cash available for debt in a period divided by the debt payments due.

A DSCR of 1.3 means the project earned 30 percent more cash than it needed for that period's payment — the cushion against a bad water year. If DSCR falls below the minimum (commonly 1.2–1.3 in Nepal), the loan agreement usually blocks dividends until it recovers — the waterfall again protecting lenders ahead of shareholders.

PRACTICAL TOOL For any Nepali hydropower company, ask first which phase it is in. Before COD, it carries construction risk (delays, overruns, uncertain water) and earns nothing; its shares are closer to a bet on successful completion. After several years of stable generation, it carries mainly operating and regulatory risk, and its shares behave more like a long-dated infrastructure asset with contracted income. The risk premium and the right valuation method differ sharply between the two.

Lesson 42.6 — Case Studies: Learning from Nepal's Hydropower Financings

Upper Tamakoshi (456 MW): financed at home

Nepal's largest operating plant, commissioned in July 2021, broke the pattern of foreign-financed mega-projects. It was developed by Upper Tamakoshi Hydropower Limited, an SPV set up by the NEA in 2007, and financed entirely by Nepali institutions and companies, without foreign commercial lenders.

Its ownership blends public and community interests: the NEA holds 41 percent; Nepal Telecom, Citizen Investment Trust and Rastriya Beema Sansthan hold smaller stakes; 10 percent is reserved for residents of Dolakha, where the project sits; 15 percent for the general public; and the rest for EPF contributors and NEA, company and financial-institution staff. It shows that a truly large plant can be financed at home when provident funds, state institutions and public shareholders come together at enough scale (its later debt and governance strains are in Chapter 32).

Chilime (22.1 MW): the community template

An earlier, smaller NEA subsidiary in Rasuwa, Chilime is often cited as the original "community-inclusive" project company, and became one of the first hydropower companies on NEPSE, with shares reserved for local people affected by the project. The local-quota-plus-public-tranche model is now close to a market norm.

Tamakoshi V: pension funds as lenders

The follow-on to Upper Tamakoshi shows the recent trend of provident funds lending directly, not just investing in shares: the NEA, the EPF and the Tamakoshi hydropower company signed a three-way loan agreement in 2023, alongside conventional bank debt.

Arun-3 (900 MW): financed abroad

Arun-3 shows the opposite end. It is a cross-border, foreign-sponsored project developed by SJVN (a joint venture of the Government of India and Himachal Pradesh), and most of its debt — reported at about INR 6,333 crore — came from Indian lenders, not Nepali banks. When a project's sponsor and sales point outward (a foreign sponsor exporting to India), its financing naturally follows.

Rasuwagadhi: reading a rating report

ICRA Nepal's IPO grading of Rasuwagadhi Hydropower shows how Nepal's rating agencies judge bankability before an IPO, using exactly the factors in this chapter: PPA quality and the NEA's payment record, water and technical risk, the debt-equity structure and security package, and construction versus operating risk. Reading an IPO grading report for any NEPSE-bound hydropower company is one of the quickest ways to see project finance analysis done by professionals.

CASE IN POINT The largest, most successfully home-financed projects (Upper Tamakoshi, and increasingly Tamakoshi V) share three features: an NEA-linked or NEA-majority SPV (giving confidence to banks and retail shareholders), a broad lending group of commercial banks and provident or pension funds rather than one lender, and a meaningful local and public shareholding that spreads ownership — and political support — beyond the promoters. Projects lacking one or more of these have usually struggled more to reach financial closure.

Two lasting weaknesses

One sobering thread runs through Nepal's hydropower financing history: gaps in bankability that persist despite a generally sound legal framework.

  1. Currency risk. The NEA will not sign dollar PPAs for domestic projects, and Nepal has no real market for hedging long-term rupee exposure. So foreign commercial lenders rarely lend directly to domestic-PPA projects, leaving the field to Nepali banks, provident funds and development institutions willing to lend in or alongside rupees.
  2. Unequal termination terms. As Lesson 42.4 showed, domestic-investment PPAs usually leave a developer with no compensation, only wheeling rights, if the NEA terminates, while foreign-investment PPAs usually include compensation. Nepali legal commentators flag this as an unresolved weakness for purely domestic developers and their lenders.
CAUTION Whether you are a lender, a bondholder or a NEPSE shareholder, never assume the PPA alone makes the income safe. Read the termination and compensation clauses. A "take-or-pay" PPA that looks airtight on its cover page can contain loopholes or one-sided termination rights that change the real credit quality of the cash flow the whole financing depends on.

Chapter recap

  • Project finance lends against one project's own cash flows and assets, held in a separate SPV. Nepali hydropower loans are limited-recourse: sponsors support the construction period; sponsors' equity is still fully at risk.
  • A deal is built from the SPV, its DoED licences and the project contracts — the PPA (most important), the EPC contract, government agreements and insurance. Typical terms: 70:30 to 75:25 debt to equity, 12–15-year loans with a 3–5-year construction holiday, and a minimum DSCR of 1.2–1.3.
  • Projects are bigger than any bank, so banks lend in consortiums. NRB removed the fixed single-obligor limit in October 2025, but sector limits (around 50% of core capital for hydropower) remain, and banks still share risk.
  • Lenders' security package: PPA assignment with step-in rights, an escrow account and cash waterfall, a DSRA, mortgages, a pledge of promoter shares, and assignment of insurance and the EPC contract. The PPA's termination terms are the weak link.
  • Risk shifts at COD: construction risk is managed with completion guarantees, standby equity, an independent engineer and milestone payments; after COD, the DSCR covenant controls dividends.
  • Cases: Upper Tamakoshi (home-financed), Chilime (community template), Tamakoshi V (pension-fund lenders), Arun-3 (financed abroad). Lasting weaknesses: no rupee hedging and unequal PPA termination terms.
