WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Relative valuation (comps) — valuing a company by comparing its multiples with similar companies, like pricing a house by recent sales on the same street. Multiple — a ratio such as P/E or P/B. Peer group — the set of genuinely similar companies used for comparison. EV/EBITDA — enterprise value (shares plus debt minus cash) divided by earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. Thin float — few shares actually available to trade. Justified multiple — the multiple a company deserves given its ROE, growth and cost of equity.

Lesson 47.1 — The Neighbourhood Comparison: What Relative Valuation Really Means

Pricing a house in Baneshwor

Imagine you want a fair asking price for a house in Baneshwor. You would not compare it with a Budhanilkantha bungalow on three times the land, or an apartment in another city. You would look for houses of similar size, age and road access, sold in the last few months, in the same or a nearby ward.

If those houses sold for about NPR 2.5 crore, and yours is similar in every important way, NPR 2.5 crore becomes your anchor. You might adjust up or down for a bigger plot, a busier road or a better view. But the starting point is what similar things actually sold for.

That is relative valuation

That is the whole logic of relative valuation, or "comps" (short for comparables). Chapter 46's DCF built a value from scratch by forecasting every future cash flow and discounting it to today. Relative valuation asks a simpler question: what is the market paying now for businesses like this one, and what does that tell us about what this one should be worth?

You built the tools in Chapters 39 to 41:

  • P/E (price-to-earnings) — share price ÷ earnings per share: how many years of current profit you are paying for;
  • P/B (price-to-book) — share price ÷ book value per share: how much you pay for each rupee of accounting net worth;
  • EV/EBITDA — enterprise value ÷ earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation: looks at the whole company, debt included, not just the shares;
  • dividend yield — yearly dividend per share ÷ share price: the cash return just for holding the share.

Relative valuation is what you do with these ratios. You stop looking at one company's multiple alone and start asking, "compared with what?"

KEY CONCEPT Relative valuation (comps) values a company by comparing its trading multiples — P/E, P/B, EV/EBITDA, dividend yield — against two reference points: (1) a group of similar peer companies, and (2) the company's own historical average multiple. A multiple only means something once you have something to compare it with.

Two kinds of comparison

This chapter uses two kinds of comparison side by side. Nepali investors often blur them, so separate them from the start.

  1. Cross-sectional: across companies, at one moment. If Nabil Bank trades at a P/B of 2.1x and Global IME Bank at 1.3x, and both are large, well-run banks with similar business models, that gap tells you how the market prices one bank against the other right now.
  2. Time-series: one company against its own past. If Chilime Hydropower has averaged a P/E of 18x over five years and now trades at 30x, the market is unusually optimistic (or pessimistic) compared with its own recent history. That does not automatically mean overvalued — something may truly have changed: a new plant coming online, a tariff revision, lower interest rates making steady utility cash more attractive. But it flags a gap worth investigating.

A seasoned analyst uses both. A bank in line with its peers but far above its own five-year average tells a different story from one far below its peers but in line with its history. Treating "cheap compared with its past" as the same as "cheap compared with peers" is a quiet error that creeps into otherwise careful work.

Why comps are popular

  • Fast. You can screen all nineteen listed commercial banks on P/E and P/B in an afternoon, using data from NEPSE, Sharesansar or Merolagani, without a full model for each.
  • Reflects the market's mood. That matters, because in the end you sell your shares to that same market, not to a spreadsheet.
  • Intuitive. "This bank is cheaper than that bank" is a sentence anyone understands at once.

The built-in weakness

Relative valuation has one weakness built into its logic: it assumes the peer group is, on average, priced correctly.

If every commercial bank in Nepal is overpriced at once because retail money flooded into NEPSE in a bull run, a bank that looks "cheap compared with its peers" may just be the least overpriced share in an overpriced sector — not a truly good buy. It is like finding the cheapest plot in an area where all land prices have doubled on rumours of a new road. Relative valuation tells you relative position, not absolute worth.

Keep that sentence in mind. It returns throughout this chapter, and it is why Lesson 47.5 introduces the "justified multiple", worked out from fundamentals instead of borrowed from the crowd.

Lesson 47.2 — Building the Right Peer Group on a Concentrated Exchange

A small, lopsided market

Choosing comparable houses is easy because most cities have thousands. Choosing comparable listed companies on NEPSE is much harder. The exchange is small, concentrated and lopsided in ways most textbooks — written with New York or Bombay in mind, where any industry has dozens or hundreds of companies — do not prepare you for.

By mid-July 2026, NEPSE listed 302 companies, up from 272 a year earlier (NRB annual report, as reported by Insurance Khabar). But they are spread very unevenly:

  • Banks and financial institutions (commercial banks, development banks, finance companies and microfinance) plus hydropower make up the large majority of listed companies and a big share of market value.
  • Insurance is a meaningful third group.
  • Manufacturing, trading, hotels and "others" make up the rest, often with only a handful of names each.

So the peer group for a mid-sized commercial bank might be all 17–20 other commercial banks — a wide set. The peer group for a niche business (the single listed cigarette maker, or the single listed cement producer of a certain type) might be one company, or none.

That means building a peer group on NEPSE needs more judgment and more care than on a larger exchange. Your choices are narrower, and the temptation to stretch "comparable" to fill a table is stronger.

Start with the economic engine

Begin with the rule from Chapter 39: deep structural differences between sectors matter more than surface likeness. A peer group should share the same basic economic engine, not just the same exchange or the same market size. For NEPSE's two big clusters:

Commercial banks should be compared with other commercial banks — ideally of similar size and business mix. Large, well-capitalised banks such as Nabil, Global IME, Nepal Investment Mega and NIC Asia sit at a different scale from smaller ones.

Within banking, also separate commercial banks from development banks and finance companies. They sit in different regulatory classes under Nepal Rastra Bank (the "A", "B" and "C" classes), with different minimum capital, permitted activities and risks. A "B" class development bank and an "A" class commercial bank both take deposits and lend, but comparing their P/B directly, without adjusting for size and class, misses real differences.

Hydropower companies should be compared with other hydropower companies — but separate operating companies from those still under construction.

A company that has sold electricity for ten years, such as Chilime or Butwal Power Company, has earnings you can measure today. One still building, with its first unit not yet running, has no real current earnings. Its P/E, if any, is often meaningless or negative; investors are really pricing future cash that does not exist yet. Comparing the P/E of an operating hydropower company with a pre-operational one is comparing a fruit tree with a sapling: both may become valuable, but only one is bearing fruit now.

WARNING Never build a peer group just by sorting on market value or picking "the next five companies alphabetically" from a sector list. A peer group must share the underlying economic engine: similar revenue model, cost structure, capital needs, regulation and roughly similar life-cycle stage (growing or mature). A peer group put together for convenience rather than real comparability gives a multiple that looks precise but means very little.

Four more things to match

  • Scale. A bank with a NPR 200 billion loan book faces different economies of scale, funding costs and regulatory attention than one with NPR 40 billion. Larger banks often (not always) earn a premium multiple for perceived stability, a strong deposit base and more easily traded shares.
  • Ownership and promoter concentration. Many NEPSE companies, especially hydropower, have promoter shares locked in for a legal period (Lesson 47.4). That changes how many shares actually trade. Two hydropower companies with identical plants and revenue can show very different multiples just because one has far fewer freely tradeable shares.
  • Growth stage. A hydropower company that just switched on a larger new project and is still building up to full output will show fast-rising earnings for a few years, with no change in the asset. Comparing its P/E with a mature company running flat confuses "temporarily low current earnings" with "lower value".
  • Capital structure. Nepali hydropower projects usually borrow heavily — often 70:30 or 80:20 debt to equity in construction and early operation, through bank consortiums and development lenders. Two companies with similar plants but very different debt will show very different relationships between EV/EBITDA and P/E. Comparing P/E alone, without EV/EBITDA (which removes the effect of debt by looking at the whole enterprise), can mislead. Lesson 47.6 develops this.

So a real peer-group exercise looks less like "find five NEPSE companies with a market value near mine" and more like a checklist: same core sector, same regulatory class, similar size, similar stage — and, where differences cannot be removed by choosing carefully, adjustments made openly rather than ignored.

PRACTICAL TOOL A five-question peer filter for NEPSE. (1) Same core sector and sub-sector (commercial bank vs. commercial bank; run-of-river hydropower vs. run-of-river hydropower)? (2) Same regulatory class and licensing? (3) Similar life-cycle stage — mature and stable, growing or ramping up, or pre-operational? (4) Size (assets, revenue, market value) within a sensible multiple of the target — say three to five times, not thirty? (5) Free float and trading enough that its price is a real market price, not a thinly traded quote? A company failing two or more should be dropped or used only with an explicit adjustment.

Lesson 47.3 — Why You Cannot Compare a Bank to a Hydropower Company

It is worth spelling out exactly why cross-sector comparison on NEPSE is dangerous — not as a vague warning, but by walking through what would actually go wrong.

The tempting mistake

An investor notices a mid-sized commercial bank on a P/E of 12x and a well-known hydropower company on 35x. He concludes the bank is "cheap" and the hydropower company "expensive", and recommends switching from hydropower to banking. This treats P/E as meaning the same thing in both. It does not.

What drives a bank's earnings

A commercial bank earns from net interest income (the gap between what it earns on loans and investments and what it pays on deposits and borrowings), fee income, and loan-loss provisioning. All of these move with the credit cycle, NRB's interest rate policy and competition for deposits among the roughly twenty commercial banks.

A bank's earnings usually swing less from year to year than a hydropower company's, because a spread-out loan book smooths individual borrowers' ups and downs. But a bank carries credit risk — the chance that borrowers fail to repay — which a hydropower company largely does not carry in the same form.

What drives a hydropower company's earnings

  • River flow, which varies by season and year. Nepali rivers run high in the monsoon and low in winter, so a dry-season earnings dip comes every single year.
  • The PPA with the Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA) or another buyer, which fixes the tariff — often with wet- and dry-season rates and escalation usually only for about the first eight years, then flat.
  • Debt service — most projects carry large loans that must be repaid however much water flows.

Also, just after a new plant starts, earnings can look artificially small or even negative. Depreciation and interest are heaviest at the start, while revenue is still building towards full output. So a "high" P/E is not necessarily "expensive"; it may reflect earnings that are temporarily low compared with the company's normal future earning power.

Same formula, different meaning

These are not small differences of style. They are structurally different businesses, with different margins, growth paths, risks and links between today's accounting profit and true value.

A 12x P/E for a bank with fairly stable, recurring earnings is not directly comparable with a 35x P/E for a hydropower company whose earnings are still ramping up. The hydropower P/E might fall to 15x within three years purely from the ramp-up, with no change in share price, simply because the "E" grows. The bank's 12x may already reflect a mature, fully provisioned earnings stream unlikely to grow much faster than nominal GDP.

WARNING A P/E, P/B or EV/EBITDA multiple can only be compared across companies with similar margins, growth paths and risk. Comparing a bank's multiple directly with a hydropower company's — or a microfinance institution's with a manufacturer's — compares numbers calculated the same way but meaning fundamentally different things. A "cheap" multiple in one sector can be more expensive, in real economic terms, than an "expensive" multiple in another.

Even within look-alike sectors

The same logic applies, less obviously, inside sectors that look alike:

  • Life and non-life insurers are both "insurance", but have different reserving rules, claim patterns and capital rules under the Nepal Insurance Authority. Comparing their P/B one-for-one is a milder version of the same mistake.
  • Microfinance institutions and commercial banks both lend, but microfinance serves different borrowers, carries different credit risk, works under a different regulatory cap on interest spreads, and often trades at consistently different multiples for these reasons — not because the market randomly misprices one.

A useful test before comparing any two multiples: "If I owned all of both companies outright, with no share market at all, would a rupee of reported earnings, book value or EBITDA mean roughly the same in both?" If not — because one's earnings are much jumpier, one carries far more risk, one grows much faster, or one sits in a completely different regulatory box — the two multiples are not directly comparable, and conclusions from comparing them raw are unreliable.

CASE IN POINT When NEPSE's index has rallied sharply, sector-rotation commentary in the Nepali financial press has sometimes called hydropower "overvalued" purely because its average P/E sits well above banking's. This skips the harder question of why the two sectors should trade at similar multiples at all. A capital-heavy, long-life hydropower asset with contracted revenue, in a country short of power, can sensibly justify a quite different multiple from a deposit-taking bank exposed to the full credit cycle. Hydropower can certainly be overvalued — but the comparison must be against hydropower peers and hydropower fundamentals, not banking benchmarks.

Lesson 47.4 — When the Market Itself Distorts the Multiple: Illiquidity and Thin Float on NEPSE

Every form of relative valuation rests on one quiet assumption: that the market price is a fairly reliable signal, produced by enough real buying and selling to reflect a genuine consensus about value. In a deep market with millions of shares changing hands daily among many owners, that mostly holds. On NEPSE, for a fair number of companies, it does not. An analyst who forgets this will read false signals straight out of the data.

Why: promoter lock-ins and small free float

Under the Companies Act and rules enforced by the Securities Board of Nepal (SEBON), plus hydropower-specific rules, promoter shareholders — founders, sponsoring institutions and early strategic investors — must hold their shares for a legal lock-in period after listing, commonly several years, before they may sell. This is meant to show commitment and stop founders dumping shares right after an IPO.

But it has a side effect. The free float — the share of total shares actually available to trade, as opposed to locked up with promoters, the government or other long-term holders — can be small. It is sometimes well under 30% for a newly listed hydropower company, against a much larger float for an established bank with a broad, long-listed shareholder base.

REGULATORY DETAIL SEBON's public offering rules generally require companies to sell a portion of shares to the general public at IPO, while promoter shares stay locked in for a period before they can be sold. The Department of Electricity Development's licence terms and SEBON's issue rules also impose local-ownership and public-issue requirements on hydropower projects as a condition of licensing. In practice, a large share of a newly listed hydropower company is not actually tradeable for several years — so the daily price is set by a small slice of total ownership, not the whole company.

How a small float bends the price

When only a few shares can trade, a modest amount of buying or selling moves the price sharply, because there are not enough shares to absorb the order. It is like a village haat with only three sellers of a vegetable: one big buyer can push the price up fast.

NEPSE also uses daily circuit breakers — limits on how far a price can move in one session. For a thinly traded share, it may take many days in a row at the upper limit before the price reaches a level that reflects real demand. During that climb, or a later slide, the multiple you see on any day is a snapshot of a price still finding its level, not a settled market judgment.

Three distortions to name

  • New small-float hydropower shares often trade at extremely high multiples in their first months or years — not necessarily because the market did careful work and decided the projects are worth that, but because retail demand for a few tradeable shares outstrips supply. Using such a multiple as a peer benchmark for another small hydropower company is anchoring on noise, not signal.
  • Low daily volumes, even for more established shares, mean the "last traded price" may reflect a handful of trades, sometimes between related parties, rather than broad market clearing. A multiple from that day's close can swing on one large trade.
  • Jumpy multiples. A P/E that moves from 22x to 27x in a week is more likely illiquidity noise than a real 23% rethink of the company's earning power.
CAUTION Before using any company's multiple in relative valuation — in a peer average or a one-to-one comparison — check its average daily traded volume and free float. A multiple from a share trading only a few thousand shares a day, with 70–80% locked up with promoters, deserves far less weight than one from a large-float, actively traded bank with daily volumes in the hundreds of thousands. When a peer average must include thinly traded names, consider weighting by float-adjusted market value or trading value rather than a simple average, and say openly that illiquid names are included rather than quietly blending them in.

When the whole market runs hot

A related distortion is NEPSE's broad market cycles. Nepal's market has moved through strong bull and bear phases, often driven by retail mood, margin lending, and wider liquidity (interest rates, remittance inflows, bank liquidity) more than by company fundamentals.

In a broad rally, average P/E and P/B across a whole sector can rise together, well above what fundamentals justify, simply because more money is chasing the same shares. Using "the sector average multiple" as the benchmark then anchors the whole valuation to a temporarily inflated crowd view. This is exactly the failure flagged at the end of Lesson 47.1, and it is why the next lesson introduces a check that does not depend on what the crowd is paying today.

Lesson 47.5 — The Justified Multiple: Deriving a Fair Multiple from Fundamentals

Everything so far compares a company's multiple with what others, or the same company in the past, trade at. That has an obvious blind spot: if the whole peer group or market is mispriced, comparing inside it tells you nothing about whether the group itself is cheap or dear.

The tool for this blind spot is the justified multiple: a multiple worked out not from what the market pays today, but from the company's own fundamentals — its profitability, its growth and the return investors need for its risk.

Justified price-to-book

The clearest and most used version, especially for banks (where book value is meaningful and closely watched because the balance sheet is the business), is the justified P/B. It comes straight from the dividend discount model for a company growing at a steady rate — the same Gordon growth logic as in Chapter 46 and Chapter 48, written as a ratio:

Justified P/B = (ROE − g) / (Ke − g)

  • ROE — the company's sustainable return on equity (net income ÷ shareholders' equity, from Chapter 40);
  • g — the expected long-run growth of earnings and book value;
  • Ke — the cost of equity: the yearly return shareholders need for this company's risk, usually estimated with CAPM from a risk-free rate (in Nepal, often long-term government or development bond yields), an equity risk premium and a beta.
PRACTICAL TOOL Justified P/B = (ROE − g) ÷ (Ke − g). Worked example: a commercial bank with a sustainable ROE of 16%, long-run growth of 8% and a cost of equity of 14% has a justified P/B of (0.16 − 0.08) ÷ (0.14 − 0.08) = 0.08 ÷ 0.06 ≈ 1.33x. If it actually trades at 2.0x, the market is pricing in a higher sustainable ROE, faster growth or a lower risk premium than your inputs — and the gap tells you exactly where to focus your research, instead of leaving you with a vague sense that the share "looks expensive".

Why some banks trade at 2.5x and others at 0.9x

The formula explains something every Nepali bank investor has noticed: why do some banks trade at a P/B of 2.5x and others, of similar size, at 0.9x? The answer is the gap between ROE and the cost of equity.

  • A bank earning an ROE well above its cost of equity creates value with every extra rupee of capital. Book value compounds, and the market will pay more than one rupee of price for one rupee of book value, because that rupee inside the bank works harder than the market's required return.
  • A bank earning an ROE close to or below its cost of equity creates no economic value with new capital, even if it is "profitable" on paper. The market sensibly refuses to pay a premium, and sometimes prices it below book (P/B under 1.0x).

Think of two moneylenders, each with Rs 10 lakh. One earns 20% a year on it; the other earns 8% when people expect 14%. Nobody would pay the same price for the two businesses, even though both have Rs 10 lakh of "book value".

This changes what "cheap" and "expensive" mean in a way pure peer comparison cannot. Two banks might both trade at a P/B of 1.5x — identical on the surface. One may be fully justified by its ROE and growth; the other overpriced, because its ROE is lower or its risk (and cost of equity) higher. A peer comparison would say they are "priced the same". A justified multiple says they are not equally attractive at all.

Other justified multiples

  • Justified P/E, under the same steady-growth framework, is the dividend payout ratio ÷ (Ke − g). This suits hydropower companies, many of which pay out a large share of earnings once they reach steady operation, having little need to keep capital for growth beyond their licensed capacity.
  • Justified EV/EBITDA has no single clean formula, because it depends on capital structure and reinvestment. But the principle is the same: a fair EV/EBITDA should rise with the EBITDA margin, EBITDA growth and how durable the cash is (a government-backed PPA running another twenty-five years justifies a different multiple from one ending in three), and fall as risk and the cost of capital rise.
  • Dividend yield can be checked against a "required yield" — the cost of equity minus expected capital growth. If a share's yield sits well below what its risk suggests investors should demand, without strong growth to make up for it, that gap is worth investigating — echoing the "high-yield trap versus growth" points from Chapter 39 (Lesson 39.4).
KEY CONCEPT A justified multiple is not an alternative to relative valuation — it is a discipline added on top. You still look at peers and historical averages (Lessons 47.2 and 47.3), but you also work out independently what multiple the company's own ROE, growth and cost of equity would justify. When the peer-average multiple and the justified multiple broadly agree, you can act with more confidence that market pricing reflects economic reality. When they diverge sharply, the divergence is the finding: either the market has mispriced the share (an opportunity, or a warning), or your own estimate of ROE, growth or cost of equity needs another look.

The limits of the tool

Be honest about the limits too. The steady-growth assumption behind justified P/B and P/E is a simplification. It assumes ROE, growth and cost of equity all stay stable for a long time, which is rarely exactly true — especially for a hydropower company whose earnings change a lot from ramp-up to full output, or a bank whose ROE can be pushed down for a while by one large loan-loss provision.

The formula is a lens for organising your thinking and finding what drives a valuation gap, not a precise "correct" answer to plug into a spreadsheet and trust blindly. Treat it like a doctor treats a blood test: it points to the right question; it does not replace the full examination.

Lesson 47.6 — Common Pitfalls and a Disciplined Comps Checklist

With peer selection, sector dangers, illiquidity and justified multiples covered, this lesson gathers the mistakes that most often spoil relative valuation on NEPSE, and ends with a process you can use every time.

Pitfall 1: comparing a growth story with a mature one

A hydropower company that just switched on a new run-of-river plant and is still ramping up, or a bank rapidly expanding branches and lending in underserved provinces, will show rising earnings for reasons unrelated to any market rethink of its multiple.

Comparing its current P/E directly with a mature, slow-growing peer — without adjusting for growth, or better, without bringing both companies' earnings to a comparable point in their life cycles — consistently makes growth companies look "expensive" and mature ones "cheap". A higher multiple may be fully justified by faster growth, exactly as the justified formulas in Lesson 47.5 predict.

Pitfall 2: ignoring different debt levels

As Lesson 47.2 noted, hydropower projects often borrow heavily. Interest is paid below the operating line, so two companies with identical plants and identical EBITDA can show very different net income — and very different P/E — just because one has more debt.

A heavily borrowed company's P/E can look deceptively low, because more of its enterprise value is financed by debt rather than equity, piling both upside and risk onto a smaller equity base. That is why EV/EBITDA — using enterprise value (market value of equity plus net debt) and EBITDA instead of net income — is often more reliable for comparing capital-heavy, borrowed businesses like hydropower. It removes the distortion debt brings to P/E. When debt-to-equity ratios differ a lot, always cross-check P/E conclusions against EV/EBITDA.

WARNING A low P/E on a heavily indebted company is not automatically a bargain. Heavy borrowing makes equity earnings more sensitive to changes in revenue, interest rates and debt payments. A company that looks "cheap" on P/E because debt has lifted its return on a smaller equity base is also carrying more risk — and that risk belongs in your cost of equity (raising Ke, which lowers the justified multiple), even if it does not show up in the P/E itself.

Pitfall 3: stale or flattering earnings

Nepali companies, like companies everywhere, do not always report earnings that cleanly reflect sustainable, repeatable performance.

  • A bank that books a big one-off gain from selling investments, or under-provisions for bad loans one quarter to flatter profit, shows a temporarily inflated EPS — and so an artificially low, falsely attractive P/E.
  • A hydropower company reporting a year with unusually strong river flow (a wet year) shows a P/E that looks cheap compared with its normal years — the seasonal and yearly swings from Lesson 47.3. Anchoring on that one good year, without averaging over a full cycle of river conditions, overstates the sustainable earning power you are paying for.
CAUTION Before trusting any P/E or EV/EBITDA, ask what the "E" or "EBITDA" actually is: the latest quarter, the last twelve months, or a normalised, cycle-adjusted figure? For hydropower, check whether the year had average, above-average or below-average water flow — one unusually wet or dry year can swing reported earnings, and the multiple, a long way with no real change in the asset's long-run earning power. For banks, check whether profit includes one-off items (asset sales, tax adjustments, unusually low provisioning) unlikely to repeat.

Pitfall 4: treating the peer average as the target price

Even with a clean, well-matched peer group, it is tempting to say: the peer average P/B is 1.6x, my company trades at 1.2x, so its "fair" price is the peer average. This skips the justified-multiple step entirely.

The right order is: build the peer group, note the peer average, then ask whether your company's own ROE, growth and risk support trading at, above or below that average. A company with below-average ROE deserves a below-average multiple. Calling it "undervalued" just because it trades below the peer average, without checking whether its fundamentals justify a discount, is a common and costly error.

Pitfall 5: forgetting thin liquidity in the peer average

One or two illiquid, small-float names can drag a whole benchmark to a distorted level — especially in NEPSE's small peer sets, sometimes only five to ten names. One thinly traded hydropower share with an inflated multiple inside an eight-company group can move the simple average noticeably. A float-weighted or liquidity-screened average is sturdier.

This table shows several of the pitfalls at once:

  • Upper Tamakoshi's P/E of 38x looks far more expensive than Chilime's 16x on a raw comparison. But Upper Tamakoshi is still ramping up towards full earnings — Pitfall 1 — and its EV/EBITDA of 11.0x is much closer to the mature peers' 9.0–9.5x than its P/E suggests, because EBITDA is less distorted by early-year depreciation and interest than net income is.
  • The newly listed small-float project shows the most extreme multiples on every measure — the thin-float distortion from Lesson 47.4. A disciplined analyst would leave it out of the peer average entirely, or flag it as unreliable, rather than treating its 85x P/E as a benchmark for anything.
PRACTICAL TOOL A six-step relative valuation checklist for NEPSE. (1) Define the target company's exact sub-sector, size and life-cycle stage. (2) Build a peer group with the five-question filter from Lesson 47.2, dropping poor matches rather than stretching "comparable". (3) Screen out or down-weight peers with thin free float and low trading, per Lesson 47.4. (4) Normalise peers' earnings for one-off items and, for hydropower, for the quality of the water year, per Lesson 47.6. (5) Work out the peer average and the target's own five-year average multiple, per Lesson 47.1. (6) Work out the target's justified multiple from ROE (or payout), growth and cost of equity, per Lesson 47.5, and explain any gap between the peer average, the historical average and the justified multiple before forming a final view.

Chapter recap

  • Relative valuation asks what the market pays for similar businesses, using P/E, P/B, EV/EBITDA and dividend yield — compared across peers and against the company's own history. It shows relative position, not absolute worth: the cheapest share in an overpriced sector is still overpriced.
  • NEPSE (302 listed companies by mid-July 2026) is concentrated in banking and hydropower. Build peers on the same economic engine: same sub-sector, regulatory class, similar size and stage, and enough float and trading. Never mix operating and pre-operational hydropower.
  • Never compare multiples across sectors: a bank's 12x and a ramping hydropower company's 35x mean different things. Even life vs. non-life insurers, or microfinance vs. banks, need care.
  • Promoter lock-ins, small free float and circuit breakers make some NEPSE prices — and multiples — unreliable. Check volume and float; weight or drop illiquid names; beware sector averages in a bull run.
  • The justified P/B = (ROE − g) ÷ (Ke − g) (e.g. 16%, 8%, 14% → about 1.33x) explains why high-ROE banks deserve premiums. Use it on top of peer comparison.
  • Avoid five pitfalls: growth vs. mature, different debt levels (cross-check EV/EBITDA), flattering or wet-year earnings, treating the peer average as a target, and illiquid names in the average. Follow the six-step checklist.
