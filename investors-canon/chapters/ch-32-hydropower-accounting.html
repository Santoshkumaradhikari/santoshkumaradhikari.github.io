WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Capital work in progress (CWIP) — the running total spent on a power plant still being built; an asset, not money gone. Interest during construction (IDC) — interest on construction loans added to the plant's cost instead of being expensed. PPA — the power purchase agreement with NEA fixing the price per unit, higher in the dry season than in the monsoon. Escalation — the agreed yearly rise in that price for a limited number of years. Royalty — the government's fee for the river's use, which steps up after year 15. Tax holiday — years in which a new plant pays little or no income tax.

Lesson 32.1 — The Two Lives of a Hydropower Balance Sheet

An empty income statement and a huge balance sheet

Open the annual report of almost any NEPSE hydropower company and something looks odd. For years — sometimes a decade — the income statement is nearly empty while the balance sheet grows enormous. A company can report no revenue and no profit, yet hold assets worth tens of billions of rupees.

That is not a company in distress. It is a company under construction. Accounting during construction follows a completely different logic from accounting during operation. Knowing which side of that line a company sits on is the most important skill in reading a Nepali hydropower share.

During construction: almost everything is capitalised

While a plant is being built, nearly every cost is added to the asset rather than charged as an expense — like a family building a house, who records each payment for bricks and labour as part of the house's value, not as household spending. The costs pile up on the balance sheet as capital work in progress (CWIP):

  • land and resettlement;
  • tunnel digging, penstock and powerhouse civil works;
  • turbines and generators;
  • the line connecting to the grid;
  • consultancy and supervision fees;
  • and — critically — interest on the loans paying for all of this.

Almost nothing reaches the profit and loss account, apart from small overheads auditors judge unrelated to building the plant. A company can spend all its equity and draw its full loan while the income statement shows only a trickle of bank interest on unspent share money.

At commercial operation: everything changes

The turning point is the commercial operation date (COD) — not to be confused with the required commercial operation date (RCOD), the deadline written into the power purchase agreement (PPA) and licence. At COD:

  • CWIP is moved into property, plant and equipment — civil structures, hydro-mechanical and electro-mechanical equipment, transmission assets;
  • depreciation begins;
  • interest stops being capitalised, because Nepal Accounting Standard 23 (Borrowing Costs) allows capitalising interest only until the asset is "substantially complete and ready for its intended use";
  • from then on, interest, depreciation and the whole PPA revenue flow through the income statement.

So a company that showed almost nothing for eight years can suddenly show loan interest, depreciation and revenue in one report. The market often misprices this both ways: treating a pre-COD company as if it already earns, or treating a newly opened plant's first full year of depreciation and interest as a permanent fall in profit rather than the new normal.

KEY CONCEPT Capital work in progress is not "cash gone". It is an asset. A hydropower company with Rs 40 arba of CWIP and no revenue is not destroying value — it is building an asset whose future earnings depend on the PPA it signs and the tariff increases built into it. Judge CWIP by the PPA terms, not just its size.

From the notes, find exactly where a company is: percentage of physical completion, expected COD and — the number analysts often skip — total interest capitalised so far. That last figure tells you how much of the future asset is financing cost rather than concrete and steel, and it previews the depreciation the company will carry for the rest of its life.

Lesson 32.2 — Interest During Construction: The Silent Multiplier

Heavy debt, long delays

Nepali hydropower projects are financed with levels of debt that would look dangerous in almost any other sector — commonly 70:30 or even 80:20 debt to equity. The debt usually comes from a group of Nepali banks in a consortium loan. For years NRB's single-obligor limit made it legally impossible for one bank to fund a large project alone; that fixed limit was removed in October 2025 (Chapter 42), but banks' own limits and sector caps still make shared lending the norm.

A run-of-river project with a five- or six-year build capitalises interest the whole time. And in Nepal delays are the rule, not the exception — landslides on access roads, tunnelling stopped by the monsoon, contractor disputes, court cases over transmission-line land. So the capitalised interest bill often surprises shareholders more than the construction cost itself.

How interest during construction is added

Under NAS 23, a company must capitalise the actual interest on money borrowed specifically for the project, minus any income earned by parking unused loan money. Where general borrowing is used, a weighted average rate applies. NRB's directives also guide banks on how long-term infrastructure loans — hydropower above all — may treat interest that builds up unpaid during construction, which shapes repayment holidays and how fast the capitalised interest grows.

REGULATORY DETAIL NRB's directives let banks allow interest to be capitalised on long-term project loans instead of demanding cash repayments before a plant earns anything. That is sound project finance — a plant cannot repay before COD — but it means the loan owed at COD is much larger than the amount first drawn. Read the loan schedule in the notes, not just the headline debt figure.

Upper Tamakoshi: the scale it can reach

The clearest example is Nepal's largest domestically financed plant, the 456 MW Upper Tamakoshi project (NEPSE: UPPER), built and run by the NEA's subsidiary Upper Tamakoshi Hydropower Limited.

  • Its construction cost estimate rose from about Rs 49.29 arba to about Rs 52.29 arba as delays piled up — first when the original hydro-mechanical contractor, India's Texmaco, withdrew and Austria's Andritz Hydro took over, then from damage in the 2015 Gorkha earthquake.
  • But the key number is different: by commercial operation, total project cost — construction plus capitalised interest — had reached about Rs 76 arba, of which about Rs 24 arba was capitalised interest.
  • It was funded with about Rs 10.59 arba of share capital (NEA, Nepal Telecom, Citizen Investment Trust and Rastriya Beema Sansthan among the big holders) and about Rs 41.70 arba of loans from the Employees Provident Fund, Nepal Telecom, Citizen Investment Trust and Rastriya Beema Sansthan — not from a bank consortium.
CASE IN POINT Upper Tamakoshi's capitalised interest of about Rs 24 arba is close to half its original construction budget. After COD it became part of the asset being depreciated, so UPPER's depreciation — and the debt behind it — reflect years of delay as much as the plant itself. When construction stretches, the operating years carry the cost of that delay for the whole depreciation period, not just the building years.
WARNING A large capitalised-interest balance can make a company's equity return look better during construction (no interest expense shows), while quietly loading much more debt and depreciation onto the operating years. Never judge a pre-COD company's book value per share without asking how much of it is capitalised interest rather than physical plant — and whether its PPA tariff was fixed years earlier, before the delay pushed costs up.

A general rule follows: the longer a project takes, the larger the share of its asset that is financing cost rather than construction — and the higher its later depreciation and interest compared with the original feasibility study. When a prospectus or annual report projects future profit, ask what construction timeline it assumes, and compare that with the physical progress the same report discloses.

Lesson 32.3 — Reading the PPA: Wet Season, Dry Season, and the Escalation Clock

Revenue comes from a contract

A hydropower company's revenue is not just units sold times a price. It comes from a contract — the power purchase agreement (PPA) with the Nepal Electricity Authority — negotiated at one moment, which decides almost mechanically what the company can earn for the next 20 to 30 years. Reading a hydropower income statement without the PPA is like reading a bank's accounts without knowing what it pays depositors.

Two seasons, two prices

Nepal's rivers are fed by the monsoon. A run-of-river plant (no storage) produces several times more power in the wet season (roughly mid-June to mid-October) than in the dry season, when flows can fall to a fraction. The NEA's standard PPA for small and medium run-of-river producers splits the price the same way: a lower rate for wet-season power, when supply is plentiful, and a much higher rate for dry-season power, when each unit is scarce.

Balephi Hydropower's PPA with the NEA, signed in December 2015, is a representative example:

  • Rs 4.80 per unit (kWh) in the wet season;
  • Rs 8.40 per unit in the dry season — 75 percent more;
  • a 3 percent rise each year, applied seven times, starting from the thirteenth month after commercial operation.

Balephi's own schedule shows one rise lost because operation started later than planned — a reminder that delay does not just raise costs; it can also shorten the period of price rises a company was counting on.

KEY CONCEPT The wet-dry price split exists because the NEA's system is short of power in the dry season and has plenty in the monsoon. A run-of-river plant that produces most of its yearly output in four monsoon months sells most of its volume at the cheaper rate and only a little at the premium rate. That is why a small run-of-river plant's revenue moves far more with its dry-season output than its total yearly generation suggests.

Because the price rises for a fixed number of years and then flattens, revenue per unit is knowable years ahead if you have the PPA schedule. Serious hydropower analysis in Nepal starts from the PPA annexes, not from past income growth.

Storage projects

A storage or peaking plant can hold back water and generate in the dry season or at peak hours. That firm dry-season power is worth far more to the grid than monsoon power, so the NEA and the Electricity Regulatory Commission (ERC) have spent years designing a separate tariff framework to make storage projects bankable.

An ERC discussion paper shows how wide the gap is expected to be — and real developers' asks are wider still once Nepal's actual financing costs (much higher interest and shorter loan terms than the soft-loan assumptions in illustrative models) are included.

CASE IN POINT The ERC's "illustrative" storage tariff assumes soft loans at 3.9 percent for 27 years. Developers of Dudhkoshi and Budhi Gandaki are asking for roughly double. That is not just greed: Nepali rupee project loans typically cost 10–11 percent with 12-year terms, against a project life of 30 to 50 years. When you read a storage-hydro IPO prospectus, check what financing its tariff was built on. A project priced on soft-loan assumptions but funded with ordinary bank loans has a revenue-cost mismatch from day one.

Take-or-pay versus take-and-pay

  • Take-or-pay: the NEA must pay for an agreed amount of available power whether or not it actually takes it (subject to force majeure and grid exceptions). Demand risk sits with the buyer, and revenue looks more like a fixed annuity — like a tenant who pays rent whether or not they use the room.
  • Take-and-pay: the NEA pays only for power it takes. The producer bears curtailment risk — real in a system that has at times been unable to absorb all monsoon power because of transmission bottlenecks.

The NEA has moved smaller hydropower PPAs towards take-or-pay in recent years, which helps predictability. But a listed company's old PPA may still carry the weaker terms, so this belongs on your checklist for every hydropower share.

What the price-rise clock means for your model

Price rises are time-limited — commonly seven or eight annual steps — so revenue growth per unit is front-loaded and mechanical, not earned by operating better. Take the exact schedule from the PPA rather than assuming a flat growth rate, and mark the year the rises stop. After that, revenue can grow only through higher plant availability.

Lesson 32.4 — Depreciation, License Life, and the Financial Asset Question

Is "depreciation" even the right model?

Once a plant is built, how should it be written off? And is depreciation even right for an asset built under a BOOT licence (build, own, operate, transfer) that must eventually be handed to the Government of Nepal? This is a live technical debate in Nepal. It matters because the choice changes reported profit, tax timing and how comparable hydropower companies are.

What most companies do

Most listed hydropower companies treat their plant as ordinary property, plant and equipment under NAS 16, depreciated in equal yearly amounts over a life set to match (or fall within) the licence period, so the asset is largely written off by the time it must be transferred. This gives a familiar income statement of depreciation and interest, and nearly all NEPSE hydropower companies use it.

The IFRIC 12 argument

There is a technical argument, based on IFRIC 12, Service Concession Arrangements, that many Nepali BOOT projects should instead record a financial asset (a receivable) rather than plant. That applies when both of these hold:

  1. the grantor (the NEA, for the state) controls what service is provided and at what price; and
  2. the grantor keeps a significant interest in the plant at the end of the concession.

A run-of-river plant with a fixed NEA tariff, take-or-pay payments tied to availability, and a required handover to the government at the end of its licence arguably meets both. Then the "right" accounting would be a financial asset that unwinds through an interest calculation over the concession — not a depreciating plant.

WARNING Under IFRIC 12's financial-asset model, the main revenue would be interest income on the receivable, not depreciation and energy sales — a completely different income statement, and one that removes management's choice over depreciation. The difference is not academic: the plant (PPE) treatment tends to defer tax and support earlier dividends tied to the PPA tariff, which is why companies lean towards it even where the concession terms may point the other way.

Two contrasting examples

  • Tamakoshi-V, a planned 99.8 MW run-of-river plant in Dolakha (still under development), has a 30-year concession with a fixed tariff (with the standard 3 percent rises) and a required handover to the government at the end — textbook IFRIC 12 conditions on paper.
  • Butwal Power Company (BPC), which runs Andhikhola (9.4 MW) and Jhimruk (12 MW), carries its concession rights as an intangible asset under NAS 38. Its argument: it runs its own distribution network and so bears demand risk itself, rather than having the NEA guarantee its sales — a genuinely different risk that can justify different accounting.

Even as an intangible asset, the write-off method matters. Equal yearly amounts over the licence give a very different profit pattern from write-offs tied to actual generation, and the two are not always easy to tell apart from the face of the accounts.

PRACTICAL TOOL In a hydropower company's notes, find the asset accounting policy and check: (1) is the concession asset classed as plant, intangible or financial asset? (2) is it written off in equal yearly amounts or by usage? (3) is the useful life shorter than, equal to or longer than the remaining licence? A useful life well beyond the remaining licence is a red flag — it probably understates depreciation and overstates near-term profit, given that the asset must be handed over or largely replaced when the licence ends.

You do not need to settle the IFRIC 12 debate yourself — even Nepali chartered accountants disagree. The practical point is narrower: because companies classify and write off their main asset differently, you cannot compare depreciation-to-revenue ratios across two hydropower companies and assume like for like. Read the policy note first.

Lesson 32.5 — Royalty, Tax Holidays, and the Government's Take

Royalty in two parts

Every licensed generator pays royalty to the Government of Nepal under the Electricity Act framework, in two parts:

  • a capacity royalty — a fixed yearly charge per kW installed;
  • an energy royalty — a percentage of the value of power produced or sold.

The step-up at year 16

For the first fifteen years of operation, a project typically pays about Rs 100 per kW a year and about 2 percent of revenue. From year 16 to year 30, both jump: capacity royalty to about Rs 1,000 per kW (ten times) and energy royalty to about 10 percent of revenue (five times).

For a large plant this is a structural shift in costs, on a fixed calendar, whatever the company's performance. Many investors miss it because they anchor on the margin a company reports in, say, year 6 or year 8.

The tax holiday

Alongside royalty runs a separate tax schedule under the Income Tax Act. Electricity generators are taxed at 20 percent, but qualifying companies (generally those reaching their required operation date within a government incentive window) get a 100 percent tax rebate for the first ten years and 50 percent for years eleven to fifteen. A project can run almost tax-free for a decade, then pay half the normal rate for five more years.

Shareholders also pay 5 percent tax on dividends, and individuals pay capital gains tax on listed hydropower shares at 3.75 percent long-term or 5 percent short-term (since 22 September 2026), like other NEPSE shares (Chapter 35).

REGULATORY DETAIL The tax holiday and the royalty step-up run on different clocks and move in opposite directions. The tax rebate is most generous exactly when royalty is lightest (years 1–15), and both turn against the company at about the same time (the rebate ends at year 15; royalty jumps five to ten times at year 16). A company's true mature margin — the one that will apply for most of its 20-to-30-year licence — is well below what it reports in years 3 to 10. Never extend a hydropower share's current margin into the long run.

Construction-phase concessions

A few construction-time concessions also shape the capital cost:

  • import duty on plant, machinery and steel penstock pipe is a nominal 1 percent, with full exemption from the normal 13 percent VAT;
  • local engineering, civil and transport costs still carry 13 percent VAT;
  • the government has offered a partial VAT refund — historically about Rs 50 lakh per MW — on engineering, transport and civil VAT paid.

These often appear as "other income" or smaller CWIP additions without a separate label. When reconciling projected and actual project cost, check the notes for VAT refund receivables before assuming an overrun is bigger than it is.

CAUTION Royalty, tax-rebate windows and VAT refunds have all been changed by successive Finance Acts. The exact terms for a company depend on its licence date, its size band and its required operation date against the incentive deadline in force when it was licensed. Treat these figures as the typical structure, and read each company's own tax and royalty notes.

Lesson 32.6 — Debt, Currency, and Governance Risk: Reading Between the Lines

Debt after the plant opens

The heavy debt that makes capitalised interest so important (Lesson 32.2) does not vanish at COD. It decides whether operating cash reaches shareholders as dividends or is swallowed by loan repayments for years.

  • Interest-rate risk: most Nepali hydropower debt is in rupees from domestic banks, which avoids currency mismatch. But bank rates float with the base-rate cycle, so a plant at 70:30 or 80:20 debt can see its ability to cover repayments swing a lot over one rate cycle, with no change in output.
  • Currency risk: on some larger cross-border or donor-funded projects, foreign-currency loans or PPA terms do appear. A company repaying a dollar loan from rupee PPA revenue carries open currency risk a purely domestic peer does not. Check the borrowings note.

Case 1: Upper Tamakoshi's governance strain

Upper Tamakoshi is Nepal's largest domestically financed plant and one of NEPSE's most widely held hydropower shares — yet, according to its March 2026 AGM notice, it has not yet paid a dividend. Its recent history shows how debt, delay and governance combine:

  • After its 15th AGM in Bhadra 2080 (August 2023), it held no AGM for more than two years, leaving shareholders without a formal way to review audited results or elect independent directors. It finally held its 16th and 17th AGMs together on Chaitra 29, 2082 (around mid-April 2026), approving accounts for the delayed years, with the appointment of two independent directors on the agenda (ShareHub and Bajarko Chirfar, March–April 2026).
  • ICRA Nepal had downgraded it to its 'D' (default) category after debt-servicing delays of more than 30 days, with long-term liabilities of about Rs 45.36 arba and negative retained earnings of about Rs 10.77 arba (reported January 2026). The company says it has since been paying on schedule.
  • Analysts have also flagged that the NEA's managing director has chaired UPPER's board, concentrating control in a company where the NEA and other public bodies hold about 51 percent — limiting minority shareholders' influence just when oversight matters most.

"Largest plant in the country" does not protect a company from a debt-service or governance crisis.

WARNING A hydropower company missing its legal AGM schedule is not a small procedural slip. It is often the first visible sign of deeper financial or governance stress, because AGM delays are often driven by unresolved audit issues, broken loan conditions or disputes the board does not want to table. Track AGM dates and rating-agency actions for every hydropower holding, not just dividend announcements.

Case 2: GMR Upper Karnali and extreme delay

The 900 MW Upper Karnali project, first taken up by India's GMR Group about two decades ago, spent some eighteen years in surveys, PPA talks (including a long price standoff with the NEA) and financing limbo before construction began in mid-2025. It is designed to export power to India and, under a three-country arrangement, Bangladesh. All the accounting questions above apply, plus cross-border pricing, approvals in several countries and financing that may mix foreign lenders and foreign-currency debt.

CASE IN POINT Eighteen years from first agreement to real construction is extreme, but not isolated. The Independent Power Producers' Association Nepal (IPPAN) estimated in October 2025 that delay-related costs across the sector's pipeline had reached about Rs 108 arba. Treat any target COD in a prospectus or annual report as today's best estimate, not a promise, and build a delay scenario into your own valuation.

The IPO "net worth" bar

These accounting issues feed a live controversy. SEBON has in recent years applied a "real net worth" bar of about Rs 90 per share for IPO approval — a figure found nowhere in the Securities Act, set administratively after a Public Accounts Committee directive in late 2023.

Construction-phase hydropower companies naturally show lower net worth per share than operating companies, so this bar has stalled hydropower IPOs in particular:

  • about Rs 66.23 arba of planned share issues by 98 companies were reported stuck in the approval queue;
  • fourteen hydropower companies — including Laughing Buddha Power Nepal, Yambaling Hydropower, Unique Hydropower, Beni Hydro and Puwa Khola One Hydro — were removed from the queue altogether;
  • an unregulated pre-IPO market has grown, with promoter-stage shares changing hands well above Rs 100 and buyers told to expect Rs 1,500–2,500 after listing — exactly the kind of informal, disclosure-free trading that hydropower's construction-phase opacity invites.
CAUTION An administrative valuation bar with no basis in law, applied to a sector whose accounting (heavy CWIP, delayed profit, capitalised interest) makes "net worth per share" a poor snapshot during construction, is a market-structure risk on top of the project risks in this chapter. For a pre-IPO or newly listed hydropower company, check not only its PPA and progress but how long it sat in the IPO queue and why.
PRACTICAL TOOL Before buying any NEPSE hydropower share: (1) Is it before or after COD? If before, get the physical completion percentage and the revised target COD. (2) From the PPA annex, get wet and dry tariffs, the price-rise schedule, and whether it is take-or-pay or take-and-pay. (3) Find total capitalised interest in the CWIP note and compare it with the original construction budget. (4) Find the asset classification (plant, intangible or financial asset) and write-off method. (5) Establish the current tax-holiday status and royalty band, and the year both turn. (6) Check the debt note for foreign currency, and the latest AGM date and any rating action.

Chapter recap

  • Hydropower accounting has two lives joined at the commercial operation date (COD). Before it, costs and interest pile up as capital work in progress; after it, depreciation, interest and PPA revenue flow through profit. Find which side a company is on first.
  • Interest during construction is the biggest hidden cost. Upper Tamakoshi's capitalised interest of about Rs 24 arba sat on about Rs 52 arba of construction cost, for a total near Rs 76 arba — and delay keeps costing through depreciation for decades.
  • Revenue comes from the PPA: the representative run-of-river rates are Rs 4.80 (wet) and Rs 8.40 (dry) per unit, with 3% rises for seven or eight years. Dry-season output drives revenue. Check take-or-pay versus take-and-pay. Storage tariffs are still being worked out, and developers ask far more than the ERC's soft-loan illustration.
  • How the plant is written off — plant, intangible or (under IFRIC 12) financial asset — varies, so depreciation is not comparable across companies without reading the policy note.
  • Royalty jumps at year 16 (about Rs 100 to Rs 1,000 per kW and 2% to 10% of revenue) just as the tax holiday ends (100% rebate for 10 years, 50% for 5). Today's margin is not the long-run margin.
  • Watch floating-rate debt, any foreign-currency loans, AGM delays and rating actions. Upper Tamakoshi went more than two years without an AGM and was rated 'D' before holding a combined AGM in April 2026.
  • Treat target CODs as estimates (IPPAN put sector delay costs at about Rs 108 arba), and note SEBON's informal Rs 90 net-worth bar for IPOs.
