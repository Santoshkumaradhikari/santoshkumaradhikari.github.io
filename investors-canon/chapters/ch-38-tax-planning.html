WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Holding period — the days between buying and selling, counted from settlement dates. Tax-loss harvesting — selling a loser so the loss offsets a gain; in Nepal it is not automatic. Turnover — how much buying and selling you do. Commercial investor — someone whose trading is large and systematic enough to count as a business, like a shopkeeper rather than a householder. BOID — the number identifying your demat account. Associated persons — close family and related parties; transfers between them are treated as sales at market value (Section 45).

Lesson 38.1 — The Architecture of Equity Taxation: What You Are Actually Planning Around

Planning starts with knowing the rules in force

Picture a Kathmandu investor with 22 companies across two brokers, three IPO allotments untouched since 2079, and a spreadsheet last updated before Dashain. Ask what tax rate they pay on NEPSE gains, and you will probably get a shrug — or a wrong number.

In Nepal, gains and dividends on listed shares are not taxed through the income slabs. They are taxed at fixed rates, cut out before the money reaches your bank. So "tax planning" here is not about hunting for deductions, as a salaried worker might. It is about three things:

  • timing — when you sell;
  • records — proving what you paid and when;
  • account structure — whose name the shares are in.

But first you need today's rates, because they change almost every year, and a plan built on last year's number is not a plan.

This chapter was first written in Bhadra 2083 (August 2026), under the Finance Act 2083, which took effect on Shrawan 1, 2083 (17 July 2026) and raised the tax on share gains. Then, on 22 September 2026, a Cabinet decision cut the rates for individuals to 3.75% (long-term) and 5% (short-term) — the lowest in years.

The lesson: Nepal's rates move often, in either direction. Usually they move with the budget, but not always. So the most important tax date in your calendar is Jestha 15, when the budget speech sets out the coming year's Finance Bill.

REGULATORY DETAIL Since 22 September 2026 (Cabinet decision published in the Nepal Gazette), capital gains tax on listed shares for resident individuals is 3.75% for shares held more than 365 days ("long-term") and 5% for shares held 365 days or less ("short-term"). From Shrawan 1, 2083 (17 July 2026), the Finance Act 2083 had raised the FY 2082/83 rates of 5% and 7.5% to 7.5% and 10%; the September cut reversed that rise and went further. Dividends on listed shares are still taxed at a flat 5%, withheld by the company. For the ordinary investor, both are final: deducted at source and not added to your other income.

You need more than one number: today's rate to plan your next sale, and earlier rates to check older contract notes.

Two features everything else rests on

First, the reward for patience is for individuals only. A company, or a mutual fund's own trading, pays a flat rate however long it held the shares. So the long-term discount is a benefit of investing in your own name, not something a company structure gives you more of (Lesson 38.6).

Second, the broker deducts the tax at settlement, through the CDSC and TMS (Trading Management System) systems. You do not calculate it, you do not pay it over, and for most retail investors you do not report it again. The number on your contract note is usually the end of the story.

KEY CONCEPT A final withholding tax settles your tax completely when it is deducted. It is not like TDS on salary, which is checked again at year-end. For a genuine retail investor, tax on share gains and on dividends are both final. That is why when you sell, not deduction-hunting, is where nearly all your lawful planning power lies.

The word "genuine" matters. Behind the final-tax system sits a second test: are you a "non-commercial" investor or a "commercial" one? Fail it, and the comfort of a final tax can disappear (Lesson 38.4). First, though, the lever every investor reaches for: the calendar.

Lesson 38.2 — Engineering the Long-Term Rate: The 365-Day Discipline

Why the gap still matters

The gap between short-term (5%) and long-term (3.75%) is now only 1.25 points. It is still not a rounding error. On a large position built over several IPO and bonus cycles, it can be tens of thousands of rupees on one sale. And it is the one number in Nepali share tax fully in your control, because you decide the day you sell.

The one-day trap

The holding period runs from settlement date to settlement date, not from the day you placed the order. With T+2 settlement (two working days after the trade), your one-year mark is tied to when the shares were credited to your demat account and when they leave it on sale.

If you bought on Poush 10 and assume the anniversary is exactly a year later, you may be a day or two off. And missing the line by one day puts the whole gain at the short-term rate. There is no part-credit. It is a cliff, not a slope — like missing the last bus by one minute: you do not get a discount for being nearly on time.

WARNING The 365-day line is a hard cliff. A sale that lands just inside the short-term window is taxed entirely at the higher rate. Before selling near an anniversary, check the exact settlement date in your Meroshare transaction history, not your memory of "around Poush".

Several purchases of the same share

It gets harder when you bought the same share several times — say an IPO allotment, a market purchase eight months later, a rights allotment, and a top-up 18 months after the first purchase. Sell half, and which shares have you sold?

Nepal does not work like US brokers that let you pick exactly which batch to sell. Cost is pooled into one weighted average (WACC) — the method the government moved to in FY 2076/77 to replace an unworkable batch-by-batch calculation — and, as Chapter 35 explained, the oldest shares are normally matched to a sale first. You cannot simply tell your broker "sell only the shares I bought 14 months ago and keep the ones from two months ago".

What you can control is the timing of the sale order itself. If a large sale would straddle an anniversary, you can split it: sell one part before the date and one after, so that at least the second part clearly qualifies for the long-term rate.

CASE IN POINT An investor holds 2,000 shares of a commercial bank, bought in one go 340 days ago, and is thinking of selling everything before an expected fall. Selling today means 5% on the whole gain. Waiting at least 26 more days — past day 365, counted from settlement dates — means 3.75%. On a gain of Rs 8 lakh, the 1.25-point difference is Rs 10,000. Waiting is a lawful tax choice — if the investment case still supports holding for those 26 days.

Do not let the tax tail wag the dog

This deserves its own rule: the long-term rate rewards conviction; it is not a reason to invent conviction you do not have.

A business that has got worse — a bank with a falling interest margin, a hydropower company in a tariff dispute, a hotel losing cash — does not become a better holding because you are 40 days short of 365. The tax saving is fixed and one-time. The loss from holding a bad share can grow every day.

Watch the budget, but do not guess it

A second timing question is the fiscal year. Rates are usually set by each budget, so a Finance Bill effective from the next Shrawan 1 could change them. But they can also change mid-year, as in September 2026.

Nobody knows a Finance Bill's contents before the budget speech, traditionally around Jestha 15. And 2026 showed rates can move both ways in one year: the July budget raised them, and two months later a Cabinet decision cut them below the old level to revive the market — just as the rate was cut from 7.5% to 5% in FY 2076/77. The lesson is about process, not prediction: follow the budget speech every Jestha as closely as you follow your portfolio.

Lesson 38.3 — Losses, Carryforwards, and the Real Limits of "Tax-Loss Harvesting" in Nepal

What "tax-loss harvesting" means elsewhere

American finance books describe tax-loss harvesting: you sell a losing share on purpose, so the loss cancels out a gain elsewhere in the same year and lowers your total tax. It is tempting to copy this on NEPSE. It does not work the same way here.

Why it does not happen automatically in Nepal

Tax on listed shares is withheld company by company, sale by sale, at settlement. The broker works out the gain on the sale you just made — sale money, less cost, less that trade's charges — and withholds tax on that sale alone. It does not look across your whole portfolio first.

So if you sell Company A at a loss and Company B at a gain on the same day, the broker still withholds tax on B's gain in full. A's loss does not reduce it. Nepali tax commentators have pointed to this as a weakness compared with India, the US and the UK, where gains and losses are netted across the year.

Think of two separate toll booths. Paying less at one does not reduce what you owe at the other.

CAUTION Selling a losing share in the same week as a winning one will generally not reduce the tax the broker withholds. If a capital loss can be used against a gain in Nepal at all, you must claim and prove it yourself, through your own tax filing and records. The system does not do it for you.

Losses are not worthless — but you must do the work

The general rules on carrying losses forward in the Income Tax Act, 2058 support the idea that a documented capital loss on listed shares can, in principle, be set against future capital gains. But it can never reduce your other income. A NEPSE loss does not cut your salary tax or business tax.

For an ordinary investor whose tax is withheld and who never files a capital gains schedule, using a loss means leaving the "sell and forget" habit. You must:

  • keep proof of the loss — the contract note showing a sale below cost; and
  • talk to a chartered accountant or tax adviser before relying on any loss claim, because practice for ordinary individual investors is far less settled than the withholding side.

Practitioners and market commentators have asked for clear written rules, such as a multi-year loss carry-forward window and portfolio-based (not company-by-company) calculation, precisely because the current framework is unclear. Treat any loss claim as something you must document and defend, not something your broker applies for you.

The safe version: a yearly review before Asar-end

In practice, the safe form of loss harvesting on NEPSE is a calm, once-a-year review before the fiscal year ends in Asar. Look for shares that pass both tests:

  1. genuinely no longer worth holding for investment reasons; and
  2. sitting at a loss.

Selling such a share before the year ends, and keeping the contract note, gives you the strongest basis for a future claim if you need one and get professional help.

Selling a share you still believe in, only to buy it back at once and create a paper loss, is not a strategy this book supports. Nepal has no written "wash-sale" rule like the US 30-day rule, but transactions with no real purpose except a tax loss carry anti-avoidance risk under the Act. Above all, it is poor investing dressed up as cleverness.

KEY CONCEPT In Nepal, a capital loss on listed shares is a claim you build and may have to defend — not a number your broker nets against your gains. Review losses once a year before Asar-end, keep every contract note, and treat any offset claim as work for a qualified tax preparer.

Lesson 38.4 — Turnover, Rebalancing, and the Commercial-Investor Trap

Every trim is a taxable sale

Rebalancing means trimming a share that has grown too big in your portfolio, or moving out of a sector that has run ahead of itself. It is good discipline. But on NEPSE every rebalancing sale is a taxable event, at the rate its holding period earns. A habit of frequent rebalancing pushes more of your gains into the short-term rate.

Compare two investors:

  • One rebalances once a year, trimming only shares that are oversized and past 365 days. Those trims pay 3.75%.
  • The other "actively manages", switching sectors every few months on news, rumour or momentum. Most gains land inside 365 days and pay 5% — plus broker commission, SEBON fees and DP charges twice as often.

Turnover — how much buying and selling you do — has a direct tax cost.

The bigger risk: being treated as a business

There is a more serious risk on top. In the FY 2080/81 budget, the government added a provision for extra income tax, on top of capital gains tax, on profits from share trading. NEPSE fell more than 90 points over two trading days before the Inland Revenue Department (IRD) issued a clarification.

CASE IN POINT After the FY 2080/81 budget, the IRD clarified that the extra income tax applied only to "commercial investors" — people whose share dealing is large and systematic enough to be a business — and not to ordinary "non-commercial" individual investors. Its example: a person earns Rs 30 lakh from share sales in a year and pays 5% capital gains tax (Rs 1.5 lakh at the rate then in force). If they are a genuine non-commercial investor, they owe nothing more on the remaining Rs 28.5 lakh. A commercial trader in the same position would pay further income tax on that remainder at slab rates.

The clarification calmed the market. But it confirmed that the distinction exists and matters.

There is no simple number

The IRD publishes no bright-line test — no trade count or turnover figure — that separates the two. As in most countries, it depends on the facts:

  • how often and how regularly you trade;
  • whether trading is your main job and livelihood, or a side activity;
  • how large your turnover is compared with your other finances;
  • whether you trade like a shopkeeper turning over stock, or like an owner holding a portfolio;
  • whether you have set it up like a business, with dedicated trading capital and short-cycle strategies.

So this is not only a worry for full-time day traders. Someone who retired early and now trades NEPSE every day, with dozens of round trips a month, is much closer to "commercial" than someone who reviews holdings each quarter and trims twice a year — even though both call themselves retail investors and use the same Meroshare app.

WARNING There is no published trade-count or turnover limit that decides whether you are a "commercial" share investor. The test is about substance. If your trading looks like a business in frequency, scale and time spent, your whole gain could face slab-rate income tax on top of the capital gains tax already withheld. If share trading has become your main activity and income, talk to a chartered accountant before a tax notice arrives.

The tax-smart choice and the sensible choice are the same

A low-turnover, patient approach gets more of your gains taxed at 3.75% rather than 5%. It is also the approach least likely to get you reclassified as a business. Rebalance on purpose, on a fixed yearly or half-yearly schedule, using clear rules — size limits, valuation triggers, a changed investment case — not market noise. Here, as rarely in personal finance, the tax-efficient choice and the sensible choice point the same way.

Lesson 38.5 — DEMAT Architecture: Individual, Family Accounts, and the Associated-Persons Trap

What is allowed

Every NEPSE trade runs through a demat account identified by a BOID (Beneficial Owner Identification number). CDSC issues it, usually through a depository participant (DP) — your broker or a bank — and you use it through Meroshare.

Many families ask whether spreading shares across several family members' accounts can save tax. The honest answer separates what is allowed from what actually saves tax. They are not the same.

  • You may hold more than one demat account in your own name, through different DPs or brokers. That is common and legitimate.
  • You may not use several accounts to apply for the same IPO more than once. CDSC checks applications against your citizenship number and rejects duplicates.
  • Each real family member — a spouse, an adult child with their own citizenship and PAN — may have their own BOID and their own IPO chance. That is a sound way for a household to widen its IPO access.

But it is not a tax-saving device, for a simple reason.

KEY CONCEPT Tax on listed-share gains and dividends is a flat, final rate, whatever the holder's income. So moving shares into a lower-earning family member's account saves nothing. A retired parent with little income and a high-earning professional pay exactly the same 3.75%/5% on gains and 5% on dividends. "Income splitting" works for salary or rent under the slabs; it does not work here.

Many people carry the instinct from salary or rental income, where shifting income to a lower-earning relative really does cut the household's tax. Share-gains and dividend tax in Nepal are fixed-rate, not slab-based, so that tax reason disappears. The real reasons for family accounts remain: succession planning, separate goals, wider IPO access, and keeping each person's money and decisions their own.

The associated-persons trap

A sharper trap awaits anyone who moves existing shares that have risen in value into a relative's account, instead of each person investing their own new money.

Section 45 of the Income Tax Act, 2058 covers transfers between associated persons — in substance, close family and other related parties — where no market price is paid. Such a transfer is not tax-free just because no cash changed hands. The law treats the person giving the shares as if they had sold them at market value that day. That triggers tax on the whole gain built up so far. The receiver's cost then becomes that market value.

WARNING Moving shares that have risen in value into a spouse's, child's or parent's demat account by a BO-to-BO transfer, without a real sale at market price, does not avoid or delay capital gains tax. Under Section 45, the transfer is treated as a sale at market value on that day: tax falls on the full gain, and the receiver's cost resets to market value. There is no lawful way to "gift" a risen NEPSE holding within the family to wipe out the built-up gain.

Keep the two goals separate

If you want the household to invest more and get more IPO chances, the right way is for each member to open their own BOID and fund it with their own money. That money can be a cash gift made before investing — cash has no built-up gain, so it does not have the same problem — or their own income. Their cost then starts with their own purchases.

If your goal is passing an existing, risen holding to the next generation, that is an important but separate conversation — with a tax adviser, and usually in the context of inheritance rather than a lifetime gift. Gifts and inheritance received fall under a different provision (Section 10's exemption for amounts received as gift or inheritance, with its cross-references) from a lifetime transfer of a risen asset between living relatives, and the results differ.

Link your PAN

A PAN is technically optional when opening a demat account, but DPs recommend it, for the reason this chapter cares about: linking your PAN to your BOID from day one makes cost and gain records much easier to match later — for a professional reviewing a loss claim, or a question from the tax office. If you skipped it, add it this week.

Lesson 38.6 — Record-Keeping, Fund Wrappers, and Closing the Tax-Planning Loop

Records are the foundation

Everything so far — timing, losses, turnover, accounts — depends on one dull habit: having records that prove what you paid, how long you held, and what you did. The electronic system helps, but it does not remember everything for you for ever.

CDSC and Meroshare keep a transaction history for every BOID, and Meroshare provides a downloadable yearly capital gains report: your sales, gains and tax withheld for the fiscal year.

PRACTICAL TOOL Download your Meroshare capital gains report every fiscal year, before filing season, not when a dispute arises. It brings together your sales, gains and tax withheld across all companies in that BOID for the year. It is the most useful document for checking your own records against what was withheld, and for supporting any future loss claim (Lesson 38.3).

Where the electronic record is weak

Keep your own copies, outside the broker's system, for three kinds of holding:

  1. Shares from before your account was fully electronic — the cost on record may have been rebuilt, not original.
  2. Bonus and rights shares — they are blended into your average cost, so rebuilding a correct WACC needs the original allotment records, not just today's balance.
  3. Any period when you changed broker or DP — a BO-to-BO transfer moves the shares, but that is exactly when paperwork goes missing.

Mutual funds: a different wrapper

With records in order, the last lever is the vehicle itself: owning shares directly, or owning units in a SEBON-approved mutual fund (a "collective investment scheme" that pools many people's money and invests it for them). This is the closest Nepal has to a tax-advantaged wrapper. It is often described as "tax-free", which overstates one side and understates another.

Section 10 of the Income Tax Act, 2058 exempts the income a SEBON-approved mutual fund earns while pursuing its stated purpose. So when the fund manager buys and sells shares inside the fund, those trades do not each carry capital gains tax, as they would in your own demat account. Your tax comes when you sell or redeem your units. Distributions from the fund to resident unit holders are also treated as exempt — unlike the 5% on dividends paid directly by a listed company.

Think of a shared jeep to Pokhara. You pay once, when you get off; you are not charged at every junction where the driver changes lanes.

This is not advice to stop picking shares yourself. This book teaches you to study individual businesses because doing that well, over many years, can earn returns a fund cannot always match. But for the part of your money where you want broad, spread-out exposure without paying tax every time a manager rebalances, a fund is a real, lawful advantage. Choose it for the right reason — the exemption on internal trading and distributions — not the wrong one, "funds pay no tax", which ignores that your own unit sale is taxed at the same rates as shares.

Chapter recap

  • Rates change often and in both directions — not only at budget time, as 2026 showed. Since 22 September 2026: 3.75% long-term and 5% short-term on listed-share gains for individuals, and 5% final on dividends.
  • Lever 1 — timing. Know each holding's settlement-date anniversary. The 365-day line is a cliff. Split large sales around it where the investment case supports waiting — but never hold a worsening business just to save 1.25 points.
  • Lever 2 — losses. The broker does not net losses against gains. Any loss claim is something you document and make with professional help; review losers once a year before Asar-end.
  • Lever 3 — turnover. Every rebalancing sale is taxed. Heavy, systematic trading can get you treated as a commercial investor, adding slab-rate income tax on top — the FY 2080/81 budget scare showed the risk. Patient, rule-based rebalancing avoids both problems.
  • Lever 4 — accounts. Family BOIDs funded with each member's own money widen IPO access but save no tax, because the rates are flat. Under Section 45, moving risen shares to a relative is treated as a sale at market value.
  • Keep contract notes, DP bills, allotment letters and a yearly Meroshare capital gains report, and link your PAN to your BOID.
  • A SEBON-approved mutual fund exempts internal trading and distributions, but your own unit sale is taxed at the same rates as shares.
