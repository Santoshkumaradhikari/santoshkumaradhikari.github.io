Status of the Canon Score (November 2026) The Canon Score is a structured way to assess company quality, not a proven way to beat the market. Its seven weights and its thresholds are this book's judgement, explained but not yet tested against results. The first out-of-sample test began on 17 November 2026, when Chapter 80 froze scores for the listed commercial banks; it will be judged in November 2027. That pilot uses a reduced, earlier form of the rules (version 1.2-P), not the current version 1.4.1, and one year is only a first look. It is a small pilot on one sector, not a validation of the full 100-point score. The current rules have no out-of-sample record at all. Until stronger evidence exists, use the score as a disciplined checklist that makes you look at every risk — and never as a reason, on its own, to buy or sell. This chapter holds the single master definition of the score; other chapters should point here rather than restate the rules.

In one sentence: the Canon Score is a structured and partly subjective checklist that combines business quality, governance, tradability and price; it has not been shown to predict returns, so it should support your judgement, not replace it.
WHAT THE CANON SCORE CAN AND CANNOT TELL YOU The Canon Score is a structured ranking tool. It is not a measure of a company's true worth, a forecast of future returns, or the chance that an investment will succeed. Its total combines business quality, governance, ease of trading and price. These are related, and some use the same financial figures, so a high score does not guarantee that a share is safe, fairly priced or right for your portfolio.

Always read the Business Quality Score (out of 75) separately from the Share Attractiveness Score (out of 25). A strong company can be a poor buy at too high a price, and a cheap company can be cheap for a good reason — like a beautiful house on a plot that floods every monsoon. Neither the total nor any single dimension should decide a purchase without a separate look at how much you could lose, how easily you could sell, how much of your portfolio is already in similar companies, and how solid the evidence behind each number is.

Version 1.4.1 is a documented scoring method, not a tested investment model. Its weights, thresholds and overrides remain this book's judgement until a forward test shows that they tell you something simpler measures do not. Even a good result from the pilot in November 2027 will not change that: the pilot tests the earlier version 1.2-P for one year. Version 1.4.1 needs its own forward record, kept over several years and through at least one downturn.

The report card came home in Kavita's school bag on a Friday in Falgun. Her father, Suman, a taxi driver in Biratnagar, opened it at the kitchen table. Seven subjects ran down the left side: Nepali, English, Mathematics, Science, Social Studies, Computer and Health. Each had a grade — mostly As, one B, and a C in Mathematics that made Suman sigh. At the bottom sat one number, her overall grade.

Suman needed both things on that page. The overall number told him how the year had gone. The seven grades told him which subject needed a tutor before the next exam.

A company listed on NEPSE deserves the same kind of report card. A company is not simply "good" or "bad". It can have excellent profits and poor governance. It can have a strong business and shares that hardly trade. It can pay generous dividends while quietly diluting shareholders with bonus shares. Chapter 63 explained why an investor needs a fixed, repeatable checklist instead of a gut feeling formed over tea at a broker's office. This chapter builds that checklist. It names the seven subjects, sets the points for each, and shows how to grade a company against thresholds set for NEPSE, not borrowed from Wall Street or Mumbai.

By the end of the chapter you will be able to take a company's annual report, its SEBON filings and its NEPSE trading data, and turn them into one number between 0 and 100: the Canon Quality Score, called the Canon Score for short elsewhere in this book.

Lesson 64.1 — The Seven Dimensions at a Glance

The score has seven dimensions, like Kavita's seven subjects. Each is scored on its own scale, and the seven scores are added together — never averaged — so the highest possible total is exactly 100.

What the Total Measures

Five dimensions describe the business. Two — Liquidity and Valuation — describe the share: how easily it trades and what it costs today. So the total mixes quality with price. The same company can score differently next month simply because its share price moved, the way the same house is a better or worse buy depending on the asking price. Two rules follow:

  • Every score must carry the date of the share price it used.
  • Every score is reported as three numbers, never the total alone: the Business Quality Score (Dimensions 1, 2, 5, 6 and 7, out of 75), the Share Attractiveness Score (Dimensions 3 and 4, out of 25), and the Canon Score (the total, out of 100).

Why These Weights

The weights are not equal. Financial Strength gets the most, 20 points, because a company that cannot earn safe profit eventually fails at everything else: a weak bank cannot keep paying dividends, cannot survive a bad-loan cycle, and cannot protect minority shareholders when capital runs short. Governance comes next at 15, because in a market where promoters hold large blocks of shares and disclosure rules are still young, a dishonest management can destroy a sound business faster than any bad quarter (Part IV). Liquidity gets only 10 points because it matters less to the business — but it matters to you: a fine company whose shares you cannot sell when you need cash has failed you personally.

Be clear about what these reasons are. They are this book's judgement. The weights have not been fitted to, or tested against, investment results, and no test yet shows that 20 points for Financial Strength works better than 15 or 25.

Where the Data Comes From

  • SEBON filings — material events, related-party transactions and changes in promoter holdings.
  • NEPSE data — daily prices, turnover and the days a share traded, used for Liquidity. (Circuit hits are not scored; see Lesson 64.3.)
  • Annual reports and quarterly statements — the main source for Financial Strength, Growth and Dividends.
  • CDSC records — the depository's shareholding registers, for promoter holdings and pledged shares.
  • NRB publications — capital minimums, and the system figures for loans, deposits and bad loans that lenders are compared with.
GROUND RULES FOR EVERY TABLE
  • Boundaries: a row that says "or higher", "or more", "at least", "or below", "or less" or "up to" includes the boundary figure itself — an ROE of exactly 15% is in "15% or higher". Otherwise a figure exactly on a boundary belongs to the range that starts at that figure (a debt-to-equity of exactly 1.0x is in "1.0x–2.0x"), or, if no range starts there, to the range that ends there. "Above" and "below" never include the boundary.
  • If a company fits two rows for any other reason, it takes the lower score. When in doubt, the rubric errs toward caution.
  • Write the rubric version (now 1.4.1), the data cut-off date and the share-price date next to every score.
  • Counting years: a five-year growth record means five year-on-year comparisons, which need six consecutive annual figures. This applies to profit, revenue, loans, deposits and EPS. "Grew" means higher than the year before; anything else counts as flat or declining. A five-year dividend record means the dividends declared for five financial years. A three-year average ROE needs three annual ROEs, and so four year-end balance sheets (each year's opening equity is the previous year's closing equity). Never confuse five figures with five comparisons.
  • Missing data — four different cases: Not applicable means the measure has no meaning for this company (a hydropower project with no earnings has no P/E); follow the rule the table gives for that case, and it does not count as missing. Unavailable means the figure is not published; unverified means it is published but you could not check it (some tables say "not verified"; it means the same). Score either one no higher than the middle row — where a table has an even number of rows, the lower of the two middle rows — unless the table sets its own limit, and write the word beside the score. Estimated means you worked the figure out from partial data; score it normally but write "estimated". If unavailable, unverified and estimated parts together add up to more than 20 of the 100 points, call the whole score "provisional".
  • Point changes: "deduct 1 point" means subtract one point from the table score, never going below 0. "Drop one row" means move to the next row down; a score already in the lowest row stays there. Every table is built so that every company fits at least one row; if you find a case that fits none, score it in the lower of the two nearest rows and report the gap.
  • Swings: a "big swing" is a change of more than 50%, up or down, from one year to the next. A change into or out of a loss always counts as a big swing, because a percentage change from a loss means nothing. "Moderate" means no big swing; "very volatile" or "erratic" means two or more big swings in five years.

Lesson 64.2 — Financial Strength & Profitability (20 points)

This dimension asks one plain question: is the company making real, lasting profit, with enough capital to survive a bad year? It draws on the financial-statement chapters in Part V and the ratio chapters in Part VII, and has three parts.

A. Return on Equity (8 points)

Return on equity (ROE) is profit for each Rs 100 of shareholders' money in the business. It shows how hard the company makes the owners' capital work — like asking a farmer how many muri of rice each ropani of his own land produced.

How to calculate it. For each of the last three years, take the net profit that belongs to ordinary shareholders and divide it by the average equity for that year (opening plus closing, divided by two). Then take the simple average of the three yearly figures, and write all three yearly figures beside the score — an average can hide a bad year. If the company publishes group (consolidated) accounts, use them, and take both lines for the owners of the parent company only: the profit that belongs to them, and the equity that belongs to them. Leave out the share owned by outside (non-controlling) shareholders on both lines, or the result is distorted. If the company publishes only its own accounts, use those. If equity was zero or negative at any year-end, ROE has no meaning: score 1.

The bands are set for NEPSE, not a textbook. The average ROE of Nepal's commercial banks fell from about 13.2% in the third quarter of FY 2022/23 to 7.73% in the same quarter of FY 2024/25, as heavy provisions and slow lending squeezed the sector (NRB figures, reported by The Annapurna Express in April 2025). In that quarter the strongest bank, Everest Bank, earned about 15.8%, and the weakest, NIC Asia Bank, 0.71%. That spread is exactly what the bands separate. ROE on its own, without comparing peers, tells you very little.

One rule overrides the average: a loss year always scores 1. Years of −5%, 20% and 35% average 16.7%, but one excellent year cannot buy back a loss — just as one bumper harvest does not mean the field never floods.

B. Capital Adequacy or Leverage (7 points)

For banks and financial institutions this uses the capital adequacy ratio (CAR): the lender's own capital as a share of its risk-weighted assets. Think of it as the size of the cushion under a stack of fragile pots. For commercial banks, NRB's Capital Adequacy Framework 2015 sets a minimum total capital ratio of 8.5%. Within that, at least 6% must be Tier 1 (core) capital, and at least 4.5% common equity — the shareholders' own money. On top sits a capital conservation buffer of 2.5 percentage points, held in common equity. So the full requirement is 11% total capital, 8.5% Tier 1 and 7% common equity. The minimum means every part of it: a bank that meets the total but misses the Tier 1 or common-equity figure counts as below its minimum.

NRB can also add a counter-cyclical buffer of up to 2.5% in boom years. It required commercial banks to hold an extra 0.5% by mid-July 2024, then set the buffer at 0% for FY 2081/82 (2024/25), as reported in August 2024. The buffer is reviewed through NRB's monetary policy and circulars, so check the rate in force on your scoring date.

Other classes of lender have their own minimums. The latest figures this book could read are in NRB's Financial Stability Report for FY 2023/24, as of mid-July 2024:

Note too that a microfinance institution's 8% capital fund is measured under a simpler framework than a commercial bank's ratio, so the two percentages are not directly comparable.

A lender sitting right at its minimum has no real cushion. One bad-loan cycle can push it below the line. It may then have to slow lending, hold back cash dividends, or raise new capital — often through a rights issue, which dilutes any shareholder who cannot or does not take it up. So the table measures the cushion above the lender's own total-capital minimum, at the latest quarter-end.

Past breaches. A lender that fell below its minimum at any quarter-end in the last 3 years but is above it now scores no more than 3. A breach today scores 0 and also triggers the hard limit in Lesson 64.5.

What CAR does not tell you. These 7 points measure a regulatory cushion, not total safety. A bank at 13% is not necessarily safer than one at 12.5%. What matters as well is what the capital is made of (common equity absorbs losses best), how the bank's risk weights were set, how good its loans really are, and whether provisions are honest. A bank's CAR is like the declared weight limit on a bridge: useful, but you still want to know what the bridge is made of. The bad-loan checks in Dimension 6 and the profit check below cover part of this; stress tests and capital quality are not scored in this version.

For other companies, this part looks at debt instead, with two separate measures that are added together. Debt-to-equity is how many rupees the company has borrowed for every rupee of its owners' money; count all interest-bearing borrowings and lease liabilities as debt. Interest cover is operating profit divided by the interest bill — how many times over the company could pay its interest. Scoring them separately means every company gets a score, whatever its mix.

Leverage (7 points) = debt-to-equity points + interest-cover points. Three cases need care. A company with no interest-bearing debt scores 4 on cover. A company that has debt but shows little or no interest in its income statement — usually because interest on a project under construction is added to the project's cost ("capitalised") — is not debt-free: work out cover using all interest incurred, including the capitalised part. And a company with an operating loss scores 0 on cover, whatever its debt. Zero or negative equity scores 0 on debt-to-equity and is a warning in its own right: the owners' cushion is gone.

These two numbers do not show when the debt must be repaid. A company whose loans all fall due next year is riskier than one with the same debt spread over ten years. Check the debt maturity note beside the score; it is not scored in this version. Hydropower and other utilities are normally built with a lot of debt. Chapter 65 does not yet set a sector version of these tables, so use them as they are and compare hydropower companies only with each other.

C. Earnings Quality (5 points)

This checks whether the profit record is a steady, believable line or a jagged one full of one-off gains, sudden losses or restated numbers. A loss is worse than a fall in profit, so one loss year scores lower than two years of decline.

Profit record: five year-on-year comparisons (six years of figures)Points (of 5)
No loss year; profit grew in at least 4 of the 5 comparisons5
No loss year; profit flat or declining in exactly 2 of the 53
No loss year, but flat or declining in 3 or more of the 5; or exactly one loss year2
Two or more loss years, or erratic (two or more big swings)1

Cash check (non-financial companies). Work out the cash conversion ratio: operating cash flow over the last three years added together, divided by net profit over the same three years added together. If it is below 0.5, deduct 1 point. Profit that never turns into cash is like a shop's credit book full of customers who never pay: it looks good on paper until you need the money. Do not use this check if the three years' profit adds up to zero or less — that company already scores low.

Profit check (lenders). Cash flow is not a useful test for a bank, so look at where the profit came from instead. If, in any of the last three years, more than a quarter of net profit came from writing back earlier loan-loss provisions or from one-off gains such as selling property or investments, deduct 1 point. The asset-quality step in Dimension 6 covers bad loans themselves.

Financial Strength = A + B + C, out of 20. Take the yearly figures from audited annual reports, and the latest CAR from the quarterly reports banks must publish on NEPSE.

Lesson 64.3 — Governance, Liquidity and Valuation

Dimension 2: Governance & Promoter Behaviour (15 points)

Part IV showed that governance is often what decides whether a minority shareholder's investment survives. A promoter is a founder or controlling shareholder who usually holds a large block of shares and controls the board. This dimension has three checks.

Promoter holdings and pledging (6 points). When promoters pledge their shares as security for a loan, a lender can force a sale that crashes the price, whatever the business is doing. Think of a farmer who has mortgaged the family land: the harvest can be good and the land still be lost.

Promoter holding pattern, last 3 yearsPoints (of 6)
Stable or rising; under 10% of promoter shares pledged6
Broadly stable; 10%–25% pledged4
Falling without a stated reason, or 25%–50% pledged2
Falling sharply without a stated reason, more than 50% pledged, or repeated insider selling0
WARNING Pledged shares never appear in the profit and loss account. They show up in CDSC records and in SEBON and NEPSE disclosures. A company can report a healthy quarter while its promoter is one margin call away from a forced sale. Check pledging before you check profit. If you cannot find pledge data, do not assume there is none: score this check at no more than 4 out of 6 and write "pledging not verified".

Where to find pledge and holding data, in this order: (1) the company's own share register or its annual report's shareholding note; (2) SEBON and NEPSE disclosures of pledges, releases and promoter sales; (3) CDSC records. Use the most recent figure from the highest source you can find, and write down which one you used.

Not every fall is a warning. A promoter holding can fall for an ordinary reason: a merger that combined two shareholder lists, a sale the law requires, or a public issue that brings in new shareholders. Count a fall only if it has no stated reason, or if it keeps happening year after year. A merger is like two families joining in marriage — each side's share of the household changes, but nobody has run away with the money.

How big is a fall? Measure the promoter group's holding as a percentage of all the company's shares, after leaving out explained changes. Stable: changed by less than 1 percentage point over 3 years. Broadly stable: fell by 1–3 points. Falling: fell by 3–5 points. Falling sharply: fell by more than 5 points. Repeated insider selling: promoters or directors sold shares, without a stated reason, in at least two of the last three years. If promoters chose not to take up their rights in a rights issue, the resulting fall counts — it was their choice.

Related-party dealings and audit opinion (5 points). Related-party dealings are business the company does with its own insiders: loans, contracts or purchases involving directors, promoters or their families. The auditor's opinion is the auditor's formal verdict on whether the accounts are fair. Read both in the annual report, and use the auditor's own heading for each finding:

  • Emphasis of matter — the accounts are fair; the auditor only points to something worth noticing.
  • Material uncertainty related to going concern — a separate section saying there is real doubt the company can carry on. More serious than an emphasis note.
  • Qualified opinion — the accounts are fair except for a specific problem.
  • Adverse opinion — the accounts are not fair.
  • Disclaimer — the auditor could not form an opinion at all.

Disclosure and board structure (4 points). Does the company file its quarterly reports and hold its AGM on time? Does its board have independent directors — people with no ties to the promoters or managers — beyond the legal minimum? For banks and other financial institutions the rule is in Section 14 of the Bank and Financial Institution Act 2073: a board of five to seven directors, of whom at least one must be appointed under Section 14(3). The Law Commission's English text calls this an "independent director"; another published translation says "professional director". Use the Act's own qualification tests, whatever the label. For other public companies, Section 86 of the Companies Act 2063 allows a board of three to eleven directors (Section 86(2)) and sets the minimum number of independent directors in Section 86(3): at least one on a board of up to seven, and at least two on a larger board, as reported by two legal publishers.

The top row needs more than a head count. Each independent director must meet the law's independence tests, must have attended at least three-quarters of board meetings in the last year, and the audit committee must include at least one of them. If you cannot check these, score 3. Even then, these 4 points measure the board's structure and the company's filing discipline, not whether the board actually stands up to management. Company A with two silent independent directors can outscore Company B with one director who blocks a bad related-party deal. Read the minutes and related-party notes yourself; this version does not score them.

Dimension 3: Liquidity & Tradability (10 points)

Part XI introduced two ideas used here. The first is average daily turnover: the rupee value a share trades on a normal day. The second is the circuit trap: a share that hits its daily price limit with almost no trading, leaving sellers stuck for days (Chapter 58). This dimension scores the first directly. It does not score circuit risk itself — see the note after the free-float table.

Average daily turnover, last 6 monthsPoints (of 5)

Rs 1–5 million (Rs 10–50 lakh)3
Rs 200,000–1 million (Rs 2–10 lakh)1
Below Rs 200,000 (Rs 2 lakh)0

How to measure it. Add up the value traded over the last six months and divide by the number of NEPSE trading days, counting the days the share did not trade at all. Thin-trading rule: if the share traded on fewer than half of those days, score turnover at no more than 1, whatever the average says. A few large block trades can make a usually frozen share look busy, like a village shop that sells a whole year's rice to one wedding party.

Free float (5 points) is the share of a company's stock that is genuinely available for ordinary trading. It leaves out promoter holdings and any other shares that do not normally come to market: government and other strategic holdings, cross-holdings by other companies, locked-in shares, and restricted employee or management shares. SEBON listed these same categories in its October 2026 working paper on a free-float benchmark index (a consultation paper, not yet a rule). If you cannot identify every restricted holding, use the public holding minus any locked-in shares the company discloses, and mark the figure "estimated". More freely tradable shares means deeper trading; a very small float is prone to circuit traps however good the business. Check the annual report's promoter–public split against CDSC records; if they disagree, treat that as a small governance flag.

Read these two checks as floors — a first screen of tradability, not proof that a share is liquid. They separate shares you can trade from shares you cannot; they do not separate liquid from very liquid. In 2026 the middle half of NEPSE trading days turned over about Rs 3.8–7.5 arba. In FY 2025/26 (2082/83), 231 of the 349 securities that traded — about two-thirds — averaged at least Rs 5 million a day, the top band. That count covers all shares, mutual funds and debentures in the dataset, each averaged over all 223 market days, so a security listed part-way through the year is averaged over the full year (Canon calculation from Chapter 11's data). Most commercial banks, with a typical 51/49 promoter–public split and few other restricted holdings, clear the 40% free-float line.

What these 10 points miss. They do not count how often a share hits its upper or lower circuit, how far its price jumps when trading resumes, or whether its turnover bunches into a few busy sessions. A share can show a respectable six-month average and still be impossible to sell in a market-wide panic — like a road that is busy on festival days and empty when you need it. Whether a share is liquid enough for your order is answered by Chapter 56's ADV rule using your actual position size, and the circuit risk by Chapter 58. Chapter 66 makes the ADV check compulsory before every purchase.

Dimension 4: Valuation Reasonableness (15 points)

Part IX warned against judging P/E (price ÷ earnings per share) or P/B (price ÷ book value per share) on their own. On 28 October 2025, NEPSE as a whole traded at about 38 times earnings and 2.8 times book value, against a historical average P/E of about 31, while commercial banks traded at about 15.8 times earnings and 1.5 times book (ShareSansar figures). Near the August 2021 peak, the average commercial bank P/E was about 29. A fixed rule such as "P/E below 15 is cheap" would misjudge almost every sector at once. So the Canon scores valuation against the company's own sector median.

A discount has to be earned. A company at 0.8 times its sector median or below gets the top band — on P/E or on P/B — only if its ROE is at or above the sector's median ROE — and, for a lender, only if its NPL ratio is also at or below its system average, because banks often trade below book when the market doubts their loans. If either test fails, or you cannot verify the sector median, score that part in the in-line row instead: 6 of 8 for P/E, 5 of 7 for P/B. Chapter 80's look back at 2021 — an exploratory observation on data this book had already seen, not proof — found that the cheapest banks in August 2021 fell further than the expensive ones. A low price is often the market's honest verdict on weaker earnings — like a cheap plot of land that floods every monsoon. Ask why it is cheap before you count it as cheap.

How to find the sector median. Use NEPSE's own sector groups, and include only comparable companies — for hydropower, operating plants only. Use the same price date for every company, and book values and earnings from the same latest published quarter, on the same accounting basis. Then build the two medians separately, because they need different peer groups:

  • P/E median: leave out companies with a loss over the last 12 months, since a negative P/E has no meaning. Use each company's last four quarters of EPS (trailing 12 months).
  • P/B median: include loss-making companies as long as their book value is positive — a company losing money still has a meaningful price-to-book. Leave out only companies with zero or negative book value.

Each median needs at least five companies of its own. With fewer, mark that part "unverified" and score it in the in-line row. Take the simple median — the middle company when all are lined up — not the average, so one extreme company cannot drag it. Leaving loss-makers out of the P/B median would be like judging house prices in a town only by the houses that rent out: the median would drift upward. Example: five banks at P/B 1.0x, 1.2x, 1.4x, 1.6x and 0.6x (the last one loss-making) have a median of 1.2x; dropping the loss-maker would make it 1.3x.

Companies with no meaningful profit. An operating company that made a loss over the last 12 months scores 1 of 8 on P/E. A hydropower company that has not yet started commercial operation has no earnings to compare, so its P/E is "not applicable" (not "missing"). Score its P/B out of 7 as usual and scale it up: valuation score = P/B points ÷ 7 × 15, rounded to the nearest whole point, and label it "one measure only". Because such a company cannot pass the ROE test, its P/B cannot reach the top row, so its valuation score is at most 11 (5 ÷ 7 × 15 = 10.7). Version 1.3 instead copied the P/B score into the P/E row, which gave the same evidence credit twice; version 1.4 removed that.

P/E and P/B move together. Both rise and fall with the same share price, so a share that looks cheap on one usually looks cheap on the other. Read the 15 valuation points as one view of the price, not two separate pieces of evidence.

Only the sector median is scored. Two cross-checks go beside the score without changing the points. First, the company's own 5-year median: if a share looks cheap against its sector but expensive against its own history, note it. Second, the plain numbers: write the actual P/E and P/B next to the relative ones. A share at 0.8 times a sector that itself trades at 40 times earnings is cheap only by comparison — on 28 October 2025 the whole market traded at about 38 times. Being the cheapest house on an expensive street does not make it a cheap house.

Lesson 64.4 — Durability, Growth and Dividends

Dimension 5: Sector & Business Model Durability (15 points)

This dimension takes the longest view: will the business still be relevant and protected in ten years? It draws on Part VI, which showed that a bank, a hydropower company, an insurer and a manufacturer must each be read differently.

Moat (8 points). A moat is a lasting barrier that keeps competitors from eating into profits — named after the water-filled ditch around an old fort.

Business positionPoints (of 8)
High entry barriers and evidence that they protect profits: ROE at or above the sector median in at least 4 of the last 5 years (for a lender, with its NPL ratio no higher than its system average); for a hydropower company, a plant in operation that delivered at least 90% of its contracted energy in 2 of the last 3 years, with at least 10 years left on its PPA8
Moderate barriers, or a licence and regulated position without that proof5
Low barriers; commodity-like business with intense competition2
A structural regulatory or technological threat to the business0

A licence is not a moat. A licence is a ticket to enter the field, not a promise of winning the match. Every listed commercial bank holds the same kind of NRB licence, yet in the third quarter of 2024/25 their returns ranged from 15.8% to 0.71%. So a licence alone earns 5 points; the full 8 need evidence that the barrier actually protects profits. Even that is evidence, not proof. Above-median returns can also come from extra borrowing, a good credit cycle or provisions set too low — which is why the test asks for five years and, for lenders, sound loans. A real moat shows itself as returns that stay high through a full cycle.

A PPA, or Power Purchase Agreement, is a long-term contract under which a buyer — usually the Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA) — agrees to buy a plant's electricity at a set price for a set number of years. It makes a hydropower company's revenue far more predictable than a project still under construction. It does not remove every risk: river flows, dry-season output, transmission bottlenecks and how promptly NEA pays still matter (Chapter 72).

Business and counterparty concentration (7 points). A business that depends heavily on one customer, one region, one product or one supplier carries a risk that may not show in a single year's accounts but shows up in the end — like a farmer who grows only one crop and sells it to only one trader. Find the largest single exposure and score that:

  • Non-financial companies: the largest of — one customer's share of revenue, one segment's or product's share of revenue, one region's share of revenue, and one supplier's share of purchases. Use revenue as the denominator for the first three and purchases for the fourth, and write down which exposure was the largest. These are different risks that happen to share a scale; version 1.4.1 scores only the largest.
  • Lenders: credit concentration — the largest share of the loan book in one sector, segment or region, from the sector-wise lending disclosure in the annual report. This measures where repayment could fail, not where income comes from; write "credit concentration" beside the score. Lending to a single borrower or connected group is a different risk, limited by NRB's single-borrower rules; write down the largest disclosed group exposure beside the score, but it is not scored in this version.
Largest single exposurePoints (of 7)

Almost every run-of-river hydropower company sells all its output to NEA under one PPA. Under this table that scores 0. That is a deliberate, provisional penalty, not a judgement that a contract with a state buyer is as risky as one private customer — nor that it is safe: NEA's payment record, transmission lines, curtailment and "deemed energy" terms, the PPA's length and enforceability, and tariff rules all matter. Dimension 6: Growth Trajectory (15 points)

This asks whether income is really growing, or only appears to grow through accounting choices, revaluations or one-off gains.

Revenue growth (8 points). CAGR, the compound annual growth rate, is the steady yearly rate that would take a starting figure to an ending figure over several years. Here: CAGR = (this year's revenue ÷ revenue five years earlier)1/5 − 1, using six annual figures. Revenue that went from Rs 100 crore to Rs 201 crore in five years grew at about 15% a year.

5-year revenue CAGR (six annual figures)Points (of 8)

Negative, or very volatile (two or more big swings)0

When CAGR does not work. If revenue five years ago was zero, or came from less than a full year of operation — a newly commissioned hydropower plant, for example — CAGR is meaningless or absurdly large. Instead, score 3 if revenue grew in at least 3 of the last 4 year-on-year comparisons, and 0 otherwise.

Use operating revenue only. Leave out one-off gains, revaluation gains and other non-operating income. If a merger or acquisition happened within the five years, part of the growth was bought, not earned: compare combined figures for both companies over the whole period, or, if you cannot, score no higher than 6 and write "merger".

Lenders are scored differently: relative loan-growth discipline. Banks, development banks, finance companies and microfinance institutions earn most of their income as interest on loans. Their revenue therefore moves with interest rates as well as with lending, so the size of the loan book is a better measure of how fast they are really expanding. Fast lending at the top of a credit cycle becomes the bad loans of the repair phase (Chapter 0.1). Chapter 80's look back at 2021 (exploratory, in-sample) found that the banks that grew loans fastest at the 2021 peak did worst over the next five years. So a lender's loan growth is compared with its whole system over the same period — all commercial banks for a bank, all development banks for a development bank, all finance companies for a finance company, the microfinance sector for a microfinance institution — using NRB's figures. Measure both as the five-year CAGR of gross loans and advances, over exactly the same six year-ends, and measure deposit growth the same way over the same window. Think of a bus driver on a crowded highway: the safe speed is the speed of the traffic, not the fastest the bus can go. Note what this measures: discipline — how closely the lender keeps pace with its system — not the quality of the growth itself. That is why the adjustments below matter.

  2. Stretched funding. If loans grew more than 5 points faster than deposits, drop one row.
  3. Weak asset quality. Otherwise a lender could improve its ratio simply by writing loans off. If the adjusted ratio is above the class average and higher than a year earlier, drop one row. If restructured or rescheduled loans exceed 5% of total loans, drop one row even if the NPL ratio looks fine (one drop at most for this step). Steps 2 and 3 together cannot take the score below 2.
  4. No verified system figure for the same period: score this part at no more than 5.

Earnings per share (7 points). EPS is the net profit that belongs to ordinary shareholders, divided by the weighted average number of ordinary shares during the year — shares issued part-way through the year count only for the months they existed. It matters more to you than total profit because it counts dilution from new shares.

Start with the EPS the company reports. Nepal's financial reporting standards (NFRS) include NAS 33 Earnings per Share, which mirrors the international standard IAS 33. (The older 2008 Nepal standard on the same subject was NAS 26.) NAS 33 requires a company that issues bonus shares, or rights shares below the market price, to restate the previous year's EPS in its new report, as if the extra shares had always existed. Each annual report therefore shows two years, both on the latest share count at the time. So for each year, take the EPS from the latest report that shows it, then adjust it only for bonus or rights issues made after that report. Never adjust for an issue the report has already reflected — that counts it twice, like subtracting the same expense from your monthly budget two times. Check the EPS note each time: the share count used should match the shares in issue.

Adjusting for a later bonus issue: divide the earlier EPS by (1 + the bonus ratio) for every later bonus issue. After a 10% bonus, last year's EPS of Rs 30 becomes Rs 27.27. A bonus issue cuts the same cake into more slices; without this step, a company that issues bonus shares every year looks as if its earnings are shrinking when they are not.

Rights issues are partly a bonus in disguise. A rights issue sold below the market price brings in new money, and that part of the dilution is real. But the discount is a gift of value to existing holders, and NAS 33 treats that gift like a small bonus issue. The theoretical ex-rights price (TERP) is (old shares × market price + new shares × issue price) ÷ (old shares + new shares), where the market price is the last price before the shares trade without the right. Earlier EPS is divided by market price ÷ TERP. Example: the share trades at Rs 400 and the company offers one new share at Rs 100 for every share held. TERP = (400 + 100) ÷ 2 = Rs 250, so divide earlier years' EPS by 400 ÷ 250 = 1.6. When you cannot tell what a report has already done, rebuild EPS from net profit and the weighted average number of shares, and write down how.

Expect a dip in the year of a rights issue: the new shares count from the day they are issued, but the new money takes time to earn its full return. A one-year fall in EPS after a rights issue is not, on its own, a sign that the business is failing.

EPS on a comparable share count: five year-on-year comparisonsPoints (of 7)

Rose in 2 of the 5, or very volatile (two or more big swings)2

Losses are not growth. A year counts as "rose" only if EPS was positive in that year. If EPS was negative in the latest year, or in two or more of the last five years, the score is at most 2. A smaller loss is an improvement, not growth.

Dimension 7: Dividend & Capital Return Discipline (10 points)

A Nepali company can return money to shareholders as cash dividends, bonus shares or both. The mix matters as much as the amount, because bonus shares feel generous but are not cash, and they dilute future EPS (Chapter 18).

Consistency (6 points). A company that pays every year, at a sensible share of its profit, is behaving predictably. One that skips dividends erratically — often because it breached a capital or liquidity requirement — is signalling stress.

Two definitions keep this check honest. First, a year counts as "paid" only if shareholders received a real cash dividend. In Nepal a bonus issue usually comes with a small cash dividend that exists only to pay the 5% tax on the bonus. A 10% bonus with 0.5263% cash is a typical example: the cash is exactly 5% of the 10.5263% total, so it covers the tax and nothing more. That year counts as a bonus year, not a cash year.

Second, the payout ratio counts cash only: cash dividends ÷ distributable profit. Record bonus shares separately as the bonus capitalisation ratio: the face value of the bonus issue ÷ distributable profit. A bonus moves money from one pocket of the company's equity to another and gives you more share certificates; it is not the same as cash in hand. The 30%–70% range for the cash payout is this book's preference for balance between sharing profit and keeping enough to grow, not a rule of finance. A payout above that range is not automatically generous: a company paying out almost everything may be starving its own future.

Distributable profit is what is left for owners after the reserves the law requires. Take it from the profit-appropriation statement or the statement of changes in equity, not from headline net profit. For banks and other financial institutions, use the figure in the statement NRB requires them to publish. Before anything can be paid, at least 20% of each year's net profit must go to the general reserve until that reserve equals twice the paid-up capital, and at least 10% a year after that (Bank and Financial Institution Act 2073, Section 44). Further amounts go to NRB's regulatory reserve and the corporate social responsibility fund. So distributable profit can be far below net profit, and using the wrong line can halve the payout ratio.

So beside the score, write down the rise in EPS over the last five years divided by the total earnings per share the company kept back (did not pay in cash) over those years, all on the same comparable share count. If a company kept Rs 60 a share over five years and its EPS rose by Rs 6, it earned about 10% on what it kept. Version 1.4.1 records this number but does not yet score it; scoring it waits for testing.

Dividends held back by design. A hydropower company before its commercial operation date, or a bank or insurer barred from paying by its regulator's capital rules, scores 4 of 6 on consistency and 2 of 4 on source, marked "retained by design", instead of zero. A farmer who keeps this year's best seed for next season is not being stingy.

Lesson 64.5 — Combining the Seven Scores Into One Number

Canon Quality Score = Financial Strength (0–20) + Governance (0–15) + Liquidity (0–10) + Valuation (0–15) + Durability (0–15) + Growth (0–15) + Dividends (0–10).

A score of 90 does not by itself justify an 8% position: a high-quality company can still have a volatile price, event risk, a lot in common with your other holdings, or a price that leaves room for a large loss. So the band sets only the ceiling. The concentration limits in Chapter 60 and Chapter 66 still apply, as does the most you can afford to lose, and every purchase must pass Chapter 56's ADV rule for your own order size. A quality score is not a risk budget.

THE GOVERNANCE OVERRIDE One rule can override the sum. It fires in either of two cases: (1) the Governance total is below 5 out of 15; or (2) one governance red flag is severe on its own — the pledging check scores 0 out of 6 (more than half of promoter shares pledged, an unexplained sharp fall in promoter holding, or repeated unexplained insider selling), or the audit check scores 1 or less out of 5 (a qualified opinion on loans, provisions or related-party dealings, a limit on the auditor's work in a material area, a going-concern material uncertainty, an adverse opinion, a disclaimer, or undisclosed related-party dealings). When it fires, the total is capped at 54, the top of the Weak / Avoid band, however well the other six dimensions score. The second trigger closes a loophole: without it, a company with most promoter shares pledged and a qualified audit could still reach 5 out of 15 just by filing on time. A brilliant income statement cannot outvote a dishonest promoter — and neither can a punctual one.

How long does the cap last? Only while the flag is current. Each re-score uses the latest data: the audit check reads the latest audited annual report, pledging reads the latest disclosed figure, and an unexplained promoter fall counts for the three years the table looks back. When the next report clears the flag, the cap lifts. A cap is not a life sentence — but a company that keeps triggering it is telling you something.
OTHER HARD LIMITS (FROM VERSION 1.3)
  • The total is capped at 54 if NRB has declared the company "problematic", if its capital is below the regulatory minimum, or if the auditor gave a disclaimer.
  • The company is excluded, whatever its score, if a court or regulator has found fraud by its management or controlling shareholders.
  • If its accounts have been materially restated, the score is provisional until a full year of new audited figures is out.

Where the Dimensions Overlap

The dimensions are meant to look at different risks, but they are not fully separate. The profit record is read in several places: ROE (8 points) is profit divided by equity; earnings quality (5) and EPS growth (7) both read the same profit history; the dividend record (6) depends on having profit to pay out; and a valuation discount counts only when ROE beats the sector median. Altogether roughly 25–30 of the 100 points rest, directly or indirectly, on the same few years of reported profit.

So one event can move several scores at once. Take a bank hit by a provisioning shock — a year when it must set aside much more for bad loans. Its net profit falls, so ROE falls; earnings quality and EPS growth weaken; cash dividends may be cut; and the share price, and so its valuation scores, may move too. That is not wrong in itself: a provisioning shock really does hurt in all those ways. But it means the score leans heavily on profit and on the credit cycle. One drought shows up in the paddy, the wheat and the farmer's savings all at once.

The emphasis is deliberate — five straight years of clean, rising per-share profit is one of the hardest things for a weak company to fake — but read these sub-scores together, not as separate pieces of evidence. Whether the overlap actually distorts rankings is a question for testing, not argument. Correlation alone does not prove double counting; what matters is whether each part improves decisions made on data it has not seen. Once enough companies are scored, Chapter 80's audit will measure how closely the sub-scores move together (a correlation table and a principal-component analysis, which finds how many independent signals the seven dimensions really contain), drop one dimension at a time to see whether the order of companies changes, and compare the result with simple equal weights.

Comparing Companies

Compare totals within a sector, and compare the Business Quality Score and the Share Attractiveness Score as well as the total. Chapter 65 explains how each dimension should be read for banks, hydropower, insurers and microfinance; where it sets an exact rule, that rule applies, and otherwise the tables in this chapter do. The aim is that a 75 means similar quality in any sector, but no test has yet shown that it does — and for hydropower it plainly does not yet, because of the single-buyer concentration rule. Across sectors, compare bands, and with care. And always read the seven sub-scores before the total. Two companies can both score 75 for very different reasons — one solid everywhere but pricey, the other excellent on growth but only middling on governance — just as Suman read Kavita's Mathematics grade before her overall result.

Three Harder Cases

HUBL is a clean, healthy bank, so it does not test the awkward rules. These three invented companies do. Only the parts that matter are shown.

  • A heavily borrowed manufacturer ("Kanchan Cement", invented). Debt-to-equity 2.4x → 1 of 3; interest cover 2.2x → 1 of 4; leverage 2 of 7. Profit grew in 4 of 5 comparisons (5 of 5 on the table), but operating cash flow over three years was only 0.45 times profit, so deduct 1 point → 4 of 5. Strong profit growth on paper, weak cash behind it: the score shows both.
  • A hydropower project not yet producing ("Tamor Khola Hydro", invented). No earnings, so P/E is "not applicable" — which does not count towards the provisional limit. P/B is 0.7x the median of operating hydropower companies with positive book value, but with no ROE it cannot pass the discount test, so P/B scores 5 of 7 → valuation 5 ÷ 7 × 15 = 10.7, rounded to 11 of 15, marked "one measure only". Dividends are retained by design: 4 of 6 plus 2 of 4. Selling all its power to NEA scores 0 on concentration, as it does for almost every hydropower company (see the note under that table).
  • A high-earning bank with hidden strain ("Sagarmatha Growth Bank", invented). 3-year ROE 17%, never below 10% → 8 of 8. But 30% of last year's profit came from writing back old provisions → earnings quality minus 1. Its gross NPL ratio, after adding back write-offs, is 5.1% against a class average of 4.0% (illustrative) and rising → drop one row in loan growth. Its moat cannot reach 8 because its NPL is above the system. And 55% of promoter shares are pledged → pledging 0 of 6, which fires the governance override: whatever the other dimensions add up to, the Canon Score is capped at 54. A high ROE cannot outvote a promoter one margin call away from a forced sale.

  • The HUBL example now shows the evidence each row needs, marking what is assumed.
HUBL's score is unchanged at 87. No other score has been published under 1.4.

Version 1.4 · adopted 22 Nov 2026 (6 Mangsir 2083). Version 1.4 was a technical patch to version 1.3, adopted the same day after a second audit. It keeps the seven weights and the score bands (85–100 Exceptional and so on) and fixes the scoring mechanics:
  • A project with no earnings no longer gets its P/B credit twice: P/B is scaled to 15 points instead.
  • Debt for non-financial companies is scored as debt-to-equity (3 points) plus interest cover (4), so every company fits a row.
  • "Revenue concentration" becomes business and counterparty concentration, with a zero row; for lenders it is credit concentration.
  • Free float now leaves out every kind of restricted holding, not just promoter shares.
  • Exact rules for point deductions, the cash conversion ratio, a profit check for lenders, consolidated ROE, past CAR breaches, promoter falls, board effectiveness, gross NPL with write-offs added back, restructured loans, loss years in EPS, and cash-only payout.
  • Every score is reported as Business Quality, Share Attractiveness and the total; position limits are labelled provisional.
Version 1.3, adopted earlier on 22 November 2026, had replaced judgement with point bands for the audit, board, P/B, free-float and leverage checks; judged CAR against each class's minimum; corrected the lender and EPS definitions; counted only real cash dividends as "paid"; and added hard limits for "problematic" status, capital below the minimum, an audit disclaimer and fraud. The only score published under 1.3 was HUBL's.

Clarifications to 1.4 (22 November 2026). Four cases the 1.4 tables left open were settled: a 15%+ ROE average with any year below 10% scores 6 (in 1.4.1: unless a year was a loss); a zero or part-year starting revenue uses the growth-years test instead of CAGR; "consistently" in the payout table means 2 or more of the 5 years; and lenders' payout uses NRB-defined distributable profit. No published score changes.

Earlier records are kept as they were. Version 1.2 (frozen 17 November 2026) and Chapter 80's forward record, version 1.2-P (pilot) — four dimensions (60 points), later five (70 points), with two years of results and four years of dividends — are not re-scored. The case studies of Nabil (Chapter 83, 75), Chhimek (Chapter 85, 77) and Karnali Development Bank (Chapter 89) remain version 1.2 scores, labelled as such, until they are re-checked. Version 1.2 had made two changes to version 1.1: lender loan growth against the whole system, and a valuation discount that counts only with above-median ROE. Version 1.1 had closed table gaps, defined EPS growth on a comparable share count, added the severe-red-flag trigger and aligned the bands with Chapter 66.

Testing status. Version 1.4.1 has no out-of-sample record at all. The forward pilot that began on 17 November 2026 tests version 1.2-P, and its November 2027 review will be a first-year look — far too short to show whether the score predicts long-term returns. Versions 1.2 to 1.4.1 were all written after seeing the 2021 bank data, so that data cannot prove they work. In a falling market, protection comes from Chapter 59's cycle settings, not from the score. A fair test needs scores frozen before results are known, every company in the sector (including those that later merged, failed or delisted), and returns that include dividends. It should then ask:
  • Ranking: do higher-scored companies go on to do better (rank correlation), within each sector as well as overall?
  • Returns and risk: total return including dividends, return per unit of risk, and the deepest fall from peak (maximum drawdown).
  • Simpler rivals: does the weighted score beat equal weights, and does it add anything beyond simple measures such as ROE, P/B and leverage alone? If not, the extra complexity is not earning its keep.
  • Dimension removal: does dropping any one dimension make the ranking better or worse?
  • Stability: does a small correction to one figure move the score a lot?
  • Point-in-time data: each past score must use only figures that had been published by its date — no later restatements.
  • Costs and execution: broker commission, SEBON and CDSC fees, and slippage on thin NEPSE shares, so the result is something an investor could actually have earned.
  • Benchmarks: the NEPSE index and the relevant sector index, not just zero.
  • Rebalancing: a rule, fixed in advance, for when scores are refreshed and when holdings change.
  • Market phases: results in rising, falling, flat and credit-stress periods, with annual return, volatility, the Sharpe ratio (return per unit of risk), downside deviation and portfolio turnover reported for each.
  • Replication: the same method, unchanged, repeated on a separate period.

STILL JUDGEMENT IN VERSION 1.4.1 A few calls still need a written reason, marked "judgement", because no simple rule fits every company: how to group customers, products and regions in the concentration check; how to treat a hydropower company's single NEA buyer until Chapter 65 sets a sector table; what counts as a "structural threat" in the moat check; when a gain is "one-off" in the dividend-source check; and what counts as "small" or "large" related-party dealings in the audit check.

Considered for a later version 2.0 — only after a test. Some improvements would change what the score measures, so they should wait for evidence: giving each indicator one "home" dimension so the profit record is not counted several times; judging growth by the return earned on new capital, not just the growth rate; comparing the current weights with simpler alternatives, such as equal weights, to see whether they actually rank companies better; scoring the return on retained capital rather than only recording it; scoring customer, regional, product and supplier concentration separately; judging a lender's valuation with asset quality and its cost of capital, not ROE alone; recording how long and how deep a past capital breach was; judging board effectiveness by documents — related-party approvals and their terms, board challenges to management, auditor independence and rotation, follow-up of audit findings, treatment of minority shareholders and capital-allocation decisions — never by unsupported claims; a circuit-risk adjustment for liquidity; capital quality and stress tests for lenders; debt maturity for other companies; and a sector table for hydropower concentration.

Chapter recap

  • One number, seven parts. The Canon Quality Score adds seven dimensions to a total out of 100: Financial Strength (20), Governance (15), Liquidity (10), Valuation (15), Durability (15), Growth (15) and Dividends (10).
  • Three numbers, not one. Report the Business Quality Score (75), the Share Attractiveness Score (25) and the total. The total changes with the share price and must carry its price date.
  • Set for NEPSE. ROE bands follow the real spread of Nepali bank returns, CAR is judged as a regulatory cushion against each class's NRB minimum, and valuation against sector medians, with the plain numbers written beside them.
  • Two lessons from 2021 are built in. A cheap price counts only with above-median ROE, and a lender's loan growth is judged against its system.
  • Some red flags override everything. A low governance score, one severe governance flag, "problematic" status, capital below the minimum or an audit disclaimer caps the total at 54; proven fraud excludes the company.
  • Read the parts, not just the total. About 25–30 points rest on the same profit record; compare totals within a sector; and read every sub-score before the total.
  • A checklist, not a proof. The weights are judgement. Version 1.4.1 (23 November 2026) fixes the scoring steps but has no test record; version 1.2 and its pilot record stay frozen, and the pilot's first-year review comes in November 2027. Position limits tied to the bands are provisional ceilings, not evidence-based sizes.
