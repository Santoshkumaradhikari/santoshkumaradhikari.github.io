Status of the Canon Score (November 2026) The Canon Score is a structured way to assess company quality, not a proven way to beat the market. Its seven weights and its thresholds are this book's judgement, explained but not yet tested against results. The first out-of-sample test began on 17 November 2026, when Chapter 80 froze scores for the listed commercial banks; it will be judged in November 2027. That is a small pilot on one sector, not a validation of the full 100-point score. Until stronger evidence exists, use the score as a disciplined checklist that makes you look at every risk — and never as a reason, on its own, to buy or sell. This chapter holds the single master definition of the score; other chapters should point here rather than restate the rules.

The report card came home in Kavita's school bag on a Friday in Falgun. Her father, Suman, a taxi driver in Biratnagar, opened it at the kitchen table. Seven subjects ran down the left side: Nepali, English, Mathematics, Science, Social Studies, Computer and Health. Each had a grade — mostly As, one B, and a C in Mathematics that made Suman sigh. At the bottom sat one number, her overall grade.

Suman needed both things on that page. The overall number told him how the year had gone. The seven grades told him which subject needed a tutor before the next exam.

A company listed on NEPSE deserves the same kind of report card. A company is not simply "good" or "bad". It can have excellent profits and poor governance. It can have a strong business and shares that hardly trade. It can pay generous dividends while quietly diluting shareholders with bonus shares. Chapter 63 explained why an investor needs a fixed, repeatable checklist instead of a gut feeling formed over tea at a broker's office. This chapter builds that checklist. It names the seven subjects, sets the points for each, and shows how to grade a company against thresholds set for NEPSE, not borrowed from Wall Street or Mumbai.

By the end of the chapter you will be able to take a company's annual report, its SEBON filings and its NEPSE trading data, and turn them into one number between 0 and 100: the Canon Quality Score, called the Canon Score for short elsewhere in this book.

Lesson 64.1 — The Seven Dimensions at a Glance

The score has seven dimensions, like Kavita's seven subjects. Each is scored on its own scale, and the seven scores are added together — never averaged — so the highest possible total is exactly 100.

What the Total Measures

Five dimensions describe the business. Two — Liquidity and Valuation — describe the share: how easily it trades and what it costs today. So the total mixes quality with price. The same company can score differently next month simply because its share price moved, the way the same house is a better or worse buy depending on the asking price. Two rules follow:

  • Every score must carry the date of the share price it used.
  • For a reading of the business alone, add Dimensions 1, 2, 5, 6 and 7 (75 points), and read Dimensions 3 and 4 (25 points) separately.

Why These Weights

The weights are not equal. Financial Strength gets the most, 20 points, because a company that cannot earn safe profit eventually fails at everything else: a weak bank cannot keep paying dividends, cannot survive a bad-loan cycle, and cannot protect minority shareholders when capital runs short. Governance comes next at 15, because in a market where promoters hold large blocks of shares and disclosure rules are still young, a dishonest management can destroy a sound business faster than any bad quarter (Part IV). Liquidity gets only 10 points because it matters less to the business — but it matters to you: a fine company whose shares you cannot sell when you need cash has failed you personally.

Be clear about what these reasons are. They are this book's judgement. The weights have not been fitted to, or tested against, investment results, and no test yet shows that 20 points for Financial Strength works better than 15 or 25.

Where the Data Comes From

  • SEBON filings — material events, related-party transactions and changes in promoter holdings.
  • NEPSE data — daily prices, turnover and circuit hits, used mainly for Liquidity.
  • Annual reports and quarterly statements — the main source for Financial Strength, Growth and Dividends.
  • CDSC records — the depository's shareholding registers, for promoter holdings and pledged shares.
GROUND RULES FOR EVERY TABLE
  • A figure exactly on a boundary — an ROE of exactly 15%, say — belongs to the higher band.
  • If a company fits two rows for any other reason, it takes the lower score. When in doubt, the rubric errs toward caution.
  • Write the rubric version (now 1.2), the data cut-off date and the share-price date next to every score.
  • If you could not check something, write "not verified" next to it. Some tables set a limit for this case; the others are listed under "Known gaps" at the end of the chapter.

Lesson 64.2 — Financial Strength & Profitability (20 points)

This dimension asks one plain question: is the company making real, lasting profit, with enough capital to survive a bad year? It draws on the financial-statement chapters in Part V and the ratio chapters in Part VII, and has three parts.

A. Return on Equity (8 points)

Return on equity (ROE) is profit for each Rs 100 of shareholders' money in the business. It shows how hard the company makes the owners' capital work.

The bands are set for NEPSE, not a textbook. The average ROE of Nepal's commercial banks fell from about 13.2% in the third quarter of FY 2022/23 to 7.73% in the same quarter of FY 2024/25, as heavy provisions and slow lending squeezed the sector (NRB figures, reported by The Annapurna Express in April 2025). In that quarter the strongest bank, Everest Bank, earned about 15.8%, and the weakest, NIC Asia Bank, 0.71%. That spread is exactly what the bands separate. ROE on its own, without comparing peers, tells you very little.

3-year average ROEPoints (of 8)
15% or higher, with no single year below 10%8
10%–15%, or 15% or higher with one year below 10%6
7%–10% (around the 2024/25 system average)4
Below 7%, or negative in any of the last 3 years1

B. Capital Adequacy or Leverage (7 points)

For banks and financial institutions this uses the capital adequacy ratio (CAR): the bank's own capital as a share of its risk-weighted assets. Think of it as the size of the cushion under a stack of fragile pots. NRB requires commercial banks to hold a total CAR of at least 11%, which includes a capital conservation buffer of 2.5 percentage points, plus a minimum of core (Tier 1) capital. A bank sitting right at 11% has no real cushion: one bad-loan cycle can push it below the line and force a rights issue that dilutes shareholders. For other companies, this part uses debt-to-equity and interest cover instead.

Capital or leverage positionPoints (of 7)
CAR 13% or higher (banks and financial institutions); or debt-to-equity under 1.0x with interest cover over 5x (others)7
CAR 11.5%–13%; or moderate leverage with adequate cover5
CAR 11.0%–11.5% (barely above NRB's minimum)3
CAR below 11% at any point in the last 3 years; or high leverage with weak cover0

C. Earnings Quality (5 points)

This checks whether the profit record is a steady, believable line or a jagged one full of one-off gains, sudden losses or restated numbers.

5-year profit historyPoints (of 5)
No loss year; profit grew in at least 4 of the last 5 years5
One loss year, or two flat or declining years3
No loss year, but profit flat or declining in 3 or more of the 5 years2
Two or more loss years, or very erratic swings1

Financial Strength = A + B + C, out of 20. Take the 3- and 5-year figures from audited annual reports, and the latest CAR from the quarterly reports banks must publish on NEPSE.

Lesson 64.3 — Governance, Liquidity and Valuation

Dimension 2: Governance & Promoter Behaviour (15 points)

Part IV showed that governance is often what decides whether a minority shareholder's investment survives. A promoter is a founder or controlling shareholder who usually holds a large block of shares and controls the board. This dimension has three checks.

Promoter holdings and pledging (6 points). When promoters pledge their shares as security for a loan, a lender can force a sale that crashes the price, whatever the business is doing. Think of a farmer who has mortgaged the family land: the harvest can be good and the land still be lost.

Promoter holding pattern (CDSC records, last 3 years)Points (of 6)
Stable or rising; under 10% of promoter shares pledged6
Broadly stable; 10%–25% pledged4
Falling, or 25%–50% pledged2
Falling sharply, more than 50% pledged, or repeated insider selling0
WARNING Pledged shares never appear in the profit and loss account. They show up only in CDSC shareholding records and SEBON event filings. A company can report a healthy quarter while its promoter is one margin call away from a forced sale. Check pledging before you check profit. If you cannot find pledge data, do not assume there is none: score this check at no more than 4 out of 6 and write "pledging not verified".

Related-party dealings and audit opinion (5 points). Read the related-party note and the auditor's opinion in the annual report. A clean (unqualified) opinion with small, well-disclosed related-party dealings scores near full marks. A qualified opinion, a going-concern note from the auditor, or large undisclosed loans to related parties score near zero.

Disclosure and board independence (4 points). Does the company file its quarterly reports and event disclosures on time with SEBON and NEPSE? Does its board meet the minimum number of independent directors, rather than being filled with promoter nominees?

Version 1.2 gives no point bands for these last two checks, so score them with written reasons. Proposed bands are listed under "Known gaps".

Dimension 3: Liquidity & Tradability (10 points)

Part XI introduced two ideas used here. The first is average daily turnover: the rupee value a share trades on a normal day. The second is the circuit trap: a share that hits its daily price limit with almost no trading, leaving sellers stuck for days (Chapter 58).

Average daily turnover, last 6 monthsPoints (of 5)
Rs 5 million (Rs 50 lakh) or more5
Rs 1–5 million (Rs 10–50 lakh)3
Rs 200,000–1 million (Rs 2–10 lakh)1
Below Rs 200,000 (Rs 2 lakh)0

Free float (5 points) is the share of a company's stock held by the public rather than promoters. A free float of 40% or more scores full marks, because more shares in public hands means deeper trading. Below 10% scores zero, because such a share is prone to circuit traps however good the business. Version 1.2 does not set the bands in between (see "Known gaps"). Check the annual report's promoter–public split against CDSC records; if they disagree, treat that as a small governance flag.

Read these two checks as floors. They separate shares you can trade from shares you cannot; they do not separate liquid from very liquid. In 2026 the middle half of NEPSE trading days turned over about Rs 3.8–7.5 arba, and in FY 2025/26 (2082/83) about two-thirds of listed securities averaged at least Rs 5 million a day — the top band (Canon calculation from Chapter 11's data). Almost every commercial bank, with its typical 51/49 promoter–public split, clears the 40% free-float line. Whether a share is liquid enough for your order is a separate question, answered by Chapter 56's ADV rule using your actual position size. Chapter 66 makes that check compulsory before every purchase.

Dimension 4: Valuation Reasonableness (15 points)

Part IX warned against judging P/E (price ÷ earnings per share) or P/B (price ÷ book value per share) on their own. NEPSE as a whole has at times traded well above 30 times earnings, while banks have often traded nearer 15–16 times earnings and about 1.5 times book. A fixed rule such as "P/E below 15 is cheap" would misjudge almost every sector at once. So the Canon scores valuation against the company's own sector median.

Current P/E compared with the sector medianPoints (of 8)
0.8x the sector median or below8
0.8x–1.1x (in line)6
1.1x–1.5x3
Above 1.5x, or no P/E because of losses1

P/B (7 points) uses the same logic against the sector median P/B: in line with the sector scores 5 of 7, and 1.1x–1.5x scores 3, as the worked examples in this book apply it.

A discount has to be earned. A company at 0.8 times its sector median or below gets the top band — on P/E or on P/B — only if its ROE is at or above the sector's median ROE. If its ROE is lower, or you cannot verify the sector median, score that part in the in-line band instead: 6 of 8 for P/E, 5 of 7 for P/B. Chapter 80's backtest found that the cheapest banks in August 2021 fell further than the expensive ones. A low price is often the market's honest verdict on weaker earnings — like a cheap plot of land that floods every monsoon. Ask why it is cheap before you count it as cheap.

Only the sector median is scored. The company's own 5-year median is a cross-check: if a share looks cheap against its sector but expensive against its own history, note it beside the score, but it does not change the points. Take sector medians from NEPSE's published sector data and the company's history from any financial portal that keeps five years of prices.

Lesson 64.4 — Durability, Growth and Dividends

Dimension 5: Sector & Business Model Durability (15 points)

This dimension takes the longest view: will the business still be relevant and protected in ten years? It draws on Part VI, which showed that a bank, a hydropower company, an insurer and a manufacturer must each be read differently.

Moat (8 points). A moat is a lasting barrier that keeps competitors from eating into profits — named after the water-filled ditch around an old fort.

Business positionPoints (of 8)
Licensed, regulated sector with high entry barriers and a defensible position (for example, an established commercial bank, or a hydropower company with a signed PPA)8
Moderate barriers; a meaningful but not dominant position5
Low barriers; commodity-like business with intense competition2
A structural regulatory or technological threat to the business0

A PPA, or Power Purchase Agreement, is a long-term contract under which a buyer — usually the Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA) — agrees to buy a plant's electricity at a set price for a set number of years. It makes a hydropower company's revenue far more predictable than a project still under construction. It does not remove every risk: river flows, dry-season output, transmission bottlenecks and how promptly NEA pays still matter (Chapter 72).

Revenue concentration (7 points). A business that depends on one customer, one region or one supplier carries a risk that may not show in a single year's accounts but shows up in the end.

Revenue concentrationPoints (of 7)
Diversified; no customer or segment above 20% of revenue7
20%–40% from one segment or counterparty4
Above 40% from one counterparty2

Almost every run-of-river hydropower company sells all its output to NEA under one PPA. Under this generic table that scores poorly, even though it is unavoidable for the whole sector. Chapter 65 replaces the generic table with a sector version for cases like this.

Dimension 6: Growth Trajectory (15 points)

This asks whether income is really growing, or only appears to grow through accounting choices, revaluations or one-off gains.

Revenue growth (8 points). CAGR, the compound annual growth rate, is the steady yearly rate that would take a starting figure to an ending figure over several years.

5-year revenue CAGRPoints (of 8)
15% or higher, positive in at least 4 of 5 years8
8%–15%6
0%–8%3
Negative, or very volatile from year to year0

Lenders are scored differently. For banks, development banks, finance companies and microfinance institutions, "revenue" is really the loan book, and fast lending at the top of a credit cycle becomes the bad loans of the repair phase (Chapter 0.1). Chapter 80's backtest found that the banks that grew loans fastest at the 2021 peak did worst over the next five years. So a lender's loan growth is compared with its whole system over the same period — all commercial banks for a bank, the microfinance sector for a microfinance institution — using NRB's figures. Think of a bus driver on a crowded highway: the safe speed is the speed of the traffic, not the fastest the bus can go.

Lender's loan growth compared with its system (same period)Points (of 8)
Within 5 percentage points of the system, either way8
5–10 points faster or slower than the system5
More than 10 points faster or slower, or shrinking while the system grows2

Three adjustments apply to lenders:

  • At a credit peak — when the checklist in Section 0.3.7 shows system credit growth above 20%, or the system CD ratio within five points of NRB's ceiling — a lender growing more than 5 points faster than its system scores 0.
  • Stretched funding: if loans grew more than 5 points faster than deposits, drop one row.
  • No verified system figure for the same period: score this part at no more than 5.

Earnings per share (7 points). EPS is net profit divided by the number of shares. It matters more to you than total profit because it counts dilution from new shares. Before comparing years, adjust for bonus shares: divide each earlier year's EPS by (1 + the bonus ratio) for every later bonus issue. After a 10% bonus, last year's EPS of Rs 30 becomes Rs 27.27. A bonus issue cuts the same cake into more slices; without this step, a company that issues bonus shares every year looks as if its earnings are shrinking when they are not. Rights shares are different: they bring in new money, so their dilution is real and is not adjusted away.

Bonus-adjusted EPS, last 5 yearsPoints (of 7)
Rose in at least 4 of 5 years, with no swing above 50% in a single year7
Rose in 3 of 5 years, moderate swings4
Rose in 2 of 5 years, or very volatile2
Rose in fewer than 2 of 5 years, or negative in 3 or more years0

Dimension 7: Dividend & Capital Return Discipline (10 points)

A Nepali company can return money to shareholders as cash dividends, bonus shares or both. The mix matters as much as the amount, because bonus shares feel generous but are not cash, and they dilute future EPS (Chapter 18).

Consistency (6 points). A company that pays every year, at a sensible share of its profit, is behaving predictably. One that skips dividends erratically — often because it breached a capital or liquidity requirement — is signalling stress.

Dividend record, last 5 yearsPoints (of 6)
Paid every year; payout consistently 30%–70% of distributable profit6
Paid in 4 of 5 years, or payout consistently very low or very high4
Paid in 2 or 3 of 5 years, erratic2
Paid in 1 or 0 of the last 5 years0

Where the money came from (4 points). Was the dividend paid from real distributable profit, or from reserves and one-off gains — real wealth shared, or a good headline manufactured?

Source of dividendsPoints (of 4)
Genuine distributable profit; reserves intact or growing4
Partly one-off or revaluation gains2
Drawn from capital or reserves0

Lesson 64.5 — Combining the Seven Scores Into One Number

Canon Quality Score = Financial Strength (0–20) + Governance (0–15) + Liquidity (0–10) + Valuation (0–15) + Durability (0–15) + Growth (0–15) + Dividends (0–10).

ScoreBandLargest position this book allows (Chapter 66)
85–100Exceptional — the highest-quality band; a candidate for a core, long-term holding6%–8% of the equity part of your portfolio
70–84Strong — a solid long-term holding, with normal monitoring4%–6%
55–69Adequate — only a small satellite position, with a time-bound reason and a close watch on its weak dimensions1.5%–3%
45–54Weak / Avoid — watch list onlyNo new money
Below 45Weak / Avoid — exclude; existing holders open a formal reviewNo new money

These sizes are risk limits, not forecasts of return. Every purchase must also pass Chapter 56's ADV rule for your own order size.

THE GOVERNANCE OVERRIDE One rule can override the sum. It fires in either of two cases: (1) the Governance total is below 5 out of 15; or (2) one governance red flag is severe on its own — the pledging check scores 0 out of 6 (more than half of promoter shares pledged, a sharp fall in promoter holding, or repeated insider selling), or the audit check scores 1 or less out of 5 (a qualified or adverse opinion, a going-concern note, or large undisclosed related-party loans). When it fires, the total is capped at 54, the top of the Weak / Avoid band, however well the other six dimensions score. The second trigger closes a loophole: without it, a company with most promoter shares pledged and a qualified audit could still reach 5 out of 15 just by filing on time. A brilliant income statement cannot outvote a dishonest promoter — and neither can a punctual one.

Where the Dimensions Overlap

The dimensions are meant to look at different risks, but they are not fully separate. The profit record is read in several places: ROE (8 points) is profit divided by equity; earnings quality (5) and EPS growth (7) both read the same profit history; the dividend record (6) depends on having profit to pay out; and a valuation discount counts only when ROE beats the sector median. Altogether roughly 25–30 of the 100 points rest, directly or indirectly, on the same few years of reported profit. That emphasis is deliberate — five straight years of clean, rising per-share profit is one of the hardest things for a weak company to fake — but it has a cost: one bad or restated year can move the total by more than any single table suggests. Read these sub-scores together, not as separate pieces of evidence.

Comparing Companies

Compare totals within a sector. Chapter 65 adjusts several sub-scores for banks, hydropower, insurers and microfinance, so that each is judged fairly; the aim is that a 75 means similar quality in any sector, but no test has yet shown that it does. Across sectors, compare bands, and with care. And always read the seven sub-scores before the total. Two companies can both score 75 for very different reasons — one solid everywhere but pricey, the other excellent on growth but only middling on governance — just as Suman read Kavita's Mathematics grade before her overall result.

Lesson 64.6 — A Worked Example: Scoring an Illustrative Bank

"Himalaya Unnati Bank Ltd" (HUBL) is an invented company built for teaching. It is not a real listed bank, and no real bank's data was used. Its figures sit in the realistic range for a well-run but unspectacular Nepali commercial bank.

HUBL's raw data:

  • 3-year average ROE 14.2%, weakest year 11.0%
  • CAR 12.8%, stable for 3 years
  • Profit grew in 4 of the last 5 years, with one flat year during a provisioning cycle
  • Promoter holding stable at 51% for 5 years; under 10% pledged
  • Unqualified audit opinion; related-party loans modest and fully disclosed
  • Quarterly filings on time; 2 of 7 board seats independent
  • Average daily turnover Rs 3.2 million over the last 6 months; free float 49%
  • P/E 13.5x against a banking-sector median of about 15.8x; P/B 1.3x against about 1.5x
  • Licensed, NRB-regulated business; about 30% of the loan book in one regional corporate segment
  • Loan book grew 11% a year over 5 years, against about 9% for the banking system, with deposits growing in step; bonus-adjusted EPS rose in 4 of the last 5 years
  • Cash-plus-bonus dividend paid every year for 5 years, within the 30%–70% payout band, from genuine distributable profit

HUBL scores 85, just inside the Exceptional band. Under version 1.1 it scored 83, because its 11% loan growth fell in the old fixed 8%–15% band and earned 6 of 8; version 1.2 compares a lender's loan growth with its whole system, and growing in step with the system earns full marks. A two-point change across a band line is a reminder to read the sub-scores, not the label.

The sub-scores show where the other 15 points went: a capital cushion thinner than ideal, a fair amount of lending in one segment, and only average trading liquidity. None of these is a red flag, and none triggers the override. Together they are the three things to watch in next year's annual report. That is the purpose of scoring in seven parts: not a verdict, but a diagnosis.

Version, Testing and Known Gaps

RUBRIC VERSION 1.2 · FROZEN 17 NOV 2026 (1 MANGSIR 2083) Write the version number next to every score, so that scores are compared only with scores made under the same rules. Version 1.2 made two changes to version 1.1 (16 November 2026), both prompted by Chapter 80's backtest: a lender's loan growth is now scored against its whole system, with a cap at credit peaks and a funding check; and a valuation discount earns the top band only when ROE is at or above the sector median. The case studies were re-checked: Nabil (Chapter 83) moves from 73 to 75, Chhimek (Chapter 85) stays at 77 with different sub-scores, and Karnali Development Bank (Chapter 89) is unchanged. Version 1.1 had earlier closed gaps and overlaps in the tables, defined EPS growth as bonus-adjusted, added the severe-red-flag trigger with a cap of 54, aligned the bands with Chapter 66 and capped unverified pledging. The seven weights have never changed.

Testing status. Version 1.2 was written after seeing the 2021 bank data, so that data cannot prove it works. Applied to the same banks, it removes the negative five-year link but does not protect against a crash. Chapter 80's forward record uses a reduced form — four dimensions (60 points), later five (70 points), with two years of results and four years of dividends — so label it version 1.2-P (pilot) whenever you compare with it. In a falling market, protection comes from Chapter 59's cycle settings, not from the score.

The full rules are also published as a data file — Canon Score specification, version 1.2 (JSON) — with the claim-by-claim checks behind this chapter in the Chapter 64 evidence register (CSV).
KNOWN GAPS IN VERSION 1.2 — PROPOSED VERSION 1.3 (NOT YET IN FORCE) An audit in November 2026 found places where version 1.2 does not say exactly how to score, so two careful investors could disagree. Until version 1.3 is adopted, score these with written reasons and mark them "judgement". The proposed rules would apply only to scores made after adoption, never to the frozen version 1.2-P record.
  • Related-party dealings and audit (5 points): 5 = clean (unqualified) opinion and small, fully disclosed related-party dealings; 3 = clean opinion but an emphasis-of-matter note, a restatement, or large though disclosed related-party dealings; 1 = qualified opinion, going-concern note, or related-party dealings disclosed late or incompletely; 0 = adverse opinion, disclaimer, or undisclosed related-party dealings found.
  • Disclosure and board (4 points): 4 = every quarterly report and the AGM on time for 3 years, and more independent directors than the legal minimum; 3 = on time with exactly the minimum, or independence not verified; 1 = repeated late filings, or fewer independent directors than required; 0 = penalised by SEBON or NEPSE for disclosure failures in the last 3 years.
  • P/B against the sector median (7 points), as already applied in the worked examples: 0.8x or below = 7 (only if ROE is at or above the sector median); 0.8x–1.1x = 5; 1.1x–1.5x = 3; above 1.5x, or negative book value = 1.
  • Free float (5 points): 40% or more = 5; 25%–40% = 3; 10%–25% = 1; below 10% = 0.
  • Leverage for non-financial companies (7 points): debt-to-equity below 1.0x and interest cover above 5x = 7; debt-to-equity 1.0x–2.0x with cover of 3x–5x = 5; debt-to-equity 2.0x–3.0x, or cover of 1.5x–3x = 3; debt-to-equity above 3.0x, or cover below 1.5x = 0.
  • Counting years: "in at least 4 of the last 5 years" compares each of the last five years with the year before it, so it needs six years of figures.
  • Average daily turnover: total value traded over the last six months divided by the number of NEPSE trading days, counting days with no trades.
  • Missing data: any sub-component you cannot verify scores no more than its middle band and is marked "not verified". If more than 20 of the 100 points are unverified or estimated, report the total as provisional.
  • Dates: every score records its data cut-off date and the date of the share price used.
  • Dividends held back by design: a hydropower company before its commercial operation date, or a bank or insurer barred from paying by its regulator's capital rules, scores the consistency check at 4 of 6 and the funding check at 2 of 4, marked "retained by design", instead of zero. Sensible reinvestment should not be punished as if it were stinginess.
  • More hard red flags: the total is capped at 54 if NRB has declared the company "problematic", if its capital is below the regulatory minimum, or if the auditor refused to give an opinion (a disclaimer); and the company is excluded, whatever its score, if a court or regulator has found fraud by its management or controlling shareholders. If its accounts have been materially restated, report the score as provisional.

Considered for a later version 2.0 — only after a test. Some improvements would change what the score measures, so they should wait for evidence: giving each indicator one "home" dimension so the profit record is not counted several times; judging growth by the return earned on new capital, not just the growth rate; and comparing the current weights with simpler alternatives, such as equal weights, to see whether they actually rank companies better.

Chapter recap

  • One number, seven parts. The Canon Quality Score adds seven dimensions to a total out of 100: Financial Strength (20), Governance (15), Liquidity (10), Valuation (15), Durability (15), Growth (15) and Dividends (10).
  • Quality plus price. Five dimensions describe the business (75 points) and two describe the share (25 points), so a score changes with the share price and must carry its price date.
  • Set for NEPSE. ROE bands follow the real spread of Nepali bank returns, CAR bands NRB's 11% minimum, and valuation is judged against sector medians, not imported rules.
  • Two lessons from 2021 are built in. A cheap price counts only with above-median ROE, and a lender's loan growth is judged against its system.
  • Governance can override everything. A low governance score or one severe red flag caps the total at 54.
  • Read the parts, not just the total. About 25–30 points rest on the same profit record; compare totals within a sector; and read every sub-score before the total.
  • A checklist, not a proof. The weights are judgement; version 1.2 is frozen; its pilot test will be judged in November 2027; and version 1.3 proposals are not yet in force.
