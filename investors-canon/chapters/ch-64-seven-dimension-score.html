Status of the Canon Score (November 2026) The Canon Score is a structured way to assess company quality, not a proven way to beat the market. Its seven weights and its thresholds are this book's judgement, explained but not yet tested against results. The first out-of-sample test began on 17 November 2026, when Chapter 80 froze scores for the listed commercial banks; it will be judged in November 2027. That pilot uses a reduced, earlier form of the rules (version 1.2-P), not the current version 1.4, and one year is only a first look. It is a small pilot on one sector, not a validation of the full 100-point score. The current rules have no out-of-sample record at all. Until stronger evidence exists, use the score as a disciplined checklist that makes you look at every risk — and never as a reason, on its own, to buy or sell. This chapter holds the single master definition of the score; other chapters should point here rather than restate the rules.

In one sentence: the Canon Score is a structured and partly subjective checklist that combines business quality, governance, tradability and price; it has not been shown to predict returns, so it should support your judgement, not replace it.
WHAT THE CANON SCORE CAN AND CANNOT TELL YOU The Canon Score is a structured ranking tool. It is not a measure of a company's true worth, a forecast of future returns, or the chance that an investment will succeed. Its total combines business quality, governance, ease of trading and price. These are related, and some use the same financial figures, so a high score does not guarantee that a share is safe, fairly priced or right for your portfolio.

Always read the Business Quality Score (out of 75) separately from the Share Attractiveness Score (out of 25). A strong company can be a poor buy at too high a price, and a cheap company can be cheap for a good reason — like a beautiful house on a plot that floods every monsoon. Neither the total nor any single dimension should decide a purchase without a separate look at how much you could lose, how easily you could sell, how much of your portfolio is already in similar companies, and how solid the evidence behind each number is.

Version 1.4 is a documented scoring method, not a tested investment model. Its weights, thresholds and overrides remain this book's judgement until a forward test shows that they tell you something simpler measures do not.

The report card came home in Kavita's school bag on a Friday in Falgun. Her father, Suman, a taxi driver in Biratnagar, opened it at the kitchen table. Seven subjects ran down the left side: Nepali, English, Mathematics, Science, Social Studies, Computer and Health. Each had a grade — mostly As, one B, and a C in Mathematics that made Suman sigh. At the bottom sat one number, her overall grade.

Suman needed both things on that page. The overall number told him how the year had gone. The seven grades told him which subject needed a tutor before the next exam.

A company listed on NEPSE deserves the same kind of report card. A company is not simply "good" or "bad". It can have excellent profits and poor governance. It can have a strong business and shares that hardly trade. It can pay generous dividends while quietly diluting shareholders with bonus shares. Chapter 63 explained why an investor needs a fixed, repeatable checklist instead of a gut feeling formed over tea at a broker's office. This chapter builds that checklist. It names the seven subjects, sets the points for each, and shows how to grade a company against thresholds set for NEPSE, not borrowed from Wall Street or Mumbai.

By the end of the chapter you will be able to take a company's annual report, its SEBON filings and its NEPSE trading data, and turn them into one number between 0 and 100: the Canon Quality Score, called the Canon Score for short elsewhere in this book.

Lesson 64.1 — The Seven Dimensions at a Glance

The score has seven dimensions, like Kavita's seven subjects. Each is scored on its own scale, and the seven scores are added together — never averaged — so the highest possible total is exactly 100.

What the Total Measures

Five dimensions describe the business. Two — Liquidity and Valuation — describe the share: how easily it trades and what it costs today. So the total mixes quality with price. The same company can score differently next month simply because its share price moved, the way the same house is a better or worse buy depending on the asking price. Two rules follow:

  • Every score must carry the date of the share price it used.
  • Every score is reported as three numbers, never the total alone: the Business Quality Score (Dimensions 1, 2, 5, 6 and 7, out of 75), the Share Attractiveness Score (Dimensions 3 and 4, out of 25), and the Canon Score (the total, out of 100).

Why These Weights

The weights are not equal. Financial Strength gets the most, 20 points, because a company that cannot earn safe profit eventually fails at everything else: a weak bank cannot keep paying dividends, cannot survive a bad-loan cycle, and cannot protect minority shareholders when capital runs short. Governance comes next at 15, because in a market where promoters hold large blocks of shares and disclosure rules are still young, a dishonest management can destroy a sound business faster than any bad quarter (Part IV). Liquidity gets only 10 points because it matters less to the business — but it matters to you: a fine company whose shares you cannot sell when you need cash has failed you personally.

Be clear about what these reasons are. They are this book's judgement. The weights have not been fitted to, or tested against, investment results, and no test yet shows that 20 points for Financial Strength works better than 15 or 25.

Where the Data Comes From

  • SEBON filings — material events, related-party transactions and changes in promoter holdings.
  • NEPSE data — daily prices, turnover and circuit hits, used mainly for Liquidity.
  • Annual reports and quarterly statements — the main source for Financial Strength, Growth and Dividends.
  • CDSC records — the depository's shareholding registers, for promoter holdings and pledged shares.
GROUND RULES FOR EVERY TABLE
  • A figure exactly on a boundary — an ROE of exactly 15%, say — belongs to the higher band.
  • If a company fits two rows for any other reason, it takes the lower score. When in doubt, the rubric errs toward caution.
  • Write the rubric version (now 1.4), the data cut-off date and the share-price date next to every score.
  • Counting years: "in at least 4 of the last 5 years" compares each of the last five years with the year before it, so it needs six years of figures.
  • Missing data: if you could not check something, write "not verified" next to it and score that part no higher than its middle row (some tables set their own limit). If more than 20 of the 100 points are unverified or estimated, call the whole score "provisional".
  • Point changes: "deduct 1 point" means subtract one point from the table score, never going below 0. "Drop one row" means move to the next row down; a score already in the lowest row stays there. Every table is built so that every company fits at least one row; if you find a case that fits none, score it in the lower of the two nearest rows and report the gap.
  • Swings: a "big swing" is a change of more than 50%, up or down, from one year to the next. "Moderate" means no big swing; "very volatile" or "erratic" means two or more big swings in five years.

Lesson 64.2 — Financial Strength & Profitability (20 points)

This dimension asks one plain question: is the company making real, lasting profit, with enough capital to survive a bad year? It draws on the financial-statement chapters in Part V and the ratio chapters in Part VII, and has three parts.

A. Return on Equity (8 points)

Return on equity (ROE) is profit for each Rs 100 of shareholders' money in the business. It shows how hard the company makes the owners' capital work — like asking a farmer how many muri of rice each ropani of his own land produced.

How to calculate it. For each of the last three years, take the net profit that belongs to ordinary shareholders and divide it by the average equity for that year (opening plus closing, divided by two). Then take the simple average of the three yearly figures. If the company publishes group (consolidated) accounts, use them, and take both lines for the owners of the parent company only: the profit that belongs to them, and the equity that belongs to them. Leave out the share owned by outside (non-controlling) shareholders on both lines, or the result is distorted. If the company publishes only its own accounts, use those.

The bands are set for NEPSE, not a textbook. The average ROE of Nepal's commercial banks fell from about 13.2% in the third quarter of FY 2022/23 to 7.73% in the same quarter of FY 2024/25, as heavy provisions and slow lending squeezed the sector (NRB figures, reported by The Annapurna Express in April 2025). In that quarter the strongest bank, Everest Bank, earned about 15.8%, and the weakest, NIC Asia Bank, 0.71%. That spread is exactly what the bands separate. ROE on its own, without comparing peers, tells you very little.

B. Capital Adequacy or Leverage (7 points)

For banks and financial institutions this uses the capital adequacy ratio (CAR): the lender's own capital as a share of its risk-weighted assets. Think of it as the size of the cushion under a stack of fragile pots. For commercial banks, NRB's Capital Adequacy Framework 2015 sets a minimum total capital ratio of 8.5%. At least 6% must be Tier 1 (core) capital, and at least 4.5% common equity. On top of that sits a capital conservation buffer of 2.5 percentage points, held in common equity. That makes 11% in all. NRB can add a counter-cyclical buffer on top in boom years; its 2022/23 supervision report announced 0.5% from 15 July 2024. Check whether it still applies on your scoring date.

Other classes of lender have their own minimums. NRB's Financial Stability Report for FY 2023/24 (figures as of mid-July 2024) states them:

  • Development banks: 11% capital adequacy with 8.5% core capital — but national-level development banks 10%, with 6% Tier 1. In mid-July 2024 there were eight national-level and nine province-level development banks, so check which kind yours is.
  • Finance companies: 10%, with 6% Tier 1.
  • Microfinance institutions: 8% capital fund, with at least 4% core capital.

Score against the minimum for the lender's own class and level, and write down the source and its date. NRB can change these through its Unified Directive, so if a newer directive sets a different figure, use that. If you cannot tell which minimum applies, mark this part "not verified" and score it no higher than 5.

A lender sitting right at its minimum has no real cushion. One bad-loan cycle can push it below the line. It may then have to slow lending, hold back cash dividends, or raise new capital — often through a rights issue, which dilutes any shareholder who cannot or does not take it up. So the table measures the cushion above the lender's own minimum, at the latest quarter-end.

Past breaches. A lender that fell below its minimum at any quarter-end in the last 3 years but is above it now scores no more than 3. A breach today scores 0 and also triggers the hard limit in Lesson 64.5.

For other companies, this part looks at debt instead, with two separate measures that are added together. Debt-to-equity is how many rupees the company has borrowed for every rupee of its owners' money; count all interest-bearing borrowings and lease liabilities as debt. Interest cover is operating profit divided by the interest bill — how many times over the company could pay its interest. Scoring them separately means every company gets a score, whatever its mix.

Leverage (7 points) = debt-to-equity points + interest-cover points. Hydropower and other utilities are normally built with a lot of debt; where Chapter 65 gives a sector version, use it, and otherwise use these tables.

C. Earnings Quality (5 points)

This checks whether the profit record is a steady, believable line or a jagged one full of one-off gains, sudden losses or restated numbers.

5-year profit historyPoints (of 5)
No loss year; profit grew in at least 4 of the last 5 years5
One loss year, or two flat or declining years3
No loss year, but profit flat or declining in 3 or more of the 5 years2
Two or more loss years, or erratic (two or more big swings)1

Cash check (non-financial companies). Work out the cash conversion ratio: operating cash flow over the last three years added together, divided by net profit over the same three years added together. If it is below 0.5, deduct 1 point. Profit that never turns into cash is like a shop's credit book full of customers who never pay: it looks good on paper until you need the money. Do not use this check if the three years' profit adds up to zero or less — that company already scores low.

Profit check (lenders). Cash flow is not a useful test for a bank, so look at where the profit came from instead. If, in any of the last three years, more than a quarter of net profit came from writing back earlier loan-loss provisions or from one-off gains such as selling property or investments, deduct 1 point. The asset-quality step in Dimension 6 covers bad loans themselves.

Financial Strength = A + B + C, out of 20. Take the 3- and 5-year figures from audited annual reports, and the latest CAR from the quarterly reports banks must publish on NEPSE.

Lesson 64.3 — Governance, Liquidity and Valuation

Dimension 2: Governance & Promoter Behaviour (15 points)

Part IV showed that governance is often what decides whether a minority shareholder's investment survives. A promoter is a founder or controlling shareholder who usually holds a large block of shares and controls the board. This dimension has three checks.

Promoter holdings and pledging (6 points). When promoters pledge their shares as security for a loan, a lender can force a sale that crashes the price, whatever the business is doing. Think of a farmer who has mortgaged the family land: the harvest can be good and the land still be lost.

Promoter holding pattern (CDSC records, last 3 years)Points (of 6)
Stable or rising; under 10% of promoter shares pledged6
Broadly stable; 10%–25% pledged4
Falling without a stated reason, or 25%–50% pledged2
Falling sharply without a stated reason, more than 50% pledged, or repeated insider selling0
WARNING Pledged shares never appear in the profit and loss account. They show up only in CDSC shareholding records and SEBON event filings. A company can report a healthy quarter while its promoter is one margin call away from a forced sale. Check pledging before you check profit. If you cannot find pledge data, do not assume there is none: score this check at no more than 4 out of 6 and write "pledging not verified".

Where to find pledge and holding data, in this order: (1) the company's own share register or its annual report's shareholding note; (2) SEBON and NEPSE disclosures of pledges, releases and promoter sales; (3) CDSC records. Use the most recent figure from the highest source you can find, and write down which one you used.

Not every fall is a warning. A promoter holding can fall for an ordinary reason: a merger that combined two shareholder lists, a sale the law requires, or a public issue that brings in new shareholders. Count a fall only if it has no stated reason, or if it keeps happening year after year. A merger is like two families joining in marriage — each side's share of the household changes, but nobody has run away with the money.

How big is a fall? Measure the promoter group's holding as a percentage of all the company's shares, after leaving out explained changes. Stable: changed by less than 1 percentage point over 3 years. Broadly stable: fell by 1–3 points. Falling: fell by 3–5 points. Falling sharply: fell by more than 5 points. Repeated insider selling: promoters or directors sold shares, without a stated reason, in at least two of the last three years. If promoters chose not to take up their rights in a rights issue, the resulting fall counts — it was their choice.

Related-party dealings and audit opinion (5 points). Related-party dealings are business the company does with its own insiders: loans, contracts or purchases involving directors, promoters or their families. The auditor's opinion is the auditor's formal verdict on whether the accounts are fair. Read both in the annual report, and use the auditor's own heading for each finding:

  • Emphasis of matter — the accounts are fair; the auditor only points to something worth noticing.
  • Material uncertainty related to going concern — a separate section saying there is real doubt the company can carry on. More serious than an emphasis note.
  • Qualified opinion — the accounts are fair except for a specific problem.
  • Adverse opinion — the accounts are not fair.
  • Disclaimer — the auditor could not form an opinion at all.
Audit opinion and related-party dealingsPoints (of 5)
Clean (unqualified) opinion; related-party dealings small and fully disclosed5

Disclosure and board independence (4 points). Does the company file its quarterly reports and hold its AGM on time? Does its board have independent directors — people with no ties to the promoters or managers — beyond the legal minimum? For banks and other financial institutions the rule is in the Bank and Financial Institution Act 2073 (Section 14): a board of five to seven directors, of whom at least one must be an independent (professional) director. For other public companies, Section 86 of the Companies Act 2063 allows a board of three to eleven directors and sets the minimum number of independent directors: at least one on a board of up to seven, and at least two on a larger board. (That last rule comes from two legal sources that agree — NepalLaws and a Prime Law FAQ — not from the Act's own wording, which this book could not reach online.)

The top row needs more than a head count. Each independent director must meet the law's independence tests, must have attended at least three-quarters of board meetings in the last year, and the audit committee must include at least one of them. If you cannot check these, score 3. Even then, this is a test of the board's structure, not proof that it actually challenges management.

Dimension 3: Liquidity & Tradability (10 points)

Part XI introduced two ideas used here. The first is average daily turnover: the rupee value a share trades on a normal day. The second is the circuit trap: a share that hits its daily price limit with almost no trading, leaving sellers stuck for days (Chapter 58).

Average daily turnover, last 6 monthsPoints (of 5)
Rs 5 million (Rs 50 lakh) or more5
Rs 1–5 million (Rs 10–50 lakh)3
Rs 200,000–1 million (Rs 2–10 lakh)1
Below Rs 200,000 (Rs 2 lakh)0

How to measure it. Add up the value traded over the last six months and divide by the number of NEPSE trading days, counting the days the share did not trade at all. Thin-trading rule: if the share traded on fewer than half of those days, score turnover at no more than 1, whatever the average says. A few large block trades can make a usually frozen share look busy, like a village shop that sells a whole year's rice to one wedding party.

Free float (5 points) is the share of a company's stock that is genuinely available for ordinary trading. It leaves out promoter holdings and any other shares that do not normally come to market: government and other strategic holdings, cross-holdings by other companies, locked-in shares, and restricted employee or management shares. SEBON listed these same categories in its October 2026 working paper on a free-float benchmark index (a consultation paper, not yet a rule). If you cannot identify every restricted holding, use the public holding minus any locked-in shares the company discloses, and mark the figure "estimated". More freely tradable shares means deeper trading; a very small float is prone to circuit traps however good the business. Check the annual report's promoter–public split against CDSC records; if they disagree, treat that as a small governance flag.

Free floatPoints (of 5)
40% or more5
25%–40%3
10%–25%1
Below 10%0

Read these two checks as floors — a first screen of tradability, not proof that a share is liquid. They separate shares you can trade from shares you cannot; they do not separate liquid from very liquid. In 2026 the middle half of NEPSE trading days turned over about Rs 3.8–7.5 arba. In FY 2025/26 (2082/83), 231 of the 349 securities that traded — about two-thirds — averaged at least Rs 5 million a day, the top band. That count covers all shares, mutual funds and debentures in the dataset, each averaged over all 223 market days, so a security listed part-way through the year is averaged over the full year (Canon calculation from Chapter 11's data). Most commercial banks, with a typical 51/49 promoter–public split and few other restricted holdings, clear the 40% free-float line. Whether a share is liquid enough for your order is a separate question, answered by Chapter 56's ADV rule using your actual position size. Chapter 66 makes that check compulsory before every purchase.

Dimension 4: Valuation Reasonableness (15 points)

Part IX warned against judging P/E (price ÷ earnings per share) or P/B (price ÷ book value per share) on their own. NEPSE as a whole has at times traded well above 30 times earnings, while banks have often traded nearer 15–16 times earnings and about 1.5 times book. A fixed rule such as "P/E below 15 is cheap" would misjudge almost every sector at once. So the Canon scores valuation against the company's own sector median.

A discount has to be earned. A company at 0.8 times its sector median or below gets the top band — on P/E or on P/B — only if its ROE is at or above the sector's median ROE — and, for a lender, only if its NPL ratio is also at or below its system average, because banks often trade below book when the market doubts their loans. If either test fails, or you cannot verify the sector median, score that part in the in-line band instead: 6 of 8 for P/E, 5 of 7 for P/B. Chapter 80's look back at 2021 — an exploratory observation on data this book had already seen, not proof — found that the cheapest banks in August 2021 fell further than the expensive ones. A low price is often the market's honest verdict on weaker earnings — like a cheap plot of land that floods every monsoon. Ask why it is cheap before you count it as cheap.

How to find the sector median. Use NEPSE's own sector groups, and include only comparable companies — for hydropower, operating plants only. You need at least five comparable, profitable companies; with fewer, mark the part "not verified" and score it in the middle row. For P/E, use each company's last four quarters of EPS (trailing 12 months) and leave loss-makers out of the median, since a negative P/E has no meaning. Take the simple median — the middle company when all are lined up — not the average, so one extreme company cannot drag it. Write down the price date.

Companies with no meaningful profit. An operating company that made a loss over the last 12 months scores 1 of 8 on P/E. A hydropower company that has not yet started commercial operation has no earnings to compare, so its P/E is "not applicable". Score its P/B out of 7 as usual and scale it up: valuation score = P/B points ÷ 7 × 15, rounded to the nearest whole point, and label it "one measure only". Because such a company cannot pass the ROE test, its P/B cannot reach the top row, so its valuation score is at most 11 (5 ÷ 7 × 15). Version 1.3 instead copied the P/B score into the P/E row, which gave the same evidence credit twice; version 1.4 removes that.

P/E and P/B move together. Both rise and fall with the same share price, so a share that looks cheap on one usually looks cheap on the other. Read the 15 valuation points as one view of the price, not two separate pieces of evidence.

Only the sector median is scored. The company's own 5-year median is a cross-check: if a share looks cheap against its sector but expensive against its own history, note it beside the score, but it does not change the points.

Lesson 64.4 — Durability, Growth and Dividends

Dimension 5: Sector & Business Model Durability (15 points)

This dimension takes the longest view: will the business still be relevant and protected in ten years? It draws on Part VI, which showed that a bank, a hydropower company, an insurer and a manufacturer must each be read differently.

Moat (8 points). A moat is a lasting barrier that keeps competitors from eating into profits — named after the water-filled ditch around an old fort.

Business positionPoints (of 8)
High entry barriers and evidence that they protect profits: ROE at or above the sector median in at least 4 of the last 5 years (for a lender, with its NPL ratio no higher than its system average); for a hydropower company, a plant in operation that delivered at least 90% of its contracted energy in 2 of the last 3 years, with at least 10 years left on its PPA8
Moderate barriers, or a licence and regulated position without that proof5
Low barriers; commodity-like business with intense competition2
A structural regulatory or technological threat to the business0

A licence is not a moat. A licence is a ticket to enter the field, not a promise of winning the match. Every listed commercial bank holds the same kind of NRB licence, yet in the third quarter of 2024/25 their returns ranged from 15.8% to 0.71%. So a licence alone earns 5 points; the full 8 need evidence that the barrier actually protects profits. Even that is evidence, not proof. Above-median returns can also come from extra borrowing, a good credit cycle or provisions set too low — which is why the test asks for five years and, for lenders, sound loans. A real moat shows itself as returns that stay high through a full cycle.

A PPA, or Power Purchase Agreement, is a long-term contract under which a buyer — usually the Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA) — agrees to buy a plant's electricity at a set price for a set number of years. It makes a hydropower company's revenue far more predictable than a project still under construction. It does not remove every risk: river flows, dry-season output, transmission bottlenecks and how promptly NEA pays still matter (Chapter 72).

Business and counterparty concentration (7 points). A business that depends heavily on one customer, one region, one product or one supplier carries a risk that may not show in a single year's accounts but shows up in the end — like a farmer who grows only one crop and sells it to only one trader. Find the largest single exposure and score that:

  • Non-financial companies: the largest of — one customer's share of revenue, one segment's or product's share of revenue, one region's share of revenue, and one supplier's share of purchases.
  • Lenders: credit concentration — the largest share of the loan book in one sector, segment or region, from the sector-wise lending disclosure in the annual report. This measures where repayment could fail, not where income comes from; write "credit concentration" beside the score.

Dimension 6: Growth Trajectory (15 points)

This asks whether income is really growing, or only appears to grow through accounting choices, revaluations or one-off gains.

Revenue growth (8 points). CAGR, the compound annual growth rate, is the steady yearly rate that would take a starting figure to an ending figure over several years.

5-year revenue CAGRPoints (of 8)
15% or higher, positive in at least 4 of 5 years8
8%–15%6
0%–8%3
Negative, or very volatile (two or more big swings)0

Use operating revenue only. Leave out one-off gains, revaluation gains and other non-operating income. If a merger or acquisition happened within the five years, part of the growth was bought, not earned: compare combined figures for both companies over the whole period, or, if you cannot, score no higher than 6 and write "merger".

Lenders are scored differently: relative loan-growth discipline. Banks, development banks, finance companies and microfinance institutions earn most of their income as interest on loans. Their revenue therefore moves with interest rates as well as with lending, so the size of the loan book is a better measure of how fast they are really expanding. Fast lending at the top of a credit cycle becomes the bad loans of the repair phase (Chapter 0.1). Chapter 80's look back at 2021 (exploratory, in-sample) found that the banks that grew loans fastest at the 2021 peak did worst over the next five years. So a lender's loan growth is compared with its whole system over the same period — all commercial banks for a bank, the microfinance sector for a microfinance institution — using NRB's figures. Think of a bus driver on a crowded highway: the safe speed is the speed of the traffic, not the fastest the bus can go. Note what this measures: discipline — how closely the lender keeps pace with its system — not the quality of the growth itself. That is why the adjustments below matter.

Lender's loan growth compared with its system (same period)Points (of 8)
Within 5 percentage points of the system, either way8
5–10 points faster or slower than the system5
More than 10 points faster or slower, or shrinking while the system grows2

Then apply these adjustments to lenders, in this order:

  1. Credit peak. When the checklist in Section 0.3.7 shows system credit growth above 20%, or the system CD ratio within five points of NRB's ceiling (this book's working definition of a credit peak, not a universal one), a lender growing more than 5 points faster than its system scores 0. Stop here.
  2. Stretched funding. If loans grew more than 5 points faster than deposits, drop one row.
  3. Weak asset quality. Use the gross non-performing loan (NPL) ratio — the share of loans that have stopped being repaid on time — at the same quarter-end as the system figure, and compare it with the average for lenders of the same class (commercial banks with commercial banks). Before comparing with a year earlier, add back any loans written off during the year, so that write-offs cannot make quality look better than it is. If the NPL ratio is above the class average and higher than a year earlier, drop one row. If restructured or rescheduled loans exceed 5% of total loans, drop one row even if the NPL ratio looks fine (one drop at most for this step). Steps 2 and 3 together cannot take the score below 2.
  4. No verified system figure for the same period: score this part at no more than 5.

Earnings per share (7 points). EPS is the net profit that belongs to ordinary shareholders, divided by the average number of shares during the year. It matters more to you than total profit because it counts dilution from new shares.

Start with the EPS the company reports. Nepal's accounting standards (NFRS, which follow the international standard IAS 33) require a company that issues bonus shares to restate the earlier years' EPS in its latest report, as if the bonus shares had always existed. So take each year's figure from the most recent annual report that shows it, and check the EPS note: the number of shares used should match the shares in issue after every bonus issue. If the latest report restates fewer than five years, take the older years from earlier reports and adjust them as below. Do not adjust these figures again — that would count the bonus twice, like subtracting the same expense from your monthly budget two times.

Only if you must use older, unrestated reports: divide each earlier year's EPS by (1 + the bonus ratio) for every later bonus issue. After a 10% bonus, last year's EPS of Rs 30 becomes Rs 27.27. A bonus issue cuts the same cake into more slices; without this step, a company that issues bonus shares every year looks as if its earnings are shrinking when they are not.

Rights issues are partly a bonus in disguise. A rights issue sold below the market price brings in new money, and that part of the dilution is real. But the discount is a gift of value to existing holders, and IAS 33 treats that gift like a small bonus issue. Example: the share trades at Rs 400 and the company offers one new share at Rs 100 for every share held. The theoretical price after the issue is (400 + 100) ÷ 2 = Rs 250. Divide earlier years' EPS by 400 ÷ 250 = 1.6 — unless the latest report has already done it for you. When you cannot tell, rebuild EPS from net profit and the weighted average number of shares, and write down how.

EPS on a comparable share count, last 5 yearsPoints (of 7)
Rose in at least 4 of 5 years, with no swing above 50% in a single year7
Rose in 3 of 5 years, with no more than one big swing4
Rose in 2 of 5 years, or very volatile (two or more big swings)2
Rose in fewer than 2 of 5 years, or negative in 3 or more years0

Losses are not growth. A year counts as "rose" only if EPS was positive in that year. If EPS was negative in the latest year, or in two or more of the five, the score is at most 2. A smaller loss is an improvement, not growth.

Dimension 7: Dividend & Capital Return Discipline (10 points)

A Nepali company can return money to shareholders as cash dividends, bonus shares or both. The mix matters as much as the amount, because bonus shares feel generous but are not cash, and they dilute future EPS (Chapter 18).

Consistency (6 points). A company that pays every year, at a sensible share of its profit, is behaving predictably. One that skips dividends erratically — often because it breached a capital or liquidity requirement — is signalling stress.

Two definitions keep this check honest. First, a year counts as "paid" only if shareholders received a real cash dividend. In Nepal a bonus issue usually comes with a small cash dividend that exists only to pay the 5% tax on the bonus. A 10% bonus with 0.5263% cash is a typical example: the cash is exactly 5% of the 10.5263% total, so it covers the tax and nothing more. That year counts as a bonus year, not a cash year. Second, the payout ratio counts cash only: cash dividends ÷ distributable profit. Record bonus shares separately as the bonus capitalisation ratio: the face value of the bonus issue ÷ distributable profit. A bonus moves money from one pocket of the company's equity to another and gives you more share certificates; it is not the same as cash in hand. The 30%–70% range for the cash payout is this book's preference for balance between sharing profit and keeping enough to grow, not a rule of finance.

Dividend record, last 5 yearsPoints (of 6)
Real cash dividend every year; cash payout 30%–70% of distributable profit6
Real cash dividend in 4 of 5 years, or cash payout consistently below 30% or above 70%4
Real cash dividend in 2 or 3 of 5 years, erratic2
Real cash dividend in 1 or 0 of the last 5 years0

Where the money came from, and why it was kept (4 points). Was the dividend paid from real distributable profit, or from reserves and one-off gains? And when the company kept profit back by issuing bonus shares instead of cash, did it say what it needed the capital for — NRB's paid-up capital requirement, say, or a named project?

Source and purposePoints (of 4)
Genuine distributable profit; reserves intact or growing; any bonus issue tied to a stated capital need4
Partly one-off or revaluation gains, or bonus issues with no stated use for the money kept2
Drawn from capital or reserves0

Record the return on retained capital. A stated plan is not proof the money was used well. So beside the score, write down the rise in EPS over the last five years divided by the total earnings per share the company kept back (did not pay in cash) over those years. If a company kept Rs 60 a share over five years and its EPS rose by Rs 6, it earned about 10% on what it kept. Version 1.4 records this number but does not yet score it; scoring it waits for testing.

Dividends held back by design. A hydropower company before its commercial operation date, or a bank or insurer barred from paying by its regulator's capital rules, scores 4 of 6 on consistency and 2 of 4 on source, marked "retained by design", instead of zero. A farmer who keeps this year's best seed for next season is not being stingy.

Lesson 64.5 — Combining the Seven Scores Into One Number

Canon Quality Score = Financial Strength (0–20) + Governance (0–15) + Liquidity (0–10) + Valuation (0–15) + Durability (0–15) + Growth (0–15) + Dividends (0–10).

ScoreBandLargest position this book allows (Chapter 66)
85–100Exceptional — the highest-quality band; a candidate for a core, long-term holding6%–8% of the equity part of your portfolio
70–84Strong — a solid long-term holding, with normal monitoring4%–6%

These sizes are this book's provisional upper limits, not sizes derived from evidence, and the score has not been shown to predict returns. A score of 90 does not by itself justify an 8% position: a high-quality company can still have a volatile price, event risk, a lot in common with your other holdings, or a price that leaves room for a large loss. So the band sets only the ceiling. The concentration limits in Chapter 60 and Chapter 66 still apply, as does the most you can afford to lose, and every purchase must pass Chapter 56's ADV rule for your own order size. A quality score is not a risk budget.

THE GOVERNANCE OVERRIDE One rule can override the sum. It fires in either of two cases: (1) the Governance total is below 5 out of 15; or (2) one governance red flag is severe on its own — the pledging check scores 0 out of 6 (more than half of promoter shares pledged, an unexplained sharp fall in promoter holding, or repeated unexplained insider selling), or the audit check scores 1 or less out of 5 (a qualified opinion on loans, provisions or related-party dealings, a limit on the auditor's work in a material area, a going-concern material uncertainty, an adverse opinion, a disclaimer, or undisclosed related-party dealings). When it fires, the total is capped at 54, the top of the Weak / Avoid band, however well the other six dimensions score. The second trigger closes a loophole: without it, a company with most promoter shares pledged and a qualified audit could still reach 5 out of 15 just by filing on time. A brilliant income statement cannot outvote a dishonest promoter — and neither can a punctual one.
OTHER HARD LIMITS (FROM VERSION 1.3)
  • The total is capped at 54 if NRB has declared the company "problematic", if its capital is below the regulatory minimum, or if the auditor gave a disclaimer.
  • The company is excluded, whatever its score, if a court or regulator has found fraud by its management or controlling shareholders.
  • If its accounts have been materially restated, the score is provisional until a full year of new audited figures is out.

Where the Dimensions Overlap

The dimensions are meant to look at different risks, but they are not fully separate. The profit record is read in several places: ROE (8 points) is profit divided by equity; earnings quality (5) and EPS growth (7) both read the same profit history; the dividend record (6) depends on having profit to pay out; and a valuation discount counts only when ROE beats the sector median. Altogether roughly 25–30 of the 100 points rest, directly or indirectly, on the same few years of reported profit. That emphasis is deliberate — five straight years of clean, rising per-share profit is one of the hardest things for a weak company to fake — but it has a cost: one bad or restated year can move the total by more than any single table suggests. Read these sub-scores together, not as separate pieces of evidence. Whether the overlap actually distorts rankings is a question for testing, not argument: once enough companies are scored, Chapter 80's audit will check how closely the sub-scores move together, drop one dimension at a time to see whether the order of companies changes, and compare the result with simple equal weights.

Comparing Companies

Compare totals within a sector, and compare the Business Quality Score and the Share Attractiveness Score as well as the total. Chapter 65 adjusts several sub-scores for banks, hydropower, insurers and microfinance, so that each is judged fairly; the aim is that a 75 means similar quality in any sector, but no test has yet shown that it does. Across sectors, compare bands, and with care. And always read the seven sub-scores before the total. Two companies can both score 75 for very different reasons — one solid everywhere but pricey, the other excellent on growth but only middling on governance — just as Suman read Kavita's Mathematics grade before her overall result.

Lesson 64.6 — A Worked Example: Scoring an Illustrative Bank

"Himalaya Unnati Bank Ltd" (HUBL) is an invented company built for teaching. It is not a real listed bank, and no real bank's data was used. Its figures sit in the realistic range for a well-run but unspectacular Nepali commercial bank.

HUBL's raw data:

  • 3-year average ROE 14.2%, weakest year 11.0%
  • CAR 12.8% at the latest quarter-end, under NRB's Capital Adequacy Framework 2015 (minimum 11% including the buffer); never below 11% in the last 3 years
  • Profit grew in 4 of the last 5 years, with one flat year during a provisioning cycle
  • Promoter holding stable at 51% for 5 years; under 10% pledged
  • Unqualified audit opinion with no emphasis-of-matter note; related-party loans modest and fully disclosed
  • Quarterly filings and AGM on time for 3 years; 2 of 7 board seats independent (the legal minimum for a bank under the Bank and Financial Institution Act is 1); both meet the legal tests, attended over three-quarters of meetings, and one chairs the audit committee
  • Average daily turnover Rs 3.2 million over the last 6 months, trading on every session; free float 49%
  • P/E 13.5x against a banking-sector median of about 15.8x; P/B 1.3x against about 1.5x
  • Licensed, NRB-regulated business; ROE above the banking-sector median in 4 of the last 5 years; about 30% of the loan book in one regional corporate segment (its largest single sector or segment)
  • Loan book grew 11% a year over 5 years, against about 9% for the banking system, with deposits growing in step; NPL ratio 2.9%, below the system average and lower than a year ago; EPS as restated in the latest report rose in 4 of the last 5 years, with no big swing
  • A real cash dividend every year for 5 years, with a cash payout of about 40% of distributable profit, plus bonus shares tied to NRB's capital plan

HUBL scores 87 under version 1.4, the same as under version 1.3, because it passes the stricter tests 1.4 added. Under version 1.2 it scored 85: the audit and board checks had no point bands, and the 1.2 example awarded 4 of 5 and 3 of 4 by judgement — even though HUBL has twice the legal minimum of independent directors. (Under version 1.1 it scored 83, because of the old fixed loan-growth bands.) Compare scores only within the same version. And note that 4 of 4 on the board is a formal test of structure; it does not show that HUBL's board actually challenges its managers.

The sub-scores show where the other 13 points went: ROE below 15% (2 points), a capital cushion thinner than ideal (2), lending concentrated in one segment (3), only average trading liquidity (2), and a price merely in line with its sector (4). None of these is a red flag, and no hard limit applies. They are the things to watch in next year's annual report. That is the purpose of scoring in seven parts: not a verdict, but a diagnosis.

Three Harder Cases

HUBL is a clean, healthy bank, so it does not test the awkward rules. These three invented companies do. Only the parts that matter are shown.

  • A heavily borrowed manufacturer ("Kanchan Cement", invented). Debt-to-equity 2.4x → 1 of 3; interest cover 2.2x → 1 of 4; leverage 2 of 7. Profit grew in 4 of 5 years (5 of 5 on the table), but operating cash flow over three years was only 0.45 times profit, so deduct 1 point → 4 of 5. Strong profit growth on paper, weak cash behind it: the score shows both.
  • A hydropower project not yet producing ("Tamor Khola Hydro", invented). No earnings, so P/E is not applicable. P/B is 0.7x the median of operating hydropower companies, but with no ROE it cannot pass the discount test, so P/B scores 5 of 7 → valuation 5 ÷ 7 × 15 = 10.7, rounded to 11 of 15, marked "one measure only". Dividends are retained by design: 4 of 6 plus 2 of 4. Concentration on one NEA PPA is scored under Chapter 65's hydropower table, not this one.
  • A high-earning bank with hidden strain ("Sagarmatha Growth Bank", invented). 3-year ROE 17%, never below 10% → 8 of 8. But 30% of last year's profit came from writing back old provisions → earnings quality minus 1. Its gross NPL ratio, after adding back write-offs, is 5.1% against a class average of 4.0% (illustrative) and rising → drop one row in loan growth. Its moat cannot reach 8 because its NPL is above the system. And 55% of promoter shares are pledged → pledging 0 of 6, which fires the governance override: whatever the other dimensions add up to, the Canon Score is capped at 54. A high ROE cannot outvote a promoter one margin call away from a forced sale.

Version, Testing and What Is Still Judgement

RUBRIC VERSION 1.4 · ADOPTED 22 NOV 2026 (6 MANGSIR 2083) Write the version number next to every score, so that scores are compared only with scores made under the same rules. Version 1.4 is a technical patch to version 1.3, adopted the same day after a second audit. It keeps the seven weights and the score bands (85–100 Exceptional and so on) and fixes the scoring mechanics:
  • A project with no earnings no longer gets its P/B credit twice: P/B is scaled to 15 points instead.
  • Debt for non-financial companies is scored as debt-to-equity (3 points) plus interest cover (4), so every company fits a row.
  • "Revenue concentration" becomes business and counterparty concentration, with a zero row; for lenders it is credit concentration.
  • Free float now leaves out every kind of restricted holding, not just promoter shares.
  • Exact rules for point deductions, the cash conversion ratio, a profit check for lenders, consolidated ROE, past CAR breaches, promoter falls, board effectiveness, gross NPL with write-offs added back, restructured loans, loss years in EPS, and cash-only payout.
  • Every score is reported as Business Quality, Share Attractiveness and the total; position limits are labelled provisional.
Version 1.3, adopted earlier on 22 November 2026, had replaced judgement with point bands for the audit, board, P/B, free-float and leverage checks; judged CAR against each class's minimum; corrected the lender and EPS definitions; counted only real cash dividends as "paid"; and added hard limits for "problematic" status, capital below the minimum, an audit disclaimer and fraud. The only score published under 1.3 was HUBL's.
Earlier records are kept as they were. Version 1.2 (frozen 17 November 2026) and Chapter 80's forward record, version 1.2-P (pilot) — four dimensions (60 points), later five (70 points), with two years of results and four years of dividends — are not re-scored. The case studies of Nabil (Chapter 83, 75), Chhimek (Chapter 85, 77) and Karnali Development Bank (Chapter 89) remain version 1.2 scores, labelled as such, until they are re-checked. Version 1.2 had made two changes to version 1.1: lender loan growth against the whole system, and a valuation discount that counts only with above-median ROE. Version 1.1 had closed table gaps, defined EPS growth on a comparable share count, added the severe-red-flag trigger and aligned the bands with Chapter 66.

Testing status. Version 1.4 has no out-of-sample record at all. The forward pilot that began on 17 November 2026 tests version 1.2-P, and its November 2027 review will be a first-year look — far too short to show whether the score predicts long-term returns. Versions 1.2 to 1.4 were all written after seeing the 2021 bank data, so that data cannot prove they work. In a falling market, protection comes from Chapter 59's cycle settings, not from the score. A fair test needs scores frozen before results are known, every company in the sector (including those that later merged, failed or delisted), and returns that include dividends. It should then ask:
  • Ranking: do higher-scored companies go on to do better (rank correlation), within each sector as well as overall?
  • Returns and risk: total return including dividends, return per unit of risk, and the deepest fall from peak (maximum drawdown).
  • Simpler rivals: does the weighted score beat equal weights, and does it add anything beyond simple measures such as ROE, P/B and leverage alone? If not, the extra complexity is not earning its keep.
  • Dimension removal: does dropping any one dimension make the ranking better or worse?
  • Stability: does a small correction to one figure move the score a lot?

The full rules are published as data files — Canon Score specification, version 1.4 (JSON), the superseded version 1.3 (JSON) and the frozen version 1.2 (JSON) — with the claim-by-claim checks behind this chapter in the Chapter 64 evidence register (CSV).
STILL JUDGEMENT IN VERSION 1.4 A few calls still need a written reason, marked "judgement", because no simple rule fits every company: how to group customers, products and regions in the concentration check; what counts as a "structural threat" in the moat check; and when a gain is "one-off" in the dividend-source check.

Considered for a later version 2.0 — only after a test. Some improvements would change what the score measures, so they should wait for evidence: giving each indicator one "home" dimension so the profit record is not counted several times; judging growth by the return earned on new capital, not just the growth rate; comparing the current weights with simpler alternatives, such as equal weights, to see whether they actually rank companies better; scoring the return on retained capital rather than only recording it; scoring customer, regional, product and supplier concentration separately; judging a lender's valuation with asset quality and its cost of capital, not ROE alone; recording how long and how deep a past capital breach was; and judging board effectiveness by what the board actually does, not its structure.

Chapter recap

  • One number, seven parts. The Canon Quality Score adds seven dimensions to a total out of 100: Financial Strength (20), Governance (15), Liquidity (10), Valuation (15), Durability (15), Growth (15) and Dividends (10).
  • Three numbers, not one. Report the Business Quality Score (75), the Share Attractiveness Score (25) and the total. The total changes with the share price and must carry its price date.
  • Set for NEPSE. ROE bands follow the real spread of Nepali bank returns, CAR is judged against each class's NRB minimum, and valuation against sector medians, not imported rules.
  • Two lessons from 2021 are built in. A cheap price counts only with above-median ROE, and a lender's loan growth is judged against its system.
  • Some red flags override everything. A low governance score, one severe governance flag, "problematic" status, capital below the minimum or an audit disclaimer caps the total at 54; proven fraud excludes the company.
  • Read the parts, not just the total. About 25–30 points rest on the same profit record; compare totals within a sector; and read every sub-score before the total.
  • A checklist, not a proof. The weights are judgement. Version 1.4 (22 November 2026) fixes the scoring steps but has no test record; version 1.2 and its pilot record stay frozen, and the pilot's first-year review comes in November 2027. Position limits tied to the bands are provisional ceilings, not evidence-based sizes.
