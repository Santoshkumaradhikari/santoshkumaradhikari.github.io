Its seven weights and its thresholds are this book's judgement, explained but not yet tested against results. The first out-of-sample test began on 17 November 2026, when Chapter 80 froze scores for the listed commercial banks and published them together with the rules for judging them; it will be judged in November 2027. That pilot uses a reduced, earlier form of the rules (version 1.2-P), not the current version 1.5.0, and one year is only a first look. It is a small pilot on one sector, not a validation of the full 100-point score. The current rules have no out-of-sample record at all. In one sentence: the Canon Score is a structured and partly subjective checklist that combines business quality, governance, tradability and price; it has not been shown to predict returns, so it should support your judgement, not replace it.
WHAT THE CANON SCORE CAN AND CANNOT TELL YOU The Canon Score is a structured ranking tool. It is not a measure of a company's true worth, a forecast of future returns, or the chance that an investment will succeed. Its total combines business quality, governance, ease of trading and price. These are related, and some use the same financial figures, so a high score does not guarantee that a share is safe, fairly priced or right for your portfolio.

Always read the Business Quality Score (out of 75) separately from the Share Attractiveness Score (out of 25). A strong company can be a poor buy at too high a price, and a cheap company can be cheap for a good reason — like a beautiful house on a plot that floods every monsoon. Neither the total nor any single dimension should decide a purchase without a separate look at how much you could lose, how easily you could sell, how much of your portfolio is already in similar companies, and how solid the evidence behind each number is.

Version 1.5.0 is a documented scoring method, not a tested investment model. Its weights, thresholds and overrides remain this book's judgement until a forward test shows that they tell you something simpler measures do not. Even a good result from the pilot in November 2027 will not change that: the pilot tests the earlier version 1.2-P for one year. Version 1.5.0 needs its own forward record, kept over several years and through at least one downturn.

Two standards of proof. This book judges the Canon in two separate ways. As a textbook, it must be correct, sourced and checkable — the standard this chapter aims to meet now. As a model, it must beat simpler methods in frozen, pre-registered forward tests — a standard it has not yet met. Success at one is never quoted as proof of the other. The rules for the model test were registered on 23 November 2026 in Chapter 80, Lesson 80.10.

The report card came home in Kavita's school bag on a Friday in Falgun. Her father, Suman, a taxi driver in Biratnagar, opened it at the kitchen table. Seven subjects ran down the left side: Nepali, English, Mathematics, Science, Social Studies, Computer and Health. Each had a grade — mostly As, one B, and a C in Mathematics that made Suman sigh. At the bottom sat one number, her overall grade.

Suman needed both things on that page. The overall number told him how the year had gone. The seven grades told him which subject needed a tutor before the next exam.

A company listed on NEPSE deserves the same kind of report card. A company is not simply "good" or "bad". It can have excellent profits and poor governance. It can have a strong business and shares that hardly trade. It can pay generous dividends while quietly diluting shareholders with bonus shares. Chapter 63 explained why an investor needs a fixed, repeatable checklist instead of a gut feeling formed over tea at a broker's office. This chapter builds that checklist. It names the seven subjects, sets the points for each, and shows how to grade a company against thresholds set for NEPSE, not borrowed from Wall Street or Mumbai.

By the end of the chapter you will be able to take a company's annual report, its SEBON filings and its NEPSE trading data, and turn them into one number between 0 and 100: the Canon Quality Score, called the Canon Score for short elsewhere in this book.

What the Total Measures

Five dimensions describe the business. Two — Liquidity and Valuation — describe the share: how easily it trades and what it costs today. So the total mixes quality with price. The same company can score differently next month simply because its share price moved, the way the same house is a better or worse buy depending on the asking price. Two rules follow:

  • Every score must carry the date of the share price it used.
  • Every score is reported as three numbers, never the total alone: the Business Quality Score (Dimensions 1, 2, 5, 6 and 7, out of 75), the Share Attractiveness Score (Dimensions 3 and 4, out of 25), and the Canon Score (the total, out of 100).

Why These Weights

The weights are not equal. Financial Strength gets the most, 20 points, because a company that cannot earn safe profit eventually fails at everything else: a weak bank cannot keep paying dividends, cannot survive a bad-loan cycle, and cannot protect minority shareholders when capital runs short. Governance comes next at 15, because in a market where promoters hold large blocks of shares and disclosure rules are still young, a dishonest management can destroy a sound business faster than any bad quarter (Part IV). Liquidity gets only 10 points because it matters less to the business — but it matters to you: a fine company whose shares you cannot sell when you need cash has failed you personally.

Be clear about what these reasons are. They are this book's judgement. The weights have not been fitted to, or tested against, investment results, and no test yet shows that 20 points for Financial Strength works better than 15 or 25.

Where the Data Comes From

  • SEBON filings — material events, related-party transactions and changes in promoter holdings.
  • NEPSE data — daily prices, turnover and the days a share traded, used for Liquidity. (Circuit hits are not scored; see Lesson 64.3.)
  • Annual reports and quarterly statements — the main source for Financial Strength, Growth and Dividends.
  • CDSC records — the depository's shareholding registers, for promoter holdings and pledged shares.
  • NRB publications — capital minimums, and the system figures for loans, deposits and bad loans that lenders are compared with.
WHICH SOURCE WINS, AND WHAT TO WRITE DOWN When sources disagree, trust them in this order: (1) the law, regulations and the regulator's own directives; (2) audited annual reports; (3) returns and disclosures filed with NRB, NIA, SEBON or NEPSE; (4) the company's official announcements; (5) CDSC and other official ownership records; (6) reputable market-data sites; (7) news reports and commentary. A lower source can help you find a fact; it never overrules a higher one — like asking a neighbour the way to the land office, but checking the ownership papers yourself. With every score, write down: the rubric version; the company and its NEPSE symbol; the data cut-off and share-price dates; whether group or company-only accounts were used; the sector and peer group; the documents and page numbers; every estimate, gap and judgement; and any regulatory rule you checked separately.
GROUND RULES FOR EVERY TABLE
  • Boundaries: every table is written so that each figure fits exactly one row. "X to under Y" includes X but not Y; "over X up to Y" includes Y but not X; "or more", "or higher", "or less" and "at least" include the figure named; "under", "below", "over" and "above" do not. So an ROE of exactly 15% is in "15% or higher", and a concentration of exactly 20% is in "20% or less".
  • If a company fits two rows for some other reason (for example a good figure but a red flag in the same row text), it takes the lower score. When in doubt, the rubric errs toward caution. This lower-score rule is for real conflicts; it never takes a company that meets every condition of a higher row down to a lower one. The tables are written so that their rows do not overlap, and a reconciliation script checks them against the specification file.
  • Write the rubric version (now 1.5.0), the data cut-off date and the share-price date next to every score. Use only information published by the cut-off date. If a figure is later restated or corrected, keep the original score as it was and publish a revised score beside it, labelled with its own date; never overwrite the original.
  • Counting years: a five-year growth record means five year-on-year comparisons, which need six consecutive annual figures; loss years and "the last five years" are counted over the five most recent figures. This applies to profit, revenue, loans, deposits and EPS. "Grew" means higher than the year before; anything else counts as flat or declining. A five-year dividend record means the dividends declared for five financial years. A three-year average ROE needs three annual ROEs, and so four year-end balance sheets (each year's opening equity is the previous year's closing equity). Never confuse five figures with five comparisons.
  • Missing data — four different cases: Not applicable means the measure has no meaning for this company (a hydropower project with no earnings has no P/E); follow the rule the table gives for that case, and it does not count as missing. Unavailable means the figure is not published; unverified means it is published but you could not check it (some tables say "not verified"; it means the same). Score either one no higher than the middle row — where a table has an even number of rows, the lower of the two middle rows — unless the table sets its own limit, and write the word beside the score. Estimated means you worked the figure out from partial data; score it normally but write "estimated", together with the method, the source figures, the main assumption and a plausible range. If the parts affected by unavailable, unverified or estimated figures are together worth more than 20 of the 100 points — counting each part's full points, not the points it was given, and counting each part once however many of its figures are missing — call the whole score "provisional". The parts are the sixteen scored parts of the seven dimensions (ROE, capital, profit record, promoter, audit, board, turnover, free float, P/E, P/B, moat, concentration, growth, EPS, dividend record, dividend source). Show which parts were affected, their points, what was missing and what would resolve it, so a reader can redo the count. Otherwise a low score could hide a large gap in the evidence. A measure cannot be called "not applicable" just because the company did badly or did not disclose it.
  • Which accounts: if the company publishes group (consolidated) accounts, use them for every profit, revenue, EPS, cash-flow and concentration figure, with profit and equity belonging to the owners of the parent company. For a lender's regulatory figures — capital adequacy, bad loans, loan and deposit growth, credit concentration — use the bank's own figures as reported to NRB, because that is what the rules are set on.
  • Point changes: "deduct 1 point" means subtract one point from the table score, never going below 0. "Drop one row" means move to the next row down; a score already in the lowest row stays there. Every table is built so that every company fits at least one row; if you find a case that fits none, score it in the lower of the two nearest rows and report the gap.
  • Swings: a "big swing" is a change of more than 50%, up or down, from one year to the next. A change into or out of a loss always counts as a big swing, because a percentage change from a loss means nothing. "Moderate" means no big swing; "very volatile" or "erratic" means two or more big swings in five years.

Lesson 64.2 — Financial Strength & Profitability (20 points)

This dimension asks one plain question: is the company making real, lasting profit, with enough capital to survive a bad year? It draws on the financial-statement chapters in Part V and the ratio chapters in Part VII, and has three parts.

A. Return on Equity (8 points)

Return on equity (ROE) is profit for each Rs 100 of shareholders' money in the business. It shows how hard the company makes the owners' capital work — like asking a farmer how many muri of rice each ropani of his own land produced.

How to calculate it. For each of the last three years, take the net profit that belongs to ordinary shareholders and divide it by the average equity for that year (opening plus closing, divided by two). Then take the simple average of the three yearly figures, and write all three yearly figures beside the score — an average can hide a bad year. If the company publishes group (consolidated) accounts, use them, and take both lines for the owners of the parent company only: the profit that belongs to them, and the equity that belongs to them. Leave out the share owned by outside (non-controlling) shareholders on both lines, or the result is distorted. If the company publishes only its own accounts, use those. If equity was zero or negative at any year-end, ROE has no meaning: score 1.

The bands are set for NEPSE, not a textbook. The average ROE of Nepal's commercial banks fell from about 13.2% in the third quarter of FY 2022/23 to 7.73% in the same quarter of FY 2024/25, as heavy provisions and slow lending squeezed the sector (NRB figures, reported by The Annapurna Express in April 2025). In that quarter the strongest bank, Everest Bank, earned about 15.8%, and the weakest, NIC Asia Bank, 0.71%. That spread is exactly what the bands separate. ROE on its own, without comparing peers, tells you very little.

One rule overrides the average: a loss year always scores 1. Years of −5%, 20% and 35% average 16.7%, but one excellent year cannot buy back a loss — just as one bumper harvest does not mean the field never floods.

The opposite pattern needs watching too. Years of 20%, 18% and 2% average 13.3% and score 6, even though the latest year has collapsed. This version does not penalise that pattern by itself — the profit-record check in Part C and the EPS check in Dimension 6 catch part of it — so always write the three yearly figures and the latest one next to the score, where a falling trend cannot hide. ROE can also rise for the wrong reason: if equity shrinks through losses, large payouts or write-downs, the same profit gives a higher ratio. When equity has fallen sharply, write that down as well.

B. Capital Adequacy or Leverage (7 points)

Which test applies. Lenders licensed by NRB (classes A, B, C and D) use the capital cushion test below. Insurers use the solvency test that follows it, because their safety is set by the Nepal Insurance Authority, not NRB, and their policy reserves are not ordinary debt. All other companies use the debt test. Never force a company into the wrong test because it is the easier one to fill in.

For banks and financial institutions this uses the capital adequacy ratio (CAR): the lender's own capital as a share of its risk-weighted assets. Think of it as the size of the cushion under a stack of fragile pots. For commercial banks, NRB's Capital Adequacy Framework 2015 sets a minimum total capital ratio of 8.5%. Within that, at least 6% must be Tier 1 (core) capital, and at least 4.5% common equity — the shareholders' own money. On top sits a capital conservation buffer of 2.5 percentage points, held in common equity. So the full requirement is 11% total capital, 8.5% Tier 1 and 7% common equity. The minimum means every part of it: a bank that meets the total but misses the Tier 1 or common-equity figure counts as below its minimum.

NRB can also add a counter-cyclical buffer of up to 2.5% in boom years. It required commercial banks to hold an extra 0.5% by mid-July 2024, then set the buffer at 0% for FY 2081/82 (2024/25), as reported in August 2024. Directive No. 1 of the Unified Directive 2082 sets it at zero percent for FY 2082/83 as well. Check NRB's later policy and circulars for the rate in force on your scoring date; never assume an old buffer still applies.

Other classes of lender have their own minimums. NRB's Unified Directive 2082, Directive No. 1, decides which rulebook each class follows: commercial banks and national-level development banks follow the Capital Adequacy Framework 2015; other development banks and finance companies follow the older Capital Adequacy Framework 2007 (updated July 2008). Microfinance institutions have their own requirement. The minimums in each rulebook are:

For every class, the minimum means every part of it: a lender below its core-capital figure counts as below its minimum, even if its total looks fine. In mid-July 2024 there were eight national-level and nine province-level development banks, so check which kind yours is. Score against the minimum for the lender's own class and level, and write down the source and its date. If you cannot tell which minimum applies, mark this part "unverified" and score it no higher than 5. A microfinance institution's 8% capital fund is measured under a simpler framework than a bank's ratio, so the two percentages are not directly comparable.

A lender sitting right at its minimum has no real cushion. One bad-loan cycle can push it below the line. It may then have to slow lending, hold back cash dividends, or raise new capital — often through a rights issue, which dilutes any shareholder who cannot or does not take it up. So the table measures the cushion above the lender's own total-capital minimum, at the latest quarter-end.

Past breaches. A lender that fell below its minimum at any quarter-end in the last 3 years but is above it now scores no more than 3. Record how many quarter-ends were below the minimum and the lowest ratio reached. A breach today scores 0 and also triggers the hard limit in Lesson 64.5.

What CAR does not tell you. These 7 points measure a regulatory cushion, not total safety. A bank at 13% is not necessarily safer than one at 12.5%. What matters as well is what the capital is made of (common equity absorbs losses best), how the bank's risk weights were set, how good its loans really are, and whether provisions are honest. A bank's CAR is like the declared weight limit on a bridge: useful, but you still want to know what the bridge is made of. The bad-loan checks in Dimension 6 and the profit check below cover part of this; stress tests and capital quality are not scored in this version.

Leverage (7 points) = debt-to-equity points + interest-cover points. Three cases need care. A company with no interest-bearing debt scores 4 on cover. A company that has debt but shows little or no interest in its income statement — usually because interest on a project under construction is added to the project's cost ("capitalised") — is not debt-free: work out cover using all interest incurred, including the capitalised part. And a company with an operating loss scores 0 on cover, whatever its debt. Zero or negative equity scores 0 on debt-to-equity and is a warning in its own right: the owners' cushion is gone.

These two numbers do not show when the debt must be repaid. A company whose loans all fall due next year is riskier than one with the same debt spread over ten years. Write down beside the score how much debt falls due in the next 12 months, any broken loan conditions (covenants), refinancing needs and the assets pledged; none of this is scored in this version. For a hydropower company financed project by project, also write down the debt-service coverage ratio (DSCR) — cash available for debt service divided by the principal and interest due — because interest cover alone misses the loan repayments that matter most for a project. Hydropower and other utilities are normally built with a lot of debt. Chapter 65 does not yet set a sector version of these tables, so use them as they are and compare hydropower companies only with each other.

Cash check (non-financial companies). Work out the cash conversion ratio: operating cash flow over the last three years added together, divided by net profit over the same three years added together. If it is below 0.5, deduct 1 point. Profit that never turns into cash is like a shop's credit book full of customers who never pay: it looks good on paper until you need the money. Do not use this check if the three years' profit adds up to zero or less — that company already scores low.

Profit check (lenders). Cash flow is not a useful test for a bank, so look at where the profit came from instead. If, in any of the last three years, more than a quarter of net profit came from writing back earlier loan-loss provisions or from one-off gains such as selling property or investments, deduct 1 point. Compare like with like: the write-back or gain as shown in the income statement (before tax) against profit before tax. Where a year's net write-back is negative (a charge), the share is zero. The asset-quality step in Dimension 6 covers bad loans themselves.

Profit check (insurers, interim). An insurer earns from two places: underwriting (premiums minus claims and costs) and investing the money it holds. If, in any of the last three years, more than a quarter of net profit came from realised gains on selling investments or from releasing reserves set aside in earlier years, deduct 1 point. Steady underwriting and recurring investment income are worth more than one-off sales. Neither the cash check nor the lender check applies to insurers.

Financial Strength = A + B + C, out of 20. Take the yearly figures from audited annual reports, and the latest CAR from the quarterly reports banks must publish on NEPSE.

Lesson 64.3 — Governance, Liquidity and Valuation

Dimension 2: Governance & Promoter Behaviour (15 points)

Part IV showed that governance is often what decides whether a minority shareholder's investment survives. A promoter is a founder or controlling shareholder who usually holds a large block of shares and controls the board. This dimension has three checks.

Promoter holdings and pledging (6 points). Score the two parts below separately; the check scores the lower of the two. A falling holding and heavy pledging are separate dangers, and either one alone is enough to worry about. When promoters pledge their shares as security for a loan, a lender can force a sale that crashes the price, whatever the business is doing. Think of a farmer who has mortgaged the family land: the harvest can be good and the land still be lost.

WARNING Pledged shares never appear in the profit and loss account. They show up in CDSC records and in SEBON and NEPSE disclosures. A company can report a healthy quarter while its promoter is one margin call away from a forced sale. Check pledging before you check profit. If you cannot find pledge data, do not assume there is none: score this check at no more than 4 out of 6 and write "pledging not verified".

Where to find pledge and holding data, in this order: (1) the company's own share register or its annual report's shareholding note; (2) SEBON and NEPSE disclosures of pledges, releases and promoter sales; (3) CDSC records. Use the most recent figure from the highest source you can find, and write down which one you used. Measure table B exactly as: pledged promoter shares ÷ all promoter shares × 100 — not as a share of all the company's shares, which would make the same pledge look smaller. Where it is disclosed, also write down the lender, the date of the pledge and any release or forced sale; the percentage alone does not show how close a forced sale is.

Not every fall is a warning. A promoter holding can fall for an ordinary reason: a merger that combined two shareholder lists, a sale the law requires, or a public issue that brings in new shareholders. Count a fall only if it has no stated reason, or if it keeps happening year after year. A merger is like two families joining in marriage — each side's share of the household changes, but nobody has run away with the money.

How big is a fall? Measure the promoter group's holding as a percentage of all the company's shares, after leaving out explained changes — so a 2-point fall caused by a merger counts as no fall at all. The bands are in table A above. Repeated insider selling: promoters or directors sold shares, without a stated reason, in at least two of the last three years. If promoters chose not to take up their rights in a rights issue, the resulting fall counts — it was their choice.

Related-party dealings and audit opinion (5 points). Related-party dealings are business the company does with its own insiders: loans, contracts or purchases involving directors, promoters or their families. How to find the sector median. Use NEPSE's own sector groups, and include only comparable companies — for hydropower, operating plants only; for banks, banks with a similar licence and business; for insurers, life and non-life separately. Do not build one median from businesses that only share a NEPSE label. Use the same price date for every company, and book values and earnings from the same latest published quarter, on the same accounting basis. Then build the two medians separately, because they need different peer groups:

  • P/E median: leave out companies with negative trailing 12-month EPS, since a negative P/E has no meaning. Use each company's last four quarters of EPS (trailing 12 months).
  • P/B median: include loss-making companies as long as their book value is positive — a company losing money still has a meaningful price-to-book. Leave out only companies with zero or negative book value.

Each median needs at least five companies of its own. With fewer, mark that part "unverified" and score it in the in-line row. Take the simple median — the middle company when all are lined up — not the average, so one extreme company cannot drag it. Leaving loss-makers out of the P/B median would be like judging house prices in a town only by the houses that rent out: the median would drift upward. Example: five banks at P/B 1.0x, 1.2x, 1.4x, 1.6x and 0.6x (the last one loss-making) have a median of 1.2x; dropping the loss-maker would make it 1.3x.

Companies with no meaningful profit. A company with negative trailing 12-month EPS — a loss for its ordinary shareholders — scores 0 of 8 on P/E: a negative P/E is not a sign of cheapness, so never rank companies by how negative it is. Whether the business itself makes an operating profit is a separate question, answered by interest cover and earnings quality. A hydropower company that has not yet started commercial operation has no earnings to compare, so its P/E is "not applicable" (not "missing"). Score its P/B out of 7 as usual and scale it up: valuation score = P/B points ÷ 7 × 15, rounded to the nearest whole point, and label it "one measure only". Because such a company cannot pass the ROE test, its P/B cannot reach the top row, so its valuation score is at most 11 (5 ÷ 7 × 15 = 10.7). Version 1.3 instead copied the P/B score into the P/E row, which gave the same evidence credit twice; version 1.4 removed that.

P/E and P/B move together. Both rise and fall with the same share price, so a share that looks cheap on one usually looks cheap on the other. Read the 15 valuation points as one view of the price, not two separate pieces of evidence.

Only the sector median is scored. Two cross-checks go beside the score without changing the points. First, the company's own 5-year median: if a share looks cheap against its sector but expensive against its own history, note it. Second, the plain numbers: write the actual P/E and P/B next to the relative ones. A share at 0.8 times a sector that itself trades at 40 times earnings is cheap only by comparison — on 28 October 2025 the whole market traded at about 38 times. Being the cheapest house on an expensive street does not make it a cheap house.

A third cross-check for banks: what P/B does the ROE justify? A sector median tells you what the market is paying, not what a bank is worth. A simple stable-growth formula links the two: justified P/B = (ROE − g) ÷ (cost of equity − g), where g is long-run growth. Take two banks both priced at 1.0 times book, with a 12% cost of equity and 5% growth. Bank A earns a 16% ROE: (16 − 5) ÷ (12 − 5) ≈ 1.6 times book is justified, so 1.0 looks cheap. Bank B earns 8%: (8 − 5) ÷ 7 ≈ 0.4, so 1.0 looks dear. Same price, opposite verdicts. The formula only holds if the ROE and growth are sustainable, so write the result beside the score; it is not scored. Case Study 64A, Lesson 64A.11 works a fuller residual-income version of this check on two real banks. Full intrinsic valuation — discounted cash flow, dividend models and book-value methods — is taught in Chapter 46, Chapter 48 and Chapter 49; this dimension only screens relative price.

A PPA, or Power Purchase Agreement, is a long-term contract under which a buyer — usually the Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA) — agrees to buy a plant's electricity at a set price for a set number of years. Earnings per share (7 points). Use basic EPS, not diluted EPS, throughout. EPS is the net profit that belongs to ordinary shareholders, divided by the weighted average number of ordinary shares during the year — shares issued part-way through the year count only for the months they existed. It matters more to you than total profit because it counts dilution from new shares.

Start with the EPS the company reports. The current NFRS standard is NAS 33 Earnings per Share, which mirrors the international standard IAS 33. (The earlier 2008 Nepal Accounting Standard NAS 26 also covered earnings per share; in the current NFRS list, NAS 26 is a different standard.) NAS 33 requires a company that issues bonus shares, or rights shares below the market price, to restate the previous year's EPS in its new report, as if the extra shares had always existed. Each annual report therefore shows two years, both on the latest share count at the time. So for each year, take the EPS from the latest report that shows it, then adjust it only for bonus or rights issues made after that report. Never adjust for an issue the report has already reflected — that counts it twice, like subtracting the same expense from your monthly budget two times. Check the EPS note each time: the share count used should match the shares in issue.

Adjusting for a later bonus issue: divide the earlier EPS by (1 + the bonus ratio) for every later bonus issue. After a 10% bonus, last year's EPS of Rs 30 becomes Rs 27.27. A bonus issue cuts the same cake into more slices; without this step, a company that issues bonus shares every year looks as if its earnings are shrinking when they are not.

Rights issues are partly a bonus in disguise. A rights issue sold below the market price brings in new money, and that part of the dilution is real. But the discount is a gift of value to existing holders, and NAS 33 treats that gift like a small bonus issue. The theoretical ex-rights price (TERP) is (old shares × market price + new shares × issue price) ÷ (old shares + new shares), where the market price is the last price before the shares trade without the right. Earlier EPS is divided by market price ÷ TERP. Example: the share trades at Rs 400 and the company offers one new share at Rs 100 for every share held. TERP = (400 + 100) ÷ 2 = Rs 250, so divide earlier years' EPS by 400 ÷ 250 = 1.6. When you cannot tell what a report has already done, rebuild EPS from net profit and the weighted average number of shares, and write down how.

Expect a dip in the year of a rights issue: the new shares count from the day they are issued, but the new money takes time to earn its full return. A one-year fall in EPS after a rights issue is not, on its own, a sign that the business is failing.

Dimension 7: Dividend & Capital Return Discipline (10 points)

A Nepali company can return money to shareholders as cash dividends, bonus shares or both. The mix matters as much as the amount, because bonus shares feel generous but are not cash, and they dilute future EPS (Chapter 18).

Consistency (6 points). A company that pays every year, at a sensible share of its profit, is behaving predictably. One that skips dividends erratically — often because it breached a capital or liquidity requirement — is signalling stress.

Two definitions keep this check honest. First, a year counts as "paid" only if shareholders received a real cash dividend. In Nepal a bonus issue usually comes with a small cash dividend that exists only to pay the 5% tax on the bonus. A 10% bonus with 0.5263% cash is a typical example: the cash is exactly 5% of the 10.5263% total, so it covers the tax and nothing more. That year counts as a bonus year, not a cash year.

Second, the payout ratio counts cash only: cash dividends ÷ distributable profit. Record bonus shares separately as the bonus capitalisation ratio: the face value of the bonus issue ÷ distributable profit. A bonus moves money from one pocket of the company's equity to another and gives you more share certificates; it is not the same as cash in hand. The 30%–70% range for the cash payout is this book's preference for balance between sharing profit and keeping enough to grow, not a rule of finance. A payout above that range is not automatically generous: a company paying out almost everything may be starving its own future.

When there is no distributable profit. If a year's distributable profit is zero or negative, the payout ratio has no meaning: mark it "not applicable" for that year. Any cash paid that year came from earlier reserves, so the year counts as outside the 30%–70% range, and the source-and-reason check scores 0. A year with no profit and no dividend counts simply as a year without a real cash dividend.

Distributable profit is what is left for owners after the reserves the law requires. Take it from the profit-appropriation statement or the statement of changes in equity, not from headline net profit. For banks and other financial institutions, use the figure in the statement NRB requires them to publish. Before anything can be paid, at least 20% of each year's net profit must go to the general reserve until that reserve equals twice the paid-up capital, and at least 10% a year after that (Bank and Financial Institution Act 2073, Section 44). Further amounts go to NRB's regulatory reserve and the corporate social responsibility fund. So distributable profit can be far below net profit, and using the wrong line can halve the payout ratio.

Record an exploratory proxy: EPS growth relative to retained earnings. First, a combined score is a ranking, not a sizing decision: until Chapter 66 sets tested gates, a total in the top two bands takes the ceiling of the band below its own if either part-score is in the bottom half of its scale — Business Quality under 38 of 75, or Share Attractiveness under 13 of 25 (interim design judgement). Second, a score marked provisional gets the ceiling of the band below its own until the missing figures are checked. If both of these rules apply, the ceiling still drops by one band only. Third, a hydropower or insurer score is never compared with another sector's to choose between them.

THE GOVERNANCE OVERRIDE One rule can override the sum. It fires in either of two cases: (1) the Governance total is below 5 out of 15; or (2) one governance red flag is severe on its own — the pledging check scores 0 out of 6 (more than half of promoter shares pledged, an unexplained sharp fall in promoter holding, or repeated unexplained insider selling), or the audit check scores 1 or less out of 5 (a qualified opinion on loans, provisions or related-party dealings, a limit on the auditor's work in a material area, a going-concern material uncertainty, an adverse opinion, a disclaimer, or undisclosed related-party dealings). When it fires, the total is capped at 54, the top of the Weak / Avoid band, however well the other six dimensions score. The second trigger closes a loophole: without it, a company with most promoter shares pledged and a qualified audit could still reach 5 out of 15 just by filing on time. A brilliant income statement cannot outvote a dishonest promoter — and neither can a punctual one.

How long does the cap last? Only while the flag is current. Each re-score uses the latest data: the audit check reads the latest audited annual report, pledging reads the latest disclosed figure, and an unexplained promoter fall counts for the three years the table looks back. When the next report clears the flag, the cap lifts. A cap is not a life sentence — but a company that keeps triggering it is telling you something.
OTHER HARD LIMITS (FROM VERSION 1.3)

    When several limits apply at once. Caps do not add up: however many limits fire, the total is capped at 54 once. A cap never raises a total that is already lower. Where the Dimensions Overlap

The dimensions are meant to look at different risks, but they are not fully separate. The profit record is read in several places: ROE (8 points) is profit divided by equity; earnings quality (5) and EPS growth (7) both read the same profit history; the dividend record (6) depends on having profit to pay out; and a valuation discount counts only when ROE beats the sector median. Altogether roughly 25–30 of the 100 points rest, directly or indirectly, on the same few years of reported profit.

So one event can move several scores at once. Take a bank hit by a provisioning shock — a year when it must set aside much more for bad loans. Its net profit falls, so ROE falls; earnings quality and EPS growth weaken; cash dividends may be cut; and the share price, and so its valuation scores, may move too. That is not wrong in itself: a provisioning shock really does hurt in all those ways. But it means the score leans heavily on profit and on the credit cycle. One drought shows up in the paddy, the wheat and the farmer's savings all at once.

Whether the overlap actually distorts rankings is a question for testing, not argument. Correlation alone does not prove double counting; what matters is whether each part improves decisions made on data it has not seen. Once enough companies are scored, Chapter 80's audit will measure how closely the sub-scores move together (a correlation table and a principal-component analysis, which finds how many independent signals the seven dimensions really contain), drop one dimension at a time to see whether the order of companies changes, and compare the result with simple equal weights. Of these, dropping one dimension at a time on data the score has not seen is the decisive test; correlations and principal components only describe the overlap. Two more design choices need the same testing. Since version 1.5.0 a loss or negative book value scores 0, but the most expensive row of each table still earns 1 point, so an expensive, profitable company gets at least 2 of 15; an alternative with 0 in that row should be compared. And every threshold is in nominal rupees and percentages (Rs 5 million a day, 15% ROE, 8% growth), so the test must check whether scores drift upward over the years simply because prices and markets grew.

Comparing Companies

Compare totals within a sector, and compare the Business Quality Score and the Share Attractiveness Score as well as the total. Would You Buy HUBL at 87?

Not automatically. A high score says HUBL is a good business at a reasonable relative price. Three further questions decide whether, and how much, to buy:

  • What does the price assume? Lesson 64.3's justified P/B, at a 12% cost of equity and 5% growth: with HUBL's three-year average ROE of 14.2%, (14.2 − 5) ÷ (12 − 5) ≈ 1.31 times book. HUBL trades at 1.3, so the price already assumes it keeps earning about 14%. It is fairly priced, not cheap.
  • What could you lose? If HUBL's ROE slipped to a long-run 10%, the same formula gives (10 − 5) ÷ 7 ≈ 0.71 times book. From 1.3, that is a fall of about 45% with no fraud and no failure — just an ordinary bank becoming more ordinary.
  • Can you get out? Chapter 56's rule caps a position at about half of the share's average daily traded value. With turnover of about Rs 3.2 million a day (the six-month average, used here in place of Chapter 56's 20-day figure), that is about Rs 16 lakh. The band allows up to 8% of an equity portfolio. For a Rs 50 lakh portfolio, 8% is Rs 4 lakh — fine. For a Rs 5 crore portfolio, 8% would be Rs 40 lakh, more than twice what the trading allows; the ADV rule wins, and the position stops at about Rs 16 lakh (about 3%).

So the same 87 can mean "buy up to 8%" for one investor and "buy no more than 3%" for another, and "wait for a better price" for a third. Company quality, share attractiveness and portfolio suitability are three different questions; the score answers the first two, and Chapter 66 the third.

Testing status. Version 1.5.0 has no out-of-sample record at all. The forward pilot that began on 17 November 2026 tests version 1.2-P, and its November 2027 review will be a first-year look — far too short to show whether the score predicts long-term returns. Versions 1.2 to 1.5.0 were all written after seeing the 2021 bank data, so that data cannot prove they work. In a falling market, protection comes from Chapter 59's cycle settings, not from the score. A fair test needs scores frozen before results are known, every company in the sector (including those that later merged, failed or delisted), and returns that include dividends. It should then ask:
  • Ranking: do higher-scored companies go on to do better (rank correlation), within each sector as well as overall?
  • Returns and risk: total return including dividends, return per unit of risk, and the deepest fall from peak (maximum drawdown).
  • Simpler rivals: does the weighted score beat equal weights, weights estimated on one period and tested on another, and established tools — the Piotroski F-Score, a Greenblatt-style return-on-capital and earnings-yield rank for non-financial companies, and justified P/B for banks? Does it add anything beyond simple measures such as ROE, P/B and leverage alone? If not, the extra complexity is not earning its keep.
  • Dimension removal: does dropping any one dimension make the ranking better or worse?
  • Stability: does a small correction to one figure move the score a lot?
  • Point-in-time data: each past score must use only figures that had been published by its date — no later restatements.
  • Costs and execution: broker commission, SEBON and CDSC fees, and slippage on thin NEPSE shares, so the result is something an investor could actually have earned.
  • Benchmarks: the NEPSE index and the relevant sector index, not just zero.
  • Rebalancing: a rule, fixed in advance, for when scores are refreshed and when holdings change.
  • Market phases: results in rising, falling, flat and credit-stress periods, with annual return, volatility, the Sharpe ratio (return per unit of risk), downside deviation and portfolio turnover reported for each. Annual return compounds the period's returns; volatility is the standard deviation of daily returns times the square root of the number of NEPSE trading days in a year; the risk-free rate for the Sharpe ratio is the average yield on NRB's 91-day treasury bills over the same period.
  • Replication: the same method, unchanged, repeated on a separate period.
  • Weight and threshold sensitivity: change each weight by 10%–20% and each band line by a small step, and count how often the order of companies changes. A one-point gap that reverses under a small change is not meaningful.
  • Band calibration: do companies scoring 85–100 really show better later outcomes — fewer losses, capital breaches and dividend cuts — than those scoring 55–69?
  • Capacity: could the strategy be followed at realistic portfolio sizes without moving prices or paying too much in costs?

Some improvements would change what the score measures, so they should wait for evidence: giving each indicator one "home" dimension so the profit record is not counted several times; judging growth by the return earned on new capital, not just the growth rate; comparing the current weights with simpler alternatives, such as equal weights, to see whether they actually rank companies better; scoring the return on retained capital rather than only recording it; scoring customer, regional, product and supplier concentration separately; judging a lender's valuation with asset quality and its cost of capital, not ROE alone; recording how long and how deep a past capital breach was; judging board effectiveness by documents — related-party approvals and their terms, board challenges to management, auditor independence and rotation, follow-up of audit findings, treatment of minority shareholders and capital-allocation decisions — never by unsupported claims; hard gates applied before the points are added (a lower capital cap, a zero dividend dimension when retained earnings are negative, a bad-loan trend penalty), tested against the current caps; a lender's own funding liquidity and its sensitivity to interest-rate changes, the two CAMELS letters the score does not cover; a circuit-risk adjustment for liquidity; capital quality and stress tests for lenders; debt maturity for other companies; a sector table for hydropower concentration; return on invested capital and incremental return on capital for non-financial companies, judged against the cost of capital; inflation-adjusted growth; absolute and profitable loan growth alongside relative discipline; bid–ask spread, price impact and days to exit in the liquidity check; a floor or trend test for a collapsing latest-year ROE, and a flag when ROE rises only because equity shrank; judging an emphasis-of-matter paragraph and a restatement by their substance and size, so that an honest correction is not treated like a concealed error; recording the lender, purpose and release history of pledged shares; sector-specific indicators under the same seven dimensions, set in Chapter 65; a separate evidence grade (audited, disclosed, calculated, estimated, unverified) published beside the score rather than built into it; and, rather than more points, separate engines that answer separate questions — a financial-strength check such as the F-Score, an intrinsic valuation, a liquidity and exit model, and portfolio sizing — kept apart from the quality score.

Chapter recap

  • One number, seven parts. The Canon Quality Score adds seven dimensions to a total out of 100: Financial Strength (20), Governance (15), Liquidity (10), Valuation (15), Durability (15), Growth (15) and Dividends (10).
  • Three numbers, not one. Report the Business Quality Score (75), the Share Attractiveness Score (25) and the total. The total changes with the share price and must carry its price date.
  • Set for NEPSE. ROE bands follow the real spread of Nepali bank returns, CAR is judged as a regulatory cushion against each class's NRB minimum, and valuation against sector medians, with the plain numbers written beside them.
  • Two lessons from 2021 are built in. A cheap price counts only with above-median ROE, and a lender's loan growth is judged against its system.
  • Some red flags override everything. A low governance score, one severe governance flag, "problematic" status, capital below the Canon's buffer-inclusive minimum in any layer or an audit disclaimer caps the total at 54; proven fraud excludes the company.
  • Read the parts, not just the total. About 25–30 points rest on the same profit record; compare totals within a sector; and read every sub-score before the total.
  • A checklist, not a proof. The weights are judgement. Version 1.5.0 (23 November 2026) fixes the scoring steps but has no test record; version 1.2 and its pilot record stay frozen, and the pilot's first-year review comes in November 2027. Position limits tied to the bands are provisional ceilings, not evidence-based sizes.
