WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Scorecard — a fixed list of questions, each worth a few points, asked of every company in the same way, like an exam marking scheme. Grade band — a range of total scores with a name, such as A or B. Emphasis of matter — a paragraph in which the auditor draws attention to something without changing the opinion. Auditor rotation — changing the audit firm after some years, so that a fresh pair of eyes checks the accounts.

Every experienced NEPSE investor keeps a private list of companies they will not touch, however cheap they look. Ask why, and you rarely get a spreadsheet. You get a story: a rights issue that hurt small shareholders the week before a related party got a cheap loan; an AGM postponed twice with no reason; a promoter family whose stake slipped from 51 to 34 percent in three years while small investors kept buying. These stories are real, and the instincts they build are worth something. But an instinct is not a system. You cannot teach it, test it, or apply it the same way to the roughly 300 companies listed on NEPSE (302 by mid-July 2026; Chapter 7). And it suffers from the biggest bias in investing: excusing the faults of shares you own and spotting them everywhere in shares you do not.

This chapter closes Part IV by turning the earlier lessons — promoter behaviour, boards, related-party deals, disclosure habits — into something you can score, record and revisit. No agency rates the governance of individual NEPSE companies on a regular basis, so what follows is this book's own practical tool. It borrows the logic, not the exact questions, of international frameworks such as the G20/OECD Principles of Corporate Governance, and it uses only what a small investor in Nepal can actually see.

Lesson 23.1 — Why a Scorecard Beats a Gut Feeling

A scorecard is a fixed list of questions, each worth a few points, asked of every company in the same way — like an exam marking scheme. It beats a gut feeling for four reasons.

  • It treats every company the same. If you check one bank for pledged promoter shares but forget to check another, you are following a mood, not a method. A scorecard asks the same six questions, in the same order, every time.
  • It resists the latest headline. One loud scandal — a cooperative collapse, a merchant banker's licence suspended, an NRB restriction on a bank — can colour your view of a whole sector for months, while quieter decay elsewhere goes unmeasured. A fixed schedule from fixed inputs means a score moves because the company changed, not because the mood did.
  • It gives you a size, not just a feeling. "I don't love this company's governance" does not tell you whether to hold nothing, half a position or a full one. A score on a 0–12 scale with set bands does (Lesson 23.5).
  • It leaves a record. When a decision goes wrong, a dated, row-by-row score lets you see which input was wrong — a related-party note taken at face value, or a shareholding filing never checked. A feeling leaves nothing to learn from.
PRACTICAL RULE A governance score does not replace financial analysis; it multiplies it. A cheap share with terrible governance is not a bargain. It is a trap waiting for its moment.

The scorecard does not replace your judgement. It organises it, makes you apply it evenly, and makes you write down why a company scored as it did.

Lesson 23.2 — Design Principles for a Nepal-Buildable Scorecard

A Nepali scorecard is only useful if you can fill it in from documents any investor can get. That rules out much of what global scorecards use, such as searchable board-minute archives and paid proxy-advisory research.

The World Bank's Report on the Observance of Standards and Codes (ROSC) for Nepal's corporate governance, published in April 2005, found large gaps: thin ownership disclosure, weak rules on related-party deals, and a regulator with limited enforcement power. That report came before today's Companies Act and Securities Act, so read it as history, not as a current audit. But several of its themes still describe what a scorecard can and cannot measure.

Since then the rules have improved step by step. The Companies Act 2063 (2006) requires a public company to have a board of three to eleven directors, with at least one independent director on a board of seven or fewer and at least two on a larger board (Section 86; see Chapter 19 and Chapter 64). Section 89 lists the independent director's qualifications, such as not being an officer or the auditor of the company. Legal commentaries also describe rules on a woman director and a director resident in Nepal; check the current text before scoring a company on them. BAFIA 2073 adds board committees and "fit and proper" tests for banks — NRB's check that a bank director or chief executive is honest and qualified. SEBON has also asked listed companies to file a standard annual corporate governance report, covering the board, risk management, internal controls and organisation, in one format so companies can be compared.

These are real, checkable duties, and they are the raw material for the scorecard: annual reports, AGM notices and minutes, the SEBON governance report, NEPSE disclosures and the audited accounts. Four principles follow.

  • Score only what is disclosed. If the annual report does not say how many board meetings were held, record that as missing — itself a governance signal — instead of guessing.
  • Prefer direction to a single snapshot. A promoter holding 45 percent says little alone. A holding that fell from 58 to 45 percent over three annual reports says a lot.
  • Separate required from voluntary disclosure. A company that publishes more than the minimum — detailed related-party tables, directors' meeting attendance — is showing something about its culture.
  • Use the same four or five documents every time, so scoring the tenth company takes no new research method.
DEFINITION A disclosure-buildable scorecard is one where every answer comes from information a small investor can legally and practically get — annual reports, AGM minutes, SEBON filings and NEPSE disclosures — with no inside contacts or paid data.

Lesson 23.3 — The Six-Dimension NEPSE Governance Scorecard

The scorecard has six rows. Each scores 0, 1 or 2, so the total runs from 0 to 12. The rows echo international categories — treatment of shareholders, disclosure, board duties — but every question is something a NEPSE investor can actually check.

Two notes on using it. First, the related-party row and the promoter-trend row belong together: a promoter quietly selling while loans to promoter-linked companies grow is a recognisable danger pattern. Read the two rows side by side, not just as two numbers to add. Second, a long, unrotated auditor is a caution flag even without a legal limit. Banks and financial institutions cannot reappoint the same auditor more than three times in a row under BAFIA, but other companies have no such ceiling. An emphasis of matter is a paragraph in which the auditor draws attention to something without changing the opinion; auditor rotation means bringing in a fresh firm after some years, so new eyes check the accounts.

CAUTION A 2 on any row means "no red flag found in what was disclosed". It does not mean "independently confirmed". Nepal does not require outside checks that would let you confirm a related-party list is complete — only that it is present.

Add the six rows and read the band:

HOW THIS CONNECTS TO THE CANON SCORE This scorecard and the Governance & Promoter Behaviour dimension of the Canon Score (15 points, Chapter 64) look at the same evidence from two distances. This chapter's six rows give a wide, first reading and a grade from A to D. Chapter 64 turns most of the same evidence into three point-scored checks: promoter holding and pledging (6 points), related-party dealings and the audit opinion (5), and disclosure and board structure (4). The dividend-and-rights row is scored there under a different dimension, Dividend & Capital Return Discipline, and Chapter 64 adds promoter pledging, which this scorecard does not score. Do not convert a grade here into points there: score each with its own rules. If the two disagree sharply — a D here but 12 of 15 there, for example — go back to the evidence before trusting either.

Lesson 23.4 — Worked Example: Scoring a Hypothetical Commercial Bank

Take an invented commercial bank, "Bank X". It is made up to show the method, not to describe any real bank.

  • Board independence: 1. Seven directors (the most a bank may have under BAFIA): four for the promoters, two for public shareholders and one independent, with a short note on the criteria used. It meets the legal minimum and explains the choice, but the promoters still dominate.
  • Related-party transparency: 2. The notes list loans to companies where a director or promoter has a large interest, by name, balance and interest rate, and say the audit committee reviewed the list. That is much better than the single total many companies give.
  • Promoter trend: 2. Over four annual reports the promoter stake moved from 60 to 58 percent, entirely because of a required public share issue, not a promoter sale.
  • Disclosure timeliness: 1. The AGM was on time in three of four years. In one year it was about two months late, with a stated reason (the audit sign-off waited on an NRB inspection query). Quarterly results were always on time.
  • Dividend and rights history: 2. A mix of cash and bonus dividends; a rights issue three years ago at par, timed to meet a capital requirement and clearly explained in the AGM notice.
  • Audit: 1. The same audit firm reappointed three times in a row — the most BAFIA allows — with clean opinions, but no word on how the next auditor will be chosen.

Total: 1 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 1 = 9, the top of band B. The useful output is not "good" or "bad" but a dated record with two named items to re-check next year: a promoter-dominated board, and no visible plan for rotating the auditor.

Lesson 23.5 — Using the Score in Practice

A score earns its place only if it changes a decision. Use it in three ways.

  • As a size limit. One simple rule: A and B companies can be sized on their financial merits; C companies are capped, for example at half of what the financial analysis alone would allow; D companies get no new money, and existing holdings are reviewed for exit.
  • As a first screen. Scoring a new name takes under an hour from public filings, before you build any valuation model. A company that scores 0 on both related-party transparency and promoter trend — related lending rising while the promoter quietly sells — is worth an automatic pass, however cheap it looks.
  • As a price discount. For B and C companies, lowering the price you are willing to pay is often more honest than excluding them. A bank that would justify 1.3 times book value on its returns and growth, but scores C, might be capped at 1.0–1.1 times book. The market's lower multiple for weakly governed companies is usually a fair discount, not a mistake to exploit.
PRACTICAL RULE Set your size limits and exclusion lines before you score a company, not after. A rule invented after a tempting share lands in band C is not a rule; it is an excuse.

Re-score at least once a year after the annual report, and again after any big rights issue, merger or promoter deal — not only when a scandal makes headlines.

HOW THIS SCORECARD FITS WITH THE CANON SCORE This 12-point scorecard is a reading tool for Part IV: it helps you ask the right questions. The book's official scoring system is the Canon Score in Chapter 64. Its Governance dimension (15 of 100 points) has its own tables — promoter holding and pledging, audit opinion, and disclosure and board — and a hard limit: a governance total below 5 of 15 caps the whole Canon Score at 54. Where the two disagree about how much to buy, follow Chapter 64 and Chapter 66. Think of this scorecard as the rough notes from a site visit, and the Canon Score as the signed inspection report.

Lesson 23.6 — What This Scorecard Cannot Catch

A score built from disclosures is only as good as the disclosures, and weak enforcement means poor disclosure costs a company little. Know the blind spots.

  • Deals never disclosed. Nepal has no strong, standard approval-and-disclosure system for related-party deals. A linked supplier or borrower may not appear anywhere if a company reads its duties narrowly. A 2 means nothing troubling was found, not that nothing exists.
  • Hidden owners. Promoter holdings are only as accurate as who is classified as a promoter. Shares held by relatives, linked companies or nominees outside that label can hide the real trend, in either direction.
  • Substance behind the words. An "independent" director who meets the criteria on paper may be the chairman's old friend. An audit committee that "reviewed" a related-party list may have done so in five minutes. No filing will say so.
  • The past, not the present. The score uses the last annual report. A bad loan made this year will not show until next year's report, perhaps a year or more later.
  • Financial tricks. A company can score well here and still have aggressive revenue recognition, thin provisions or poor asset quality. Those belong to the financial analysis in Volume 2. The two checks work together; a high governance score is not a health certificate.
  • Your own bias. The biggest risk is scoring generously a company you already want to own. Score before you look at the valuation, and revisit old scores honestly when new reports arrive.
CAUTION Read every 2 as "no red flag found in available disclosure", not "confirmed clean". Where enforcement relies more on persuasion than penalties, the absence of a disclosed problem is weak evidence.

Chapter recap

  • A scorecard beats a gut feeling because it treats every company alike, resists headlines, turns unease into a size, and leaves a record you can learn from.
  • Build it only from documents any investor can get, prefer trends to snapshots, and reward companies that disclose more than the minimum.
  • Six rows — board independence, related-party transparency, promoter trend, disclosure timeliness, dividend and rights history, audit — each score 0–2, for a total of 0–12 and a grade from A to D.
  • Most real companies land in "adequate with named weaknesses"; the value is in naming those weaknesses and re-checking them.
  • Use the score as a first screen, a size limit and a price discount — but for official sizing follow the Canon Score in Chapter 64, where governance below 5 of 15 caps the total at 54.
  • It cannot see undisclosed deals, hidden owners, hollow independence, this year's events or financial tricks; a strong score means nothing troubling was found, not that nothing exists.
