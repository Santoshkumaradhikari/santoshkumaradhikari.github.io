WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE ROE — return on equity: profit divided by owners' money. ROA — return on assets: profit divided by everything the company controls. Leverage — using borrowed money; it lifts ROE in good years and deepens losses in bad ones, like farming with a loan-financed tractor. NIM — a bank's net interest margin. COD — commercial operation date, when a power plant starts selling electricity. Underwriting — the insurance business itself, separate from investment income.

Lesson 40.1 — ROE and ROA: The Universal Starting Point (and Why It Misleads Fast)

Two simple ratios

Every analyst who opens a NEPSE annual report ends up circling two lines: net profit and shareholders' equity.

  • Return on equity (ROE) = net profit ÷ average shareholders' equity. It shows how much the company earned for its owners. It is the number Nepali investors quote most to decide whether a company is "good".
  • Return on assets (ROA) = net profit ÷ average total assets. It shows how much the company earned from everything it controls, however that was financed.

Both are yearly percentages, and both look backwards: they describe what has happened, not what will. That is fine for judging how management used the money it was given. It is dangerous when used, unadjusted, to rank a bank against a hydropower company against an insurer against a noodle factory — which happens every day in NEPSE Facebook groups and broker WhatsApp threads.

Why comparing ROE across sectors misleads

  • A commercial bank usually has assets nine to eleven times its equity. Deposits fund most of it, and a thin slice of equity absorbs both the risk and, in good years, an outsized share of the return.
  • A hydropower company under construction may hold IPO money in fixed deposits and earn no operating revenue, so its ROE looks near zero — not because it is bad, but because it has not started.
  • A life insurer's profit depends as much on where its investment team places policyholders' reserves as on how well its actuaries priced the policies.
  • A manufacturer with a decades-old, barely diluted equity base can show a triple-digit ROE from a modest margin, simply because the denominator is tiny.

None of these numbers is wrong. Each is correctly calculated. What is wrong is reading them as if they measured the same thing — like comparing a bus's earnings per seat with a motorbike's.

Why ROA is fairer across sectors

ROA divides profit by all assets, not just equity, so it removes the effect of borrowing and comes closer to pure operating efficiency: how much profit each rupee in use produces, whether it came from shareholders, depositors or lenders. That is why bank ROA looks small (often under 2 percent) next to bank ROE in the mid-teens. Banks are built to run on thin equity; ROA shows that honestly while ROE magnifies it.

KEY CONCEPT ROE tells you how much a company earned for its owners. ROA tells you how much it earned from everything it owns, owners' and lenders' money combined. A high ROE built on heavy borrowing is a different animal from a high ROE built on high margins — and only ROA, or a full DuPont breakdown, tells you which one you are looking at.

So never let ROE alone decide a purchase. Pair it with ROA, and break it down further with DuPont analysis (Lesson 40.2). A hydropower share at 4 percent ROE two years after opening may be a better long-term hold than a finance company at 14 percent ROE built on heavy borrowing into a rate-sensitive loan book. The number alone cannot tell you that; the sector and the parts underneath can.

WARNING Screening NEPSE shares with one ROE cut-off (say, "only buy above 15 percent") without adjusting for sector systematically favours heavily borrowed financial companies and thin-equity manufacturers, and systematically excludes newly opened hydropower and young insurers — whatever their real long-run quality.

This chapter goes sector by sector, then shows how to put them back on a common footing when you really must compare — say, deciding whether your next lakh goes into a bank or a hydropower IPO.

Lesson 40.2 — Banks: Leverage, Net Interest Margin, and the Regulatory Ceiling on Returns

A bank runs on borrowed money

A bank borrows short (deposits) and lends long (loans), keeping the spread, at a scale many times its own equity. NRB's minimum paid-up capital for commercial banks — Rs 8 arba since the 2015 capital increase — sits under balance sheets that, for the larger banks, now exceed Rs 150–250 arba. That is an equity multiplier of about nine to twelve times, which is why bank ROE looks so much bigger than bank ROA.

Sector numbers, and why they fell

Banking has had a hard few years: tight liquidity, heavy provisions for bad loans and squeezed margins.

  • NRB's Financial Stability Report data and sector coverage through FY 2024/25 show aggregate commercial-bank ROE falling into the high single digits — as low as about 7.7 percent in one widely cited reading — down from the low-to-mid teens before the 2022–23 liquidity crunch.
  • A reading of the ten largest banks for the second quarter of FY 2081/82 put their combined ROE at about 9.4 percent.
  • Individual banks vary widely: well-capitalised banks with strong current and savings (CASA) deposits can still reach the mid-teens in a soft year, while weaker banks sit in the low single digits or near zero.
  • Sector ROA stays in a much narrower band, roughly 1.0 to 1.8 percent, because it depends on margin and efficiency, which move slowly, while ROE magnifies whatever leverage a bank runs.
REGULATORY DETAIL NRB limits bank profitability through levers no other NEPSE sector faces: a limit on the interest spread (historically about 4.4 percentage points between average lending and deposit rates for commercial banks, later commonly reported as 4 points, with somewhat more room for development banks and finance companies — though by mid-2025 the actual commercial-bank average had fallen to about 3.7 points, below the limit, as surplus liquidity pushed lending rates down); minimum capital ratios under the Basel framework; a credit-to-deposit (CD) ratio ceiling, which replaced the older CCD ratio in 2021–22; and countercyclical provisioning rules. Each caps how high bank ROE can go. A bank cannot simply widen its spread the way a manufacturer raises prices.

Net interest margin

No other sector has net interest margin (NIM), because no other sector's business is borrowing at one rate and lending at another.

NIM = net interest income ÷ average interest-earning assets

Net interest income is interest earned (on loans, government securities and interbank lending) minus interest paid (on deposits, borrowings and debentures). NIM is the engine room: it drives most operating income, and through it ROA and ROE.

Nepali commercial banks have usually run NIM of about 3.0 to 4.5 percent. It was squeezed in the 2022–23 liquidity crunch, when banks fought for deposits; more recently the pressure has come from the other side, as surplus liquidity and weak loan demand pull lending rates down.

A bank's NIM trend is the earliest, cleanest sign of whether its core lending is getting healthier. It moves before ROE, because ROE also absorbs one-offs — provision reversals, tax adjustments, trading gains — that can hide or exaggerate the trend for a quarter or two.

PRACTICAL TOOL When comparing two Nepali banks, look at the NIM trend over at least four quarters before looking at the latest ROE. A bank with rising NIM and falling ROE (because of a one-off provision) is usually a better long-term hold than one with falling NIM and a flattering ROE.

DuPont analysis: taking ROE apart

DuPont analysis splits ROE into three parts that multiply together, so one opaque number becomes three clear ones:

ROE = net profit margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier
= (net profit ÷ total operating income) × (total operating income ÷ average total assets) × (average total assets ÷ average equity)

For a bank, use total operating income — net interest income plus fees plus other operating income — not gross interest income, which overstates real activity because much of it is paid straight back to depositors.

An illustrative bank, built to look like a typical mid-to-large Nepali commercial bank (a teaching composite, not a real bank):

The breakdown shows what the headline 12.9 percent hides. The margin (25.3 percent) is healthy; asset turnover (5.1 percent — in effect the yield on the whole balance sheet) is normal for the sector; and the result leans heavily on the 10× multiplier.

If NRB tightened capital rules and forced this bank to raise new equity, cutting the multiplier to 8×, ROE would fall to about 10.4 percent with exactly the same business. Only the capital structure changed. NEPSE investors lived through exactly this in 2022–23, when several banks issued rights shares or FPOs to meet capital rules, diluting equity and mechanically lowering ROE even where NIM and loan quality were steady or improving.

CASE IN POINT When a Nepali bank announces a large rights issue, expect ROE to dip for one or two years purely because equity grew, before profit catches up. Investors who sold on the falling ROE alone, without checking that NIM and margin held steady, missed the recovery once the new capital was put to work.

DuPont is also the clearest way to compare two banks with similar ROE reached differently. One may get there with a fat margin and modest leverage, the other with a thin margin and heavy leverage. The first is more resilient in a downturn; the second gets hurt fastest when loans go bad, because its equity cushion is thinner.

Lesson 40.3 — Hydropower: The Pre-COD Mirage and the Post-COD Reality

One date splits the story

For a hydropower company, one date splits its whole profit picture in two: the commercial operation date (COD), when the plant joins the grid and starts selling power to the NEA under its power purchase agreement (PPA). Before COD, it earns almost no operating revenue. After COD, it becomes something like an annuity: a fixed buyer and a fixed price for the life of the PPA (typically 25 to 35 years), with output depending mainly on the river.

Why pre-COD ROE means almost nothing

Under NFRS (NAS 23), interest on construction loans is added to the project's cost rather than expensed while it is being built. That is correct accounting, but it means a company under construction shows little interest expense and little revenue. Its small reported profit comes almost entirely from bank interest on IPO money and promoters' capital parked in fixed deposits.

A 1–2 percent ROE before COD tells you almost nothing about ROE once the turbines turn. Screening hydropower IPOs on pre-COD profit really screens for which promoters parked their construction money best in fixed deposits — not which project will earn best. It is like judging a mango orchard by the interest on the farmer's savings before the trees have fruited.

WARNING A pre-COD or newly opened hydropower company's past ROE and ROA do not predict its profitability after ramp-up. For a hydropower IPO, read the Detailed Project Report's expected output, tariff schedule and debt-service cover instead of the past ratios.

The lumpy first years

Opening does not switch on full profit overnight. In the first one or two years, output is often below design as staff learn how the plant responds to the river, and early defects are fixed with the contractor. And run-of-river plants — most of NEPSE's hydropower — are highly seasonal: wet months (roughly Asar to Asoj, June to October) can produce several times the power of dry months (Poush to Chaitra, December to March).

A single quarter's ROA or ROE, without knowing the season, is close to meaningless. A strong monsoon quarter flatters annualised ROE; a dry quarter can make a healthy company look unprofitable. NEPSE Trading's coverage of companies such as Khanikhola Hydropower shows the pattern: steady or growing yearly revenue alongside losses in particular periods — which makes sense only once you separate seasonal swings and ramp-up from real decline.

After two or three full monsoon cycles, profits settle and become comparable. Mature plants such as Chilime Hydropower and Butwal Power Company have built long dividend records past this stage; Chilime in particular is known for steady double-digit cash dividends in mature years, reflecting a business that throws off more cash than it needs to reinvest.

CASE IN POINT An illustrative mid-sized run-of-river plant (Rs crore). Year −1 (before COD): revenue 0, profit 4 (deposit interest only), equity 120, ROE about 3%. Year 1 (ramp-up): revenue 65, profit 8, equity 128, ROE about 6%. Year 4 (mature): revenue 95, profit 22, equity 145, ROE about 15%. The jump from year 1 to year 4 is not a different asset — it is the same plant on the same tariff, simply past ramp-up and running at design output with its debt well covered.

Leverage again

Once mature, hydropower ROE is also driven by borrowing, and here it resembles banking more than manufacturing. Projects are typically funded 70:30 or even 80:20 debt to equity by groups of commercial and development banks, under NRB's hydropower lending norms. Once the plant earns cash, even a modest return on the whole project becomes a much bigger return on the thin equity slice — the same multiplier that drives bank ROE, applied to one physical asset. That is why mature hydropower ROE can look surprisingly close to bank ROE, though the businesses have almost nothing else in common.

Lesson 40.4 — Insurance: When Investment Income, Not Underwriting, Drives ROE

Profit comes mostly from the life fund

Casual commentary assumes an insurer profits from underwriting — collecting more in premiums than it pays in claims and costs. For Nepali life insurers especially, that is largely wrong.

A life insurer holds a large, growing pool of policyholders' reserves — the life fund — because premiums come in up front while claims and maturity payments go out years or decades later. The Nepal Insurance Authority (formerly Beema Samiti) dictates how it is invested: much in government securities and bank fixed deposits, a capped share in shares, and limits on property and other assets.

For an established insurer such as Nepal Life Insurance — NEPSE's largest listed life insurer — the life fund is many times its own equity. The income it earns (interest on government bonds and deposits, dividends, gains on the share portion) usually provides most of the profit in a normal year, far more than the underwriting result.

ROE follows interest rates

Because equity is small next to the fund, and investment income depends mainly on government-security yields and deposit rates rather than underwriting skill, insurer ROE moves with interest-rate cycles almost as much as with premium growth or claims. Established life insurers have posted ROE of roughly 12 to 25 percent or more in strong years — above banks — because investment income compounds on a big fund over a thin equity layer. It is the insurance version of the same leverage effect seen in banks and hydropower; here the "borrowed money" is policyholders' reserves.

KEY CONCEPT For a life insurer, split profit into two halves before trusting ROE: the underwriting result (premium minus claims, commission and acquisition costs) and the investment result (income on the life fund). A rising ROE from better underwriting is a more durable quality signal than one from a temporary jump in bond yields or one-off share gains.

Non-life: thinner margins, bumpier claims

Non-life insurers (motor, fire, marine, engineering and others) have shorter liabilities and faster turnaround from premium to claim. Track their combined ratio — claims plus expenses as a share of earned premium; below 100 percent means underwriting makes money — alongside ROE.

Non-life underwriting in Nepal has been squeezed by frequent motor claims and price competition among many insurers, so investment income on their smaller reserves often keeps ROE respectable in bad claims years. Non-life ROE has usually run lower and bumpier than life — commonly about 8 to 15 percent.

CASE IN POINT Sharesansar's quarterly reviews of non-life insurers routinely show wide differences in profit growth between companies even when premium growth looks similar across the sector. The swing factor is mostly investment income and one-off claims, not business growth.
CAUTION Do not rank Nepali insurers on past ROE without checking interest rates during the period. An insurer's ROE can look excellent just because government-security yields were high that year — a tailwind, not an underwriting achievement — and can fall just as easily when yields drop, with no change in how well it prices and manages risk.

Insurance on NEPSE also includes the two domestic reinsurers — Nepal Reinsurance Company and Himalayan Reinsurance — which insure the insurers. Their profit depends on the share of risk they accept from primary insurers and on their own investments. "Insurance" on NEPSE is really several business models — life, non-life and reinsurance — each with its own drivers.

Lesson 40.5 — Manufacturing and Consumer Names: High Margins, Thin Equity, and the Illusion of an Extraordinary ROE

Why some ROEs look unbelievable

Manufacturing and consumer names are a small but closely watched corner of NEPSE, led by long-established brand companies such as Unilever Nepal and the bottlers tied to global drinks brands. They need a third, separate way of thinking.

Unilever Nepal is the textbook case. It has an unusually small equity base relative to its profit, together with strong margins from established brands — soaps, detergents, personal care, tea — with real pricing power and reach across Nepal. The result is an ROE in the tens of percent, in some years far beyond what any bank, insurer or hydropower company could post.

That is not because its business is dozens of times better run than a good bank. It is because the denominator — equity — is so small relative to profit. A company with an ordinary margin and ordinary asset turnover can still show an extraordinary ROE if its equity multiplier is high because so little equity has built up.

CAUTION A manufacturer's headline ROE can be inflated by a small equity base rather than by exceptional performance. Always check ROA and the operating margin. A consumer company with a genuinely strong 15–20 percent net margin and healthy asset turnover is a good business whatever its ROE says; in a thin-equity name, ROE alone tells you more about capital history than about today's management.

ROA and margin tell the truer story

Manufacturers do not run on borrowed money the way banks and mature hydropower do, so their ROA is far more representative. For average NEPSE manufacturers it usually sits in the mid-to-high single digits or low double digits. For the strongest brands with pricing power and light assets (for example, contract bottling and manufacturing that keep fixed assets lean), it can reach the high teens or twenties. The gap between a very high ROE and a strong-but-modest ROA is the clearest illustration of why ROE alone is the wrong tool for comparing sectors.

PRACTICAL TOOL When a NEPSE manufacturer is promoted on social media for its eye-catching ROE, check its total equity against the profit it earns. ROE divides profit by total equity, reserves included, so a small paid-up capital alone does not lift ROE — it mainly inflates EPS and the face-value dividend percentage. What lifts ROE is a small total equity relative to profit, for example when a high payout keeps reserves thin. That is a thin-equity ROE: worth knowing, but not comparable with a bank's or a hydropower company's.

Dividends keep equity thin

Mature, low-investment businesses like these tend to pay out most of their profit as cash dividends, so equity barely grows — reinforcing the thin-equity, high-ROE pattern over time. A growing manufacturer that reinvests heavily (new capacity, wider distribution) will see equity grow faster, so its ROE trend must be read in that light. A falling ROE at a manufacturer in an investment phase may simply mean a bigger equity base doing work not yet rewarded — not a worsening business.

Lesson 40.6 — Building Your Own Cross-Sector Benchmark Table

If you hold, or are considering, shares in more than one sector, build a benchmark table — in your head or a spreadsheet — that judges each share against its own sector's realistic range, not against one NEPSE-wide number that fits none of them.

Three rules for using it honestly

  1. Find the company's row first. A 12 percent ROE is disappointing for a strong life insurer in a normal year, about average for a bank, and quite good for a two-year-old hydropower plant still ramping up.
  2. Look at trend and ROA, not one period's ROE. A bank's ROE dip after a rights issue, a hydropower plant's swing between wet and dry quarters, and an insurer's bump from high bond yields are all explained by mechanics in this chapter. None alone should trigger a buy or sell.
  3. Compare across sectors with ROA and DuPont, not headline ROE. Use sector-specific measures — NIM for banks, output and tariff for hydropower, combined ratio and investment yield for insurers — as the context that explains the ROA.
PRACTICAL TOOL Before comparing two NEPSE shares from different sectors, write side by side: (1) ROE, (2) ROA, (3) the equity multiplier (assets ÷ equity) and (4) the sector's driver (NIM, plant output, combined ratio or net margin). If the ROE gap mostly disappears when you look at ROA, the "better" share is mainly a leverage story, not an operating-quality story — worth knowing before you invest.

This does not make profitability ratios useless across sectors; it means using them correctly. An investor who knows that a hydropower company's low first-year ROE is structural and temporary, that a bank's ROE dip after a rights issue is mechanical, that an insurer's outstanding ROE in a high-yield year partly reflects interest rates, and that a manufacturer's spectacular ROE partly reflects decades-old capital history is reading the same numbers as everyone else on NEPSE — but reading them correctly.

Chapter recap

  • ROE (profit ÷ equity) shows the return to owners; ROA (profit ÷ assets) shows operating efficiency regardless of financing. Never compare ROE across sectors without ROA and the sector context.
  • Banks run on 9–12× leverage, so ROE magnifies a ROA of about 1–1.8%. Sector ROE fell to about 7.7–9.4%. NRB limits on spread, capital and the CD ratio cap returns. NIM is the leading indicator; DuPont (margin × turnover × multiplier) shows whether ROE comes from margin or leverage, and why rights issues dilute it.
  • Hydropower ROE before COD is just deposit interest; the first years are lumpy and seasonal; mature plants earn about 8–20% helped by project leverage.
  • Insurers earn mostly from investing the life fund, so ROE follows interest rates. Split underwriting from investment results; track the combined ratio for non-life.
  • Manufacturers can show extreme ROE from thin equity; ROA and margin tell the truer story.
  • Judge each share against its own sector's row, look at trends and ROA, and use ROA and DuPont to compare across sectors.
