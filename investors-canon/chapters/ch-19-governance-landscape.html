WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Promoter shares — the founders' shares, locked for years before they can be sold. Independent director — a board member with no family or business tie to the owners, like a neutral elder on a village committee. Audit committee — the board group that checks the accounts and the auditor. Related party — a person or company linked to the owners or directors, such as a director's brother's construction firm. Agency risk — the risk that the people in control use the company for themselves. "Type I" is hired managers against owners; "Type II", the usual Nepali case, is controlling owners against small shareholders — like the eldest brother of a joint family holding the shop's cash box while the younger brothers only see the year-end total. Comply or explain — follow the rule, or write down why not. Fit and proper — NRB's test that a bank director or chief executive is honest and qualified.

Imagine you buy 100 shares of a bank through a broker in Kathmandu. A family founded that bank thirty years ago and still owns more than half of it. The law says your 100 shares carry the same rights, share for share, as theirs. In practice, they choose the board, the board chooses the chief executive, and the chief executive decides who gets loans. Whether your money stays yours depends on how that power is used. That is what corporate governance means: the rules and habits that decide whether the people in control treat small shareholders fairly.

This chapter opens Part IV because everything after it rests on this idea. Nepal has a fairly modern rulebook on paper. What it often lacks is the staff, speed and independence to enforce it. An investor who sees that gap reads the same annual report very differently from one who trusts a neat cover page.

KEY IDEA Governance is not paperwork. It decides whether a small shareholder's money is protected or quietly taken. On NEPSE, where a few owners control most companies, that test comes up far more often than in big, widely owned markets.

Lesson 19.1 — Why Governance Is the Central Risk in Nepali Equities

Who owns the company

Most NEPSE companies did not start as public companies. They began as family firms or groups of local business people — a trading house, a hydropower developer, the founders of a finance company. Later they sold shares to the public, usually because the law required it (banks and insurers must have public shareholders) or because they needed money to grow. The founders kept a big block. These are the promoters.

Promoter shares and public shares have the same face value, the same dividend and the same one vote per share. The difference is that promoter shares are locked in for years after the IPO: three years for most companies under SEBON's Securities Registration and Issue Regulation, 2073, and longer for banks (Chapter 13 gives the detail). For banks, NRB policy also expects promoters to hold a large share — commonly 51 percent — so control is not an accident. It is required.

So the risk on NEPSE is different from the risk in America or Britain. There, shares are spread across millions of owners, and the worry is that hired managers run the company for themselves. Experts call that "Type I" agency risk. Here, the worry is that the controlling owners run the company for themselves at the expense of the small owners. That is "Type II" agency risk. Think of a joint family where the eldest brother holds the shop's cash box. The younger brothers own part of the shop too, but they only see the year-end total. The danger is not a hired shop assistant stealing; it is the brother with the key.

WATCH FOR In a promoter-controlled company, do not only ask, "Is the board independent of the managers?" Ask, "Is the board independent of the controlling owners?" They are different questions, and Nepali annual reports rarely answer the second one.

This is why a cheap share price is not automatically a bargain. A low price may be the market's way of saying that profits might never reach small shareholders — because they leak out through loans to the owners' friends, through overpriced contracts, or through money kept back for the family's other businesses.

Lesson 19.2 — The Legal and Regulatory Architecture

Three layers of rules apply to every listed company, and a fourth applies to banks:

  • The Companies Act 2063 (2006) — the basic law for all companies.
  • The Securities Act 2063 (2007) — the law for shares and the stock market. It gives SEBON its powers.
  • SEBON's directives — detailed rules for listed companies.
  • For banks and financial institutions, NRB's rules under BAFIA (the Bank and Financial Institution Act 2073). This is often the strongest layer, because NRB has real power over banks.

What the Companies Act requires

Board size and independent directors (Section 86). A public company must have a board of three to eleven directors. It must have at least one independent director if the board has seven or fewer members, and at least two if it has more. An independent director is someone with no family or business tie to the company — like a neutral elder on a village committee. Section 89 lists the conditions; for example, the person must not be an officer or the auditor of the company. The law is real, not optional. But whether a so-called independent director is really free of the promoter family depends largely on what the company itself says.

Audit committee (Section 164). A listed company with paid-up capital of Rs 30 million or more — which covers almost every company that matters on NEPSE — must have an audit committee of at least three members. A director who does not run the day-to-day business must chair it, and at least one member must have an accounting qualification or relevant experience. The board must follow the committee's advice or explain in the annual report why not. In practice, many audit committee reports are one short paragraph saying the committee met. That tells you almost nothing.

Directors' shareholdings (Section 100). Directors of a listed company must report the shares they hold. The company passes this to the stock exchange, which publishes it. This is how you can track whether directors and promoters are buying or selling (Lesson 19.5).

The Securities Act and SEBON's governance directive

The Securities Act set up SEBON as the market regulator, with power to make rules and punish breaches with fines or prison. Under it, SEBON issued a Corporate Governance Directive for listed companies (the 2074 directive, applied from fiscal year 2074/75, that is, mid-July 2017 to mid-July 2018). It goes further than the Companies Act. Its main rules include:

  • a board term of at most four years, with no extension;
  • the chairperson and the chief executive must be different people;
  • board members may not also act as auditor, adviser, surveyor, insurance agent or broker for other companies;
  • a three-member audit committee, plus risk-management and internal-control arrangements;
  • no lending to board members' family members;
  • disclosure when several family members sit on one board;
  • a ten-year bar on anyone convicted of a financial crime serving as a director or executive.

Together, these make a fairly complete rulebook. The weak point is supervision. SEBON has had a small enforcement staff for the number of companies and brokers it watches. Its visible actions have more often been small fines on brokers for procedural slips than cases against company insiders. More recently SEBON has spoken of "zero tolerance" for insider trading. A statement is not the same as a finished case, so watch for published orders, not headlines.

Lesson 19.3 — The NRB Overlay: Governance for Banks and Financial Institutions

Banks, development banks and finance companies make up a large part of NEPSE. For them, NRB acts as a second, stricter regulator on top of the Companies Act and SEBON.

NRB sets minimum qualifications for bank chief executives and directors. A commercial or development bank CEO typically needs a master's degree in a relevant subject — management, banking, finance, economics, commerce, accounting, statistics, mathematics or law — or a relevant bachelor's degree plus about ten years of experience. Directors have several routes to qualify, all tied to real experience. The aim is to keep people without financial knowledge, or with only political connections, off bank boards. NRB can also question directors about particular loans, order a bank to fix how it classifies loans or pays interest, warn or penalise officials, and in serious cases change the management.

CASE IN POINT: NIC ASIA BANK, 2025 In January 2025, NRB's supervision report said NIC Asia Bank had paid directors, their families and linked institutions higher interest than its published rates, that some fixed-deposit accounts had been opened with backdated entries, and that the bank had not followed NRB's directives on loan classification, provisioning, capital and risk weights. NRB warned the chief executive, the chairman and two directors and told the bank to recover the extra interest (Fiscal Nepal, 22 January 2025). A later report said the bank had not fully complied, and the chief executive resigned on 17 September 2025, about a year before his term ended (eKantipur, 18 September 2025; Nepal News, 19 October 2025). An unnamed retired NRB executive director told Nepal News that the bank's "influence and access" had made action hard. The lesson: governance failures at listed banks are not just history from the 2010s finance-company crisis. They happen at big, well-known banks too.

This extra layer matters when you compare sectors. A listed manufacturer answers only to the Office of the Company Registrar, SEBON and its own board. A listed bank also answers to NRB, which has real power over licences, directors, provisions and capital. That does not make banks safe — NIC Asia shows that — but it means the floor is higher. A public NRB action against a bank is a strong outside signal that has no equal for most non-financial companies.

Lesson 19.4 — Where the Framework Breaks Down in Practice

A rulebook is only as good as the people enforcing it. Three Nepali episodes show what happens when enforcement is weak.

The 2009–2011 banking and finance company crisis

Banks and finance companies poured loans into land, housing and construction. Reports said loans backed by fixed assets made up more than 70 percent of commercial bank lending at the peak. Underneath was a licensing failure: by 2011 there were more than 200 banks, development banks, finance companies and microfinance institutions — far more than the economy could support. Commentators at the time also spoke of regulatory capture: officials close to retirement had little reason to confront an industry where they might later look for jobs. Some institutions, such as Vibor Bikas Bank, needed direct NRB intervention because of heavy real-estate lending, and some development banks were later closed. The point: governance failure in Nepal has often been system-wide, not just one bad company.

The cooperative collapse — a warning from outside NEPSE

Savings and credit cooperatives are not listed and fall under a different ministry, mostly with local-government oversight. In 2024 a parliamentary probe committee reported that about 40 cooperatives had embezzled roughly Rs 87 billion (Rs 87 arba), with directors diverting members' savings into land and other ventures. This shows the worst case: control by a small inside group, plus almost no outside checking. Money can disappear long before anyone acts. Smaller, less-watched listed companies carry a milder version of the same risk.

Insider trading and leaked news

Price-sensitive news — results, dividends, rights issues, mergers — sometimes reaches brokers or connected people before it reaches everyone. The Securities Act does deal with this. Section 91 defines unpublished, price-sensitive "insider information", and Section 92 deems three groups to have access to it: the company's directors, employees and shareholders who can obtain it; people who provide professional services to the company; and anyone in direct or indirect contact with either group (Chapter 21). The punishment under Section 101 is a fine equal to the amount involved, up to one year in prison, or both. The weakness has been proving cases, not the wording of the law. SEBON has more often issued general warnings than finished prosecutions. So unusual price or volume moves just before an announcement deserve your attention.

ENFORCEMENT GAP A rule that is never used against a real offender deters less than a smaller rule that is used. Weigh Nepal's governance rules by how often they bite, not by how they read.

Lesson 19.5 — Reading Governance Quality From the Annual Report: A Practical Checklist

Because enforcement is thin, the checking falls on you. The good news: annual reports, AGM notices and company disclosures contain real information. You do not need a Bloomberg terminal, only patience. Run this checklist on every company before you value it.

  • Board independence in substance. Does the company meet the legal minimum of independent directors? Then go further: do any "independent" directors share a surname, an address or a past business with the promoter family? Does an "independent" director at one company sit as a promoter's nominee at a sister company? That is a warning sign, not proof.
  • What the audit committee actually did. Read its report. Does it describe real work — a problem the auditor raised, a related-party deal it questioned, a control weakness fixed? Or is it one paragraph saying it met? Year after year of saying nothing, at a company with visible problems, is itself a signal.
  • Related-party dealings. A related party is anyone linked to the owners or directors — a director's brother's construction firm, a promoter's insurance agency. Does the annual report name these counterparties and give amounts? Or does it use vague totals? Look for loans, deposits, contracts and leases with directors, their families or linked companies.
  • Board terms and chair/CEO separation. Are the chair and CEO different people, as the directive requires? Has the same small circle rotated seats for a decade? That keeps the letter of the four-year rule while defeating its purpose.
  • Directors' and promoters' share dealings. Use the Section 100 records to see whether directors and promoters are net buyers or sellers, and whether trades cluster just before big announcements.
  • Regulatory history. Search for any NRB or SEBON action, fine or warning against the company or its officers. For banks, an NRB action deserves more weight than a tidy annual report.
  • The auditor. Has the same auditor stayed for many years? Banks and financial institutions cannot reappoint the same auditor more than three times in a row under BAFIA, but other companies have no such limit. Read the audit opinion for qualifications, emphasis-of-matter paragraphs or going-concern doubts.
  • Dividends against profits. Compare cash returned to shareholders with reported profit and the stated dividend policy. A lasting gap with no explained reinvestment plan is a classic sign that value is being kept for someone else.
PRACTICAL TOOL None of these checks needs special access. They use documents every listed company must already publish. The skill is reading them every year, in the same order.

Lesson 19.6 — Governance as a Valuation and Position-Sizing Input

Your governance reading should change two decisions: how much you pay, and how much you hold.

How much to pay

A company with weak, owner-captured governance deserves a lower price multiple than an equally profitable company with a genuinely independent board, clean related-party records and a good regulatory history. This is not a matter of taste. Its profits are less likely to reach you — through dividends, buybacks (rare on NEPSE) or an eventual sale — because part of the value may leak to the controlling group first. A careful lender charges a weaker borrower more interest. A careful investor pays a weaker-governed company a lower price. The Canon Score in Chapter 64 builds this in: its Governance dimension is worth 15 of 100 points, and a governance total below 5 caps the whole score at 54.

How much to hold

Two shares may look equally cheap. If one has clearly stronger governance, it can support a larger position, because the chance of a sudden, governance-driven loss is lower. Be careful about false comfort: five promoter-controlled banks in your portfolio are five shares, but they may share one weakness. A change in NRB's stance, or a shock to trust in enforcement, can hit them all at once — as happened across the sector in 2009–2011.

Keep re-checking

Governance changes. A board that adds a truly independent director, a company that starts naming related parties, or a bank that visibly fixes an NRB finding is improving. An audit committee that goes quiet on a problem it used to flag, or related-party notes that get vaguer, is getting worse — whatever the profits say. Re-score at every annual report and AGM.

Chapter recap

  • On NEPSE the main governance risk is controlling owners against small owners ("Type II"), not managers against owners. Promoters often hold most of the shares, and banks are expected to keep about 51 percent with promoters.
  • The rulebook is fairly modern: independent directors under Companies Act Section 86 (one on a board of seven or fewer, two on a larger board), audit committees, and SEBON's 2074 directive (four-year terms, chair and CEO separated, no loans to directors' families).
  • NRB adds a stricter layer for banks — qualifications, supervision and real penalties — as the 2025 NIC Asia case showed.
  • The weak point is enforcement: the 2009–2011 crisis, the cooperative collapse and few finished insider-trading cases all show rules that existed but did not bite in time.
  • You can read governance yourself from public documents: board independence, the audit committee's work, related-party notes, director share dealings, regulatory history, the auditor and dividends against profits.
  • Use the result twice: pay less for weak governance, hold less of it, and re-check every year. In the Canon Score, governance below 5 of 15 caps the total at 54.
