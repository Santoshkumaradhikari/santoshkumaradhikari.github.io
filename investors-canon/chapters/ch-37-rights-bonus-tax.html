WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Rights share — a new share you choose to buy, usually at Rs 100, because you already own the company. Bonus share — a free share made from the company's reserves and taxed as a dividend. Cost basis — the figure your profit is measured from when you sell. ASBA — the system that blocks the application money in your bank account. Renounce — to hand your rights entitlement to someone else. Face value — the printed value of a share, Rs 100 for most NEPSE companies.

Ramesh followed Chapter 36 carefully. When Karnali Finance Ltd declared a 15% bonus, he watched his demat account update and filed away the record of the 5% tax. Six months later, Himalchuli Cement Ltd offered a 1:1 rights issue at Rs 100. He paid through Connect IPS, received his new shares, and thought the matter was closed.

It was not. When he later sold both blocks, his broker's contract note showed two different gains, measured from two different costs, and neither matched what he expected. (Both companies are made-up examples.)

The confusion was not Ramesh's fault. This is a genuinely unsettled corner of Nepali tax practice. It has produced a tax directive, investor protests and a government task force. This chapter untangles it, one step at a time.

Lesson 37.1 — Two Different Doors: How Rights and Bonus Shares Enter the Tax System

Bonus shares: the company's money coming to you

Chapter 36 set out the rule for bonus shares. When a company turns its saved profits (reserves) into new shares for existing owners, the tax office treats it like a cash dividend. Tax of 5% applies when the shares are issued, and for a resident individual that 5% is a final tax. Every company that declares a bonus must find the cash to pay that tax, even though the "dividend" arrives as shares, not rupees.

Rights shares: your money going to the company

Rights shares work the other way round. A rights issue is not the company handing you its profits. It is an offer to buy new shares, at a price the company sets (usually Rs 100, sometimes more), with your own money.

You earned that money already and paid tax on it — through salary, business or an earlier share sale. So paying for rights shares creates no new income. You are just swapping cash for shares, as if you bought more on NEPSE. No tax is withheld when you subscribe. The tax comes only when you sell.

Think of two ways of getting more goats for your farm. In one, the cooperative gives you goats from its own herd — that is a bonus, and the cooperative's gift is taxed. In the other, you buy goats from the cooperative with your own savings — that is a rights issue, and buying is not income.

KEY CONCEPT A bonus share moves the company's value to you, and is taxed like a dividend when issued. A rights share moves your money to the company, and is treated like any purchase — no tax until you sell. Mixing the two up is the most common error investors make when checking their yearly capital gains statements.

Lesson 37.2 — Rights Shares: Establishing Cost Basis From Day One

What goes into the cost

A rights subscription is a real purchase. So its cost basis — the figure your profit is later measured from — is the price you paid plus the costs of getting the shares. For a rights issue that usually means:

  • the issue price per share (often Rs 100);
  • any bank or ASBA charge — ASBA ("Applications Supported by Blocked Amount") is the system that blocks the money in your bank account when you apply;
  • any DP charge for crediting the shares to your demat account.

This is the same logic Chapter 35 (Lesson 35.3) used for market purchases. The charges are small, but people forget them, and over many years the error adds up.

Example: you hold 500 shares of Himalchuli Cement. It offers rights at 1:2 (one new share for every two held) at Rs 100. You are entitled to 250 shares.

Cash paid: 250 × Rs 100 = Rs 25,000
Bank/ASBA charge (example): Rs 10
Cost added: Rs 25,010
Cost per rights share: Rs 25,010 ÷ 250 = Rs 100.04

That Rs 100.04 goes into your average cost. It is not zero, not the market price, and not the face value used for bonus shares. It is simply what you paid.

What if you do not subscribe?

A rights entitlement gives you three choices: take up all of it, take up part of it, or let it lapse. In some offers you can also renounce it — transfer it privately to another investor through the share registrar before the window closes. Rights cannot yet be traded on NEPSE; a SEBON proposal of 19 November 2026 may change this (Chapter 15).

If you let it lapse, there is no tax. But your share of the company shrinks, because others add money and you do not. Unsubscribed shares go to a company auction (Chapter 15).

If someone pays you for a renounced entitlement, the money is a capital gain. You paid nothing for the right itself — it came automatically with your shares — so its cost is zero and the whole amount is gain. The rate depends on how long you held the parent shares that gave you the right.

WARNING Money for a renounced right feels like "found money", because you never paid for it. But the whole amount is taxable gain, with no cost to subtract. Set some aside for tax.

Lesson 37.3 — Bonus Shares Revisited: From Deemed Dividend to Cost Basis

How the 5% is usually paid

Chapter 36 showed that companies usually pay the bonus tax by declaring a small cash dividend alongside the bonus. The cash part is sized so that it equals 5% of the whole distribution — the bonus value divided by 0.95. A "5% bonus" thus comes with about 0.26% cash, because 5 ÷ 0.95 = 5.26, and 5% of 5.26 is about 0.26.

The cost should be the value already taxed

The principle is simple. If a bonus share's value has already been taxed once, as a dividend, then that value should be its cost from then on. When you sell, capital gains tax should fall only on the rise above it. Otherwise the same rupee is taxed twice.

In practice, that value has been anchored to the share's face value — Rs 100 for most NEPSE companies — because the bonus tax itself is worked out on face value, not on the market price.

REGULATORY DETAIL In Jestha 2075 (June 2018), the Inland Revenue Department told NEPSE and the depository system to measure gains on bonus and rights shares using the paid-up (face) value as the cost. Before that, brokers had used an "average base price" taken from market data. For a Rs 100 share, every bonus share's cost became Rs 100, whatever the market price had done — much lower than the old method for most rising shares, but still a cost, not zero.

The directive caused an immediate backlash. Investor groups said it had been imposed without notice and clashed with how brokers' systems tracked cost. Trading was disrupted. Within days the government postponed it and set up a task force to review the formula.

Practice has changed since then, and different broker systems still show different figures. But the episode shows the exact fault line investors keep hitting: is a bonus share's cost its face value, or zero?

Why the answer matters

  • Cost recorded as Rs 100: sell at Rs 850, and your taxable gain is Rs 750 per share.
  • Cost recorded as zero — as some broker calculators and statements show, taking "you paid nothing" too literally — your taxable gain becomes the full Rs 850.

A zero cost adds Rs 100 to the taxable gain on every bonus share, on top of the 5% you already paid when the bonus was issued. That stacking is the "double taxation" complaint heard in investor forums and budget consultations year after year.

CASE IN POINT An investor holds 1,000 shares of a made-up company, Sagarmatha Hydro Ltd, and receives a 10% bonus: 100 new shares of Rs 100. The company withholds about Rs 526 of tax on the grossed-up bonus (100 × Rs 100 ÷ 0.95 × 5%). Two years later the investor sells the 100 bonus shares at Rs 620 — Rs 62,000 in total. With a Rs 100 cost (Rs 10,000), the taxable gain is Rs 52,000. With a zero cost, it is Rs 62,000. The Rs 10,000 difference is exactly the value already taxed as a dividend. A face-value cost prevents the double count; a zero cost does not.

This book recommends holding your broker and DP records to the face-value position: the Rs 100 already taxed must carry forward as cost. Keep the TDS (tax deducted at source) record issued at the time of the bonus. It is your proof that this value was taxed, and it is what you would show to get a zero-cost entry corrected.

Lesson 37.4 — WACC Mechanics: Blending Purchases, Rights, and Bonus Into One Average Cost

One average per company

Capital gains on listed shares are worked out company by company. Within one company, there is one blended average cost — WACC, "weighted average cost" — not a separate cost for each purchase, rights allotment or bonus. This is the figure your DP account and Meroshare portfolio should show.

WACC = Total cost of all shares held ÷ Total shares held

Every new event changes both the top and the bottom of that fraction, but in different ways:

  • A rights allotment adds shares and adds real cost (what you paid). It can push the average up or down, depending on whether the rights price is above or below your current average.
  • A bonus allotment adds shares and adds only the Rs 100 face value as cost. That is almost always below your current average, so a bonus nearly always pulls your average cost down.

Think of mixing tea. Adding a cheap batch lowers the average price per kilo of the whole sack; adding an expensive batch raises it.

Worked example, step by step

An investor's history in one made-up company, Himal Hydro Ltd, over three years:

Look at the direction of each step:

  • The rights at Rs 100 were far below the Rs 401.51 average, so the average fell to Rs 301.04. Rights priced above your average would push it up instead.
  • The bonus, at Rs 100 a share, pulled it further down to Rs 282.76. This is almost certain for any company trading above face value — which is nearly every company on NEPSE.
  • The second purchase at Rs 550, above the average, pushed it back up to Rs 329.95.

That Rs 329.95 is what the broker's system will use for any Himal Hydro share you sell next — whether it was originally a purchase, a rights share or a bonus share. The system does not ask "which shares are these?" It asks "what is your average cost in this company?"

PRACTICAL TOOL Meroshare and most broker systems show a running WACC for each company. After every rights or bonus credit, check that it has actually changed. Some systems lag behind by a settlement cycle, and a stale figure will misstate your gain on any sale in that gap. If in doubt, rebuild the table above by hand from your own records. It takes minutes, and it is the only way to catch a bonus batch wrongly entered at zero cost.
CAUTION Two investors who bought the same share on the same day at the same price can end up with very different tax bills years later — simply because one took up a rights issue and the other let it lapse. Do not assume your WACC matches a friend's or a figure in a Facebook group. It depends on your own history, so rebuild it from your own contract notes and allotment records.

Lesson 37.5 — The Double-Taxation Debate and the Current Rate Environment

The investors' argument

The face-value-or-zero question is not settled history. It comes back every time capital gains policy is reviewed. Knowing the shape of the argument is more useful than memorising one year's position.

Investors argue that a bonus share is not free money. Its value comes from past profits on which the company already paid company tax. That is the first layer. The shareholder then pays 5% when the bonus is issued. That is the second. If, at sale, the system records the share's cost as zero, the same value is taxed a third time, in full. It is this third layer that investor groups object to.

The tax office's view

The tax administration's view is that capital gains tax is a tax on the rise in value since your cost was fixed. The whole question is where that fixed point should be. Face value ties it to the amount already taxed as a dividend. The old market-based average, or zero, puts it somewhere else, with different results for revenue and fairness. That is the tension the 2075 directive, the protest and the task force were trying to resolve.

So treat a bonus share's cost as something to check against your own records each time, not a fact fixed for ever.

The rate has moved twice in 2026

On top of this, the headline rate has changed twice this year (Chapter 35):

  • The Finance Act for FY 2083/84, from Shrawan 1, 2083 (mid-July 2026), raised it from 7.5% to 10% for shares held one year or less, and from 5% to 7.5% for shares held longer.
  • A Cabinet decision published in the Nepal Gazette on 22 September 2026 cut it to 5% (one year or less) and 3.75% (more than one year).

Gains on unlisted shares stay at a flat 10% for individuals, whatever the holding period. For an individual investor who is not running a share-trading business, the broker deducts the tax at settlement, and it does not get added to your other income for the slabs.

REGULATORY DETAIL Capital gains tax on listed shares for a resident individual since 22 September 2026: 5% if held 365 days or less; 3.75% if held more than 365 days. From mid-July to 21 September 2026 the rates were 10% and 7.5%; in FY 2082/83 they were 7.5% and 5%. Unlisted shares: a flat 10%. Whatever the rate, every rupee of wrongly recorded cost becomes a rupee of overstated gain.

The practical lesson: rates change often, but a cost error costs you every time. A bonus batch wrongly recorded at zero overstates your gain by Rs 100 on every bonus share, whatever the rate when you sell. Check your numbers; do not assume the system has them right.

Lesson 37.6 — Practical Filing and Recordkeeping Checklist for Rights and Bonus Shares

Everything in this chapter leads to one habit: keep your own records for every rights and bonus event, separate from what the broker or DP system shows. If the wrong number is used at sale, you — not the broker — bear the cost.

For every rights issue, keep:

  • the allotment confirmation from the company or registrar;
  • the bank debit or ASBA confirmation showing exactly what you paid;
  • any DP or bank charges on the application.

For every bonus issue, keep:

  • the AGM or board resolution giving the bonus percentage and the face value at the time;
  • most importantly, the TDS record showing the 5% was deducted and deposited. This is your proof that the face value was already taxed.

When you sell part of a holding

If you sell only some of your shares in a company — a mix of original, rights and bonus shares built up over years — one blended WACC applies across the whole holding. You cannot choose to sell "the expensive batch first" to cut your tax; the average applies to every share. (The holding-period clock is separate: each batch keeps its own start date, as Chapter 35 explained.) That is why keeping your WACC accurate after every corporate action matters more than tracking each batch's cost.

Compare it with Meroshare or your broker's statement. A mismatch found before you sell is a correction. A mismatch found after you sell is a tax dispute.

Chapter recap

  • Rights shares are a cash purchase: no tax when you subscribe, and your cost is what you paid plus bank, ASBA and DP charges.
  • Bonus shares are a dividend paid in shares: 5% final tax on their face value when issued, usually paid from a small cash dividend declared with the bonus.
  • Money received for a renounced rights entitlement is taxable gain in full, because the right cost you nothing.
  • The face value already taxed should become the bonus share's cost. The IRD's Jestha 2075 directive used face value, but it was paused after protests and a task force was set up. Some systems still show zero, which taxes the same value again.
  • All purchases, rights and bonus shares in one company blend into one WACC. Bonus shares almost always pull it down; rights can move it either way.
  • Since 22 September 2026, individuals pay 3.75% on listed shares held more than 365 days and 5% on shares held 365 days or less; unlisted shares stay at 10%.
  • Keep your own records — allotment confirmations, TDS records and a hand-built WACC — and check them against Meroshare before you sell.
  • Chapter 38 turns from these two events to planning tax across a whole portfolio.
