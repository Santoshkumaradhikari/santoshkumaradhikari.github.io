How Nepal's Stock Market Actually Works

Picture the vegetable market (sabzi mandi) near your home early in the morning. Farmers arrive with baskets of tomatoes and greens. Buyers walk around, ask prices, haggle a little, and a deal is struck. Money changes hands, vegetables change hands, and everyone goes home. NEPSE — the Nepal Stock Exchange — is, underneath all the jargon, doing exactly this same basic thing: matching people who want to sell something with people who want to buy it, at a price both sides agree to. The "something" is simply shares of a company instead of tomatoes.

In Part I, we defined a share as a small ownership piece of a company. This chapter explains the actual machinery: who you deal with, what happens behind the scenes when you click "buy," and the safety rules that stop the whole market from spinning out of control on a wild day.

You can't walk into NEPSE yourself — meet the broker

In the vegetable market, you deal directly with the farmer or the stall owner. NEPSE doesn't work quite like that. An ordinary investor cannot walk into NEPSE and place an order directly. Instead, every order has to go through a licensed broker — a company authorized to trade on NEPSE on your behalf.

Think of the broker as a trusted agent (dalal) who stands inside the market for you, because you're not allowed in yourself. You tell the agent what you want to buy or sell and at what price, the agent goes and executes it in the market, and takes a small commission for the service. In practice today, this happens through the broker's own online platform rather than someone physically shouting across a floor.

Before you can trade: the demat account

In the old vegetable market, when you buy tomatoes, you carry them home in a bag. Shares can't be carried home in a bag — they exist only as electronic records. So before you can own a single share, you first need a demat account ("dematerialized" account) — an electronic locker, held with the Central Depository System and Clearing Limited (CDSC), where your shares are recorded and stored in your name.

Most investors manage this demat account through Meroshare, an online portal where you can view your share holdings, apply for new share issues (IPOs), and track your transactions — much like an online banking app, but for shares instead of cash.

Placing an order and getting it matched

Once you have a demat account and a broker account, placing an order works like this: you tell your broker "I want to buy 50 shares of Company X at a maximum price of Y," and your broker enters that order into NEPSE's trading system, called the TMS (Trading Management System).

On the other side, other investors are placing sell orders through their own brokers — "I want to sell my shares of Company X at a minimum price of Z." The TMS constantly looks for a buy order and a sell order that agree on a price, exactly like two people in the mandi finally agreeing on a price for a basket of tomatoes after a bit of back and forth. The moment a buy price and a sell price match, a trade is executed.

Settlement: when the shares and money actually change hands

Here is something that catches many new investors off guard: when your order is matched, the trade is agreed, but the shares don't land in your demat account instantly, and the seller doesn't get their money instantly either. NEPSE currently settles on what's called a T+2 cycle, meaning the actual exchange of shares and money is completed two trading days after the day the trade was agreed (T being the trade day itself).

DayWhat happens
T (trade day)Your buy or sell order is matched and the trade is agreed.
T+1The buyer's payment is arranged through the broker; the seller's shares are set aside for delivery.
T+2 (settlement day)Shares are credited to the buyer's demat account, and money is released to the seller.

It helps to think of this like agreeing on a price for a sack of rice at the mandi, but arranging for it to actually be delivered to your house two days later, once payment has been properly arranged through a middleman both sides trust. Because of this, you cannot resell shares the very same day you bought them — you have to wait for settlement to complete first.

Circuit breakers: the market's safety brakes

Imagine if, in the vegetable market, panic spread that tomatoes were about to become scarce, and every buyer rushed in at once, bidding the price up wildly within minutes. A sensible market would want some kind of brake to stop things from spiralling too fast in a single day. NEPSE has exactly this kind of brake, called a circuit breaker.

Two separate limits apply. First, an individual stock's price is capped from moving more than a fixed percentage in a single day (15%, under rules that took effect in April 2026), so no single company's share price can swing wildly in one session. Second, the entire market has its own circuit breaker based on how much the overall NEPSE index moves: if the index moves 5% within the first two hours of trading, the whole market pauses briefly (15 minutes) to let things settle; if the index moves a full 8% in a day, trading is suspended for the rest of that day entirely.

Rules change over time NEPSE and its regulator periodically revise these exact percentages and timing rules — the figures above reflect the rules in effect as of mid-2026. Always check NEPSE's current trading regulations for the figures that apply on the day you're trading.

The NEPSE index: one number for the whole market

On any given day, some shares go up and others go down. So how does anyone say whether "the market" went up or down overall? This is exactly what the NEPSE index is for. It's a single number, recalculated continuously through the trading day, that summarizes the overall direction of share prices across the whole exchange, with larger companies (by total market value) counting for more in the calculation than smaller ones.

Think of it like a single, market-wide "average vegetable price" figure that a district office might publish at the end of each day — even though tomato prices and potato prices moved differently, one summary number tells you, roughly, whether food prices in general rose or fell that day. The NEPSE index does the same job for shares: individual companies move differently, but the index gives everyone one number to watch.

Chapter recap

  • You cannot trade on NEPSE directly — every order goes through a licensed broker.
  • A demat account (usually viewed through Meroshare) is the electronic locker that holds your shares.
  • Orders are matched by NEPSE's TMS (Trading Management System) when a buyer's and seller's price agree.
  • Trades settle on a T+2 cycle — shares and money actually change hands two trading days after the trade.
  • Circuit breakers cap how far an individual stock (currently 15%/day) or the whole index (5% and 8% thresholds) can move in a single day.
  • The NEPSE index is one number that summarizes the direction of the whole market, weighted toward larger companies.
