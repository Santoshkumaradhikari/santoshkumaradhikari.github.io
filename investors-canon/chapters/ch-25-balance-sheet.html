WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Balance sheet — a snapshot of what a company owns (assets) and owes (liabilities) on one day, like a family listing its land, gold and loans. Equity — what is left for owners after all debts: assets minus liabilities. Reserves — past profits kept in the company rather than paid out. Receivables — money customers owe the company, such as NEA's unpaid bills to a hydropower firm. Contingent liability — a possible future debt, such as a guarantee or a tax case under appeal. Other comprehensive income — gains and losses (often on investments) that go straight to equity without passing through profit.

Most Nepali investors read the profit and loss account first, because it tells a story: revenue grew, profit rose. The balance sheet sits at the back of the annual report, dense and dull. That instinct is backwards.

The income statement tells you what happened over a period. The balance sheet tells you what the company actually is on one day — what it owns, what it owes, and who gets paid first. Some examples of what only the balance sheet shows:

  • A hydropower company reports a profitable quarter, but its balance sheet shows that money owed by the Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA) has stretched to eight months, and day-to-day spending is funded by short-term loans at 13 percent (a rate from the tight-money year of 2023).
  • A microfinance company reports double-digit loan growth, but its provisions for bad loans have not kept up with its worsening loan book.
  • A trading company shows rising profit, but its "other assets" line — the graveyard of Nepali balance sheets — swells every year with money owed by related companies that will never be collected.

In each case the profit and loss account misleads by leaving things out. The balance sheet, read carefully, tells the truth.

Three Nepali complications

  1. Different formats for financial companies. About half of NEPSE's tradable value sits in banks, development banks, finance companies, microfinance institutions and insurers. Their balance sheets use a format set by Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB) or the Insurance Authority, ordered by liquidity, not the "current / non-current" layout taught in commerce colleges.
  2. NFRS changes. The move to Nepal Financial Reporting Standards brought revaluation reserves, actuarial gains and fair-value adjustments into equity — easy to mistake for real growth.
  3. Ownership. Promoter and public shares, cross-holdings between group companies, and related-party deals routed through sister companies create risks less common in markets with spread-out ownership.

Lesson 25.1 — Why the Balance Sheet Is the Senior Document

Who gets paid first

Every company is a set of claims:

  • Assets — what the company controls.
  • Liabilities — what it owes to lenders, depositors and policyholders. They are paid first, and in full, however the business does.
  • Equity — what is left for shareholders after everyone else is paid.

Think of a family selling its house to move. First the bank loan is repaid; only what remains belongs to the family. That order is why a bank earning 18 percent on equity can still be a poor investment if its capital is sliding towards the legal minimum — and why a factory with flat profit but little debt can beat a fast-growing rival loaded with loans.

KEY CONCEPT The balance sheet answers one question the profit and loss account cannot: if the company stopped today, who would be paid in what order, and would anything be left for you? Assets minus liabilities equals equity — but equity is only as good as the assets behind it.

Leverage makes it matter more

NEPSE's biggest sector is banks and financial institutions (BFIs). Every one of them is, by design, financed ten to twelve times over by borrowed money. A commercial bank with Rs 15 arba of capital and reserves typically has a balance sheet of Rs 150–200 arba, funded mostly by depositors.

That is the business model, not a warning sign. But it means a small fall in loan quality eats equity many times faster than it would in an unborrowed business. If loans worth eleven times equity lose 2 percent, a large slice of the shareholders' money is gone.

That is why NRB watches bank balance sheets far more closely than the Company Registrar watches a factory's, and why your first check on any bank should be its capital and loan classification — before its P/E.

Profit is easier to dress up

A hydropower company can count sales to the NEA as revenue while the cash sits unpaid for months, because the NEA itself is short of cash. The income statement shows growing profit. The balance sheet shows receivables (money owed to the company) growing faster than revenue — a company lending its own customer money instead of being paid.

Profit without cash is a balance sheet problem in an income statement disguise. Only reading both together catches it.

PRACTICAL TOOL Each time you read a NEPSE company's profit and loss, turn at once to the balance sheet. Compare the growth of trade receivables (for a bank, loans and advances) with the growth of revenue (for a bank, interest income). If receivables are growing much faster, more and more of the reported profit is a promise, not cash.

Lesson 25.2 — The Anatomy of a NEPSE Balance Sheet

The standard layout

For non-financial companies — manufacturers, hotels, traders and most hydropower developers — the balance sheet follows the standard NFRS format:

  • Non-current assets: property, plant and equipment; intangible assets; long-term investments.
  • Current assets: inventory, trade receivables, cash, short-term investments.
  • Equity: share capital, reserves and surplus, retained earnings.
  • Non-current liabilities: long-term loans, deferred tax liabilities, staff benefit obligations.
  • Current liabilities: short-term loans, trade payables, the part of long-term debt due this year, provisions.

Ask how each block changed

For each block, ask not only "how big?" but "how has it changed?"

  • Plant and equipment growing with revenue is normal. Growing while revenue stalls means over-investment, and years of depreciation will squeeze margins.
  • Inventory growing faster than sales is either a bet on future demand or goods that will not sell. The inventory-ageing note, if given, tells you which.
  • Trade receivables, as Lesson 25.1 showed, best separate accounting profit from cash profit.

The shape of the debt

The make-up of debt matters as much as its total. Funding a long-lived asset — a power plant or factory — with short-term loans is a maturity mismatch. It turns into a crisis the moment banks tighten. Several Nepali hydropower developers found this out when they covered construction overruns with short-term working-capital loans instead of long-term loans matched to the plant's life.

It is like building a house with money borrowed for three months: fine until the lender wants it back before the house can earn rent. By contrast, long-term debt against long-lived, cash-earning assets, with cash comfortably covering repayments, is leverage used properly.

WATCH FOR A rising share of short-term borrowing in total borrowing is one of the more reliable early warnings for a Nepali industrial or hydropower company. It usually means long-term credit was unavailable or too costly, or that working-capital lines are funding what should be long-term investment. Both raise refinancing risk just when the business can least absorb a shock.

The equity block

For a non-financial company, equity is made of:

  • paid-up share capital — the face value of shares issued, usually Rs 100 each on NEPSE (unrelated to today's market price);
  • share premium — money received above face value, usually from rights issues or FPOs;
  • reserves — general reserve, retained earnings and, importantly, revaluation reserve (Lesson 25.4);
  • minority interest, for the few companies whose subsidiaries are not wholly owned.

Lesson 25.3 — Reading a Bank's Balance Sheet: The NRB Format and the Loan Book

Ordered by liquidity

Banks, development banks, finance companies and microfinance institutions use a format set by NRB. It is ordered by liquidity — most easily turned into cash first — because a regulator's first question about a bank is "can it meet withdrawals?", not "is this asset current or fixed?"

The loan book is everything

The most important number on a bank's balance sheet — and the one most retail investors skip — is the quality of "loans and advances to customers". More than profit, EPS or dividends, it decides whether the bank is sound in five years' time.

NRB requires every BFI to sort all its loans into risk classes every quarter and set aside a minimum provision for each — like a farmer keeping back more grain for each field that looks likely to fail:

Loan classBasisMinimum provision
Pass (Good)Principal and interest not overdue, or overdue up to 1 month1%
WatchlistOverdue 1–3 months, or performing loans with early warning signs (restructured, in court, or to a borrower with other classified loans)5%
SubstandardPrincipal or interest overdue 3–6 months25%
DoubtfulPrincipal or interest overdue 6–12 months50%
LossOverdue more than 12 months, or otherwise unrecoverable100%

The 1 percent pass-loan provision is the rate in force since March 2025. It has moved several times: NRB had raised it to 1.25 percent, then cut it in steps — to 1.20 percent, to 1.10 percent in the 2024/25 monetary policy, and back to 1.0 percent by March 2025 (NRB Monetary Policy 2025/26). Check the current Unified Directive before relying on any one figure.

Substandard, doubtful and loss loans together are non-performing loans (NPLs). Watchlist loans are not yet non-performing, but they deserve the closest watch: a loan passes through watchlist before it becomes a statistic. A watchlist ratio rising quarter after quarter often signals an NPL problem before it shows in the headline ratio.

REGULATORY DETAIL NRB's Unified Directives require provisions to be calculated on these classes and disclosed in the notes, with the NPL ratio and capital position. Since NFRS 9, banks also estimate an expected credit loss (ECL) provision using forward-looking models. Where that ECL figure is lower than NRB's, the difference must be held as a regulatory reserve, not released to distributable profit — so a bank cannot use a gentler accounting model to free up more money for dividends.

Why under-provisioning catches up

A bank's profit is only as reliable as its provisions. A bank that under-provides — by restructuring loans that should be downgraded, calling a loan "watchlist" when it is really "substandard", or relying on stale collateral values — shows higher profit today and a bigger, sudden provision later, when an NRB inspection or a rule change forces reclassification. Several NRB-ordered reclassifications have done exactly this, reminding the market that reported profit and real loan quality can differ.

Capital: the second pillar

Under NRB's Basel III-based framework, commercial banks must hold minimum levels of core equity (CET1), Tier 1 capital and total capital against risk-weighted assets, plus a capital conservation buffer on top. This brings the total most commercial banks must hold to about 11 percent of risk-weighted assets, with extra for the largest banks and any countercyclical buffer NRB switches on.

A bank close to that floor has little room to absorb a bad year of loan losses before it must issue rights shares, slow its lending, or — under stress — face limits on dividends and expansion.

CASE IN POINT Nepali commercial banks publish a separate quarterly "Capital Adequacy" (Basel) disclosure that many retail investors never open. It shows the CET1 ratio, Tier 1 ratio, total capital ratio and risk-weighted assets. Two banks can report almost the same profit and EPS growth while one has several points of spare capital and the other sits near the floor. The second bank's ability to keep growing loans — and future profit — is far more limited, and the income statement alone will not show it.

Finance companies, microfinance and insurers

Finance companies and microfinance institutions face the same logic with sharper edges: thinner capital, riskier borrowers, and bad-loan ratios that have jumped when interest rates rose. For a microfinance institution, look at loans by sector (agriculture, small trade, group lending), the watchlist-plus-NPL ratio over at least eight quarters, and whether provisions have kept pace with loan growth.

Insurers use yet another format, set by the Nepal Insurance Authority. Their liabilities are dominated by policy reserves — the actuary's estimate of future claims and benefits owed. Their assets are investments meant to match those promises: government securities, bank fixed deposits and shares (capped by regulation). Ask whether the solvency margin is comfortable, and whether heavy exposure to weaker banks' deposits or to shares would turn a banking downturn into a double blow for the insurer.

Lesson 25.4 — The Equity Side: Promoters, the Public, and the Reserves That Lie

Promoter and public shares

NEPSE companies show shareholding in two groups: promoter and public. This split is one of the most useful single facts available to a small investor, because it shows how the people who know the company best have positioned themselves.

Promoter shares are locked for a period after listing, so promoters cannot leave just after the IPO. Because of the lock-in and their thin market, promoter shares usually trade at a discount to public shares of the same company.

Watch promoter share pledging — promoters using their shares as security for loans, visible in CDSC and company filings. It means promoters are drawing cash from their stake without reducing it on paper. If the lender sells the pledged shares in a falling market, the price can drop sharply — like gold pawned at the bank, sold off when the loan goes bad.

WATCH FOR A steady fall in promoter shareholding over several quarters — through open-market sales, not dilution from a rights issue or FPO — is one of the more reliable behavioural signals on NEPSE. Promoters know their business better than any outsider. Persistent selling into a rising or flat price deserves more weight than almost any single ratio.

Three reserves to read carefully

1. Revaluation reserve. Under NFRS, a company may revalue its property — usually land — to today's value instead of its old cost. The increase goes straight to a revaluation reserve in equity, not to profit.

This is legitimate, and often reflects reality: land bought decades ago in Kathmandu Valley or the Tarai may be worth many times its book cost. The danger is what it does to the ratios. It inflates equity, flatters debt-to-equity and book value per share, and lowers return on equity — without any change in the cash the business earns, and without the land being sold. A company under pressure has a reason to revalue, because it lifts the equity base that loan covenants and capital ratios are measured against, without raising a rupee of new money.

WARNING When comparing return on equity, debt-to-equity or book value per share — between companies or over time — check the notes for a revaluation reserve and the date of the last revaluation. If equity grew mainly through revaluation rather than retained profit, the balance sheet looks stronger than the business. Remove the revaluation reserve and recalculate the ratios on cost-based equity before deciding.

2. Retained earnings and general reserve. Company law and, for BFIs, NRB rules require part of each year's profit to go to a general reserve until it reaches a set multiple of paid-up capital. Only the rest is available for dividends. A large general reserve is often a sign of prudence. But to judge cash dividend capacity, look at free, distributable retained earnings and the cash behind them — not the combined "reserves and surplus", which may be built from statutory transfers and bonus capitalisation with little that is both distributable and backed by cash.

3. Deferred tax and actuarial items. NFRS requires deferred tax assets and liabilities, and actuarial gains and losses on staff gratuity and pensions, often recorded through "other comprehensive income". These are legitimate. But a deferred tax asset is only worth something if the company earns enough future taxable profit to use it. A growing deferred tax asset in a company with a history of losses is a promise, not cash.

CAUTION "Other comprehensive income" and its running total in equity is one of the least-read parts of a NEPSE annual report. It usually holds actuarial gains and losses on staff benefits and, for some companies, changes in the value of investments. These skip the profit and loss account, so the company's full result for the year can differ noticeably from the "profit for the year" used for EPS.

Lesson 25.5 — Sector Variations: Hydropower, Insurance, and Manufacturing

Hydropower: from building site to power plant

Hydropower is NEPSE's most capital-hungry sector. During construction, the balance sheet is dominated by capital work in progress — an asset that earns nothing yet — financed by project loans at a debt-to-equity ratio agreed with the lending banks, commonly 70:30 or 80:20 for run-of-river projects.

During this phase the question is not profit (there is none) but whether the project is on time and on budget. A cost overrun forces more promoter equity, more debt, or delay — and all three show on the balance sheet before any investor announcement.

Once the plant starts operating, work in progress becomes fixed assets and depreciation begins. Receivables from the NEA appear. For run-of-river plants these swing with the seasons — high in the monsoon, lower in the dry season — and across the sector they have at times stretched to several months of sales. It is an easy drag on cash to miss if you look only at revenue and profit.

CASE IN POINT A hydropower company's notes usually show receivables ageing — how much of the NEA's dues are 0–3 months old, 3–6 months old, and older. A rising share in the older groups, even with growing revenue, means the company is lending more and more to its only customer. It cannot spread that risk, because the NEA is the required single buyer for most licensed generation in Nepal.

Manufacturing and trading: related parties and off-balance-sheet risks

Cement, consumer goods and diversified groups use the standard layout, with two Nepali wrinkles.

First, related-party balances. Many such companies belong to larger unlisted business groups. Their balance sheets often show money owed by or to sister companies, holding companies or firms with the same promoters. A related-party receivable that grows every year, is never settled in cash and carries no commercial interest is, in effect, an interest-free loan from public shareholders to the promoters' other businesses, dressed up as an "other receivable".

Second, guarantees and letters of credit. Many companies use letters of credit and bank guarantees to import raw materials or bid for contracts. These do not appear as liabilities; they sit in the contingent liabilities note in small print, described as obligations "not provided for".

WARNING Contingent liabilities — bank guarantees given for the company, open letters of credit, disputed tax demands under appeal, and guarantees for subsidiaries' or related parties' debts — are real obligations. They are left off the balance sheet because the payment is uncertain, not because they are small. For a company guaranteeing sister companies' loans, this note can reveal a risk bigger than all its recorded liabilities. Read it in full, and treat large or fast-growing guarantees to related parties as extra debt.

Insurers: the actuary's assumptions

An insurer's policy reserves are estimates, not observed facts. Their quality depends on the caution of the actuary's assumptions — mortality tables, how many policies lapse, the discount rate on future claims. A small investor cannot check these, but the appointed actuary signs them and the actuarial note summarises them. A sudden favourable change in assumptions that lifts net worth, with no change in the business, is a reason to read that note closely rather than trust the improved solvency figure.

Lesson 25.6 — Building the Red-Flag Checklist

These lessons point to six recurring Nepali balance-sheet warning signs. Gathering them into one checklist gives you a habit to apply to every company, instead of relying on memory.

  1. Related-party receivables. The most Nepal-specific flag, because promoter families and business groups control several listed and unlisted companies. In the related-party note, check whether related-party receivables grow faster than total receivables, whether they carry commercial interest, and whether the same related parties appear as both debtors and creditors across several group companies — a sign of money going round in circles rather than real trade.
  2. Revaluation reserves inflating equity. Recalculate ROE, debt-to-equity and price-to-book on equity without the revaluation reserve. A big difference shows how much apparent strength comes from an accounting choice.
  3. Contingent liabilities. Guarantees and letters of credit, especially for related parties, are left out of reported debt-to-equity. If they are a large multiple of net worth, the real risk is understated.
  4. Loan classification drift (BFIs). A rising watchlist ratio, more restructured or rescheduled loans, or provisions growing slower than the loan book all point to bad loans that will appear later.
  5. Short-term debt against long-lived assets. Mainly for hydropower and manufacturing. Check the maturity of debt in the notes, not just the debt-to-equity ratio.
  6. Falling promoter holdings. The easiest to track: CDSC and NEPSE shareholding reports show it every quarter. Check it routinely for every holding.
PRACTICAL TOOL Before buying, apply this checklist to every NEPSE balance sheet: (1) receivables growth against revenue growth; (2) revaluation reserve as a share of equity; (3) contingent liabilities as a multiple of net worth; (4) for BFIs, the watchlist-plus-NPL trend over eight quarters and the capital buffer above the minimum; (5) short-term borrowing as a share of total borrowing; (6) the promoter shareholding trend over four quarters. All six need only the annual report, quarterly reports and shareholding filings — all public.

None of this is a reason to avoid Nepali banks or hydropower companies. Leverage, related-party structures and regulator formats are simply the ground you walk on. Many NEPSE companies have clean, well-provided, conservatively financed balance sheets. Reading the balance sheet closely is how you tell them apart from fragile peers — long before the difference shows in profit or price. It is slower to read than the profit and loss account, but it tells you plainly what you own a claim on when you buy a share.

Chapter recap

  • The balance sheet shows what a company is on one day and who gets paid first: liabilities before equity.
  • Banks are financed ten to twelve times over by deposits, so small loan losses eat equity fast. Check capital and loan classification before P/E.
  • Compare receivables growth with revenue growth to see whether profit is arriving as cash.
  • Watch the shape of debt: short-term loans funding long-lived assets create refinancing risk.
  • NRB sets a liquidity-ordered bank format and five loan classes with minimum provisions (Pass 1% since March 2025, Watchlist 5%, Substandard 25%, Doubtful 50%, Loss 100%). A rising watchlist ratio is an early warning.
  • Banks must hold about 11% total capital including the conservation buffer; read the quarterly Basel disclosure.
  • Read equity carefully: falling promoter holdings and pledging, revaluation reserves, distributable versus total reserves, deferred tax and other comprehensive income.
  • By sector: hydropower work in progress and NEA receivables ageing; related-party balances and contingent liabilities in groups; actuarial assumptions for insurers.
  • Use the six-point red-flag checklist before every purchase.
