WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Red flag — a warning pattern that makes trouble more likely, not proof of fraud — like dark clouds before rain. Evergreening — giving a struggling borrower a new loan to repay the old one, so a bad loan looks healthy. Related party — a person or company linked to the owners or directors. Qualified opinion — an audit opinion saying the accounts are fair except for a named problem. Restructured loan — a loan whose terms were changed, often because the borrower could not pay. Provision coverage — how much a bank has set aside against its bad loans.

A balance sheet is a photograph, not an X-ray. It shows where assets and liabilities sit on one day. It does not show whether a loan marked "standard" is actually being repaid, whether "other income" hides a one-off gain dressed as regular profit, or whether the promoter family has quietly moved working capital to a sister company that never appears in the notes.

The earlier chapters taught you to read financial statements. This chapter teaches something more urgent in a market as young and promoter-dominated as Nepal's: how to read what a statement is trying not to say.

Looking for red flags is not cynicism. In every market — and especially one where the securities regulator is still building its enforcement strength and the central bank is managing a structurally weak banking sector — some reported numbers are managed, smoothed or simply false. Your first defence is pattern recognition, not trust.

Nepal's weak points are specific:

  • promoter families often sit on both sides of a loan — as owners of the bank and owners of the borrower;
  • tradable shares are so few that a handful of insiders can move a price without the business changing;
  • the largest audit firms are small next to the balance sheets they sign;
  • the regulators — SEBON for the market and Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB) for banks and financial institutions (BFIs) — have themselves admitted gaps between what companies report and what happens in their loan books and boardrooms.

Lesson 28.1 — Why Nepal's Institutional Setting Makes Red Flags More Likely, Not Less

Disclosure quality usually improves with stronger regulators, independent auditors and deeper markets. Nepal is at an early stage on all three at once, and the weaknesses multiply rather than simply add up. There are four reasons.

1. Ownership

Many NEPSE companies — and nearly all Class A commercial banks, Class B development banks and Class C finance companies — were founded and are still largely controlled by a promoter group, often one family or a tight circle of partners. They usually keep the minimum promoter holding required by NRB and SEBON, and the rest trades publicly.

That is common in South Asia. But in Nepal the same group often also owns private companies in trading, real estate, hotels and manufacturing that deal with the listed company all the time — as borrowers, suppliers, landlords or guarantors. NFRS requires related-party disclosure, but the capacity to catch an incomplete or softened note is thin compared with the number of deals it must police.

2. Thin markets

In most NEPSE shares, the free float — shares actually available to trade — is a small fraction of the total, and daily volume is so thin that a few dozen coordinated traders can move a share 5 to 10 percent in a day.

A 2021–22 SEBON review identified roughly fifty listed companies whose price movements it considered out of line with fundamentals and irregular in trading pattern. That says less about any one company's honesty than about how easily thin float and coordinated buying can create the look of business momentum. A rising price is not proof that the numbers are honest. In a shallow market it can be the opposite — a smokescreen that discourages scrutiny.

3. Audit capacity

Nepal has many audit firms registered with ICAN, but few with the size, banking and insurance experience, and bargaining power to resist a client. A finance company or mid-sized development bank with billions of rupees in loans may be audited by a two- or three-partner firm for whom that one fee is a large share of yearly income.

Theory predicts — and common sense agrees — that such dependence weakens an auditor's willingness to challenge management's loan classification or provisions. Think of a village health worker asked to certify the health of the household that pays most of her income. This is not an accusation against any firm. It is a description of the market, and it should raise your starting level of scepticism before you open a report.

KEY CONCEPT A red flag is not proof of fraud. It is a probability signal — a pattern that, across many companies and years, goes with more misstatement, aggressive accounting or concealment. Your job is not to prosecute a case. It is to raise your required margin of safety, ask for more disclosure, or walk away when enough flags gather on one share.

4. Gaps between regulators

Savings and credit cooperatives are not supervised by NRB the way banks are. They sat in a gap between the Department of Cooperatives, provincial authorities and NRB, and produced some of Nepal's most damaging collapses. Cooperatives are not listed on NEPSE. But the same pattern — owners capturing the institution, lending to themselves, and hiding it — appears in milder, more regulated form inside licensed BFIs and listed non-bank companies. The cooperative story is the clearest, least disguised version of a disease found more mildly across the listed market.

Lesson 28.2 — Evergreening: How a Bad Loan Learns to Look Good

For anyone holding shares in a NEPSE bank, development bank or finance company, the most important red flag is evergreening: keeping a bad loan looking "performing" by methods that bring in no real repayment. It matters more in Nepal because banks are a big part of NEPSE's value and trading, the rules leave room for interpretation on restructured loans, and NRB's own reviews have described the practice as widespread rather than rare.

How it works

A borrower's business weakens and interest payments stop. Classing the loan as non-performing would force the bank to move it to "substandard", "doubtful" or "loss", set aside much more in provisions, and take a hit to profit under NFRS 9.

Instead, the bank makes a new loan — often to a related company, or as a fresh "working capital" facility — and the money is used to pay the interest on the old one. On paper, interest has been "received", the old loan stays standard or watchlist, and the bad-loan ratio, capital ratio and profit all look healthier. In reality the debt was paid from a new loan, and the bank's exposure to that borrower has grown.

It is like a cousin who borrows from you to repay what he already owes you. Your notebook shows the old loan repaid; your money is still gone, and now he owes you more.

Interest capitalisation

A close cousin of evergreening is interest capitalisation: unpaid interest is simply added to the loan balance. The loan grows although no new money was lent or repaid, and the bank's assets now include interest that was already counted as profit and may never be collected.

NRB's rules on recognising interest income exist because this became common enough across banks to need a system-wide rule. A 2019 guideline came first. In July 2025 NRB replaced its rule-based tests with a Guidance Note on Interest Income Recognition that ties interest to each loan's NFRS 9 credit-loss stage and makes banks track, account by account and quarter by quarter, interest booked, interest actually received and interest held in suspense. On non-performing (Stage 3) loans, only interest received in cash counts. A regulator does not build that much record-keeping unless the old, looser tests had left room to flatter profit.

REGULATORY DETAIL Under the NFRS carve-out applied by ICAN and NRB, a BFI must hold the higher of (a) its NFRS 9 expected-credit-loss figure and (b) the provision required by NRB's loan-classification directive. The carve-out exists because a bank's own NFRS 9 model was judged too easy to push down with hopeful assumptions about recoveries and collateral. In the notes, check which basis produced the reported provision. If it is consistently the NFRS 9 figure rather than the NRB floor, ask why the bank's model produces less than the regulator's rule of thumb.

The regulator's own evidence

The clearest evidence that evergreening is real came from NRB. In a loan portfolio review approved for roughly ten major commercial banks, NRB's on-site teams reportedly found recurring "malpractices" in loan recovery and credit-risk reporting, including credit risk under-reported compared with what banks had disclosed.

That matters. The regulator with inspection powers no outside analyst has concluded that reported bad-loan figures across a meaningful part of the sector understated the true risk. So if you work only from published statements, assume a bank's true loan quality is probably worse than its headline NPL ratio. Treat that ratio as a floor, not an estimate.

Four linked indicators

No single ratio settles the question. Look at these together:

  1. Loan growth against funding. Loans growing much faster than deposits and net interest income — especially if concentrated in a few large borrower groups — are more dependent on roll-overs.
  2. Restructured loans. A rising share of "restructured and rescheduled loans" (an NRB-required disclosure) without a rising NPL ratio is almost a textbook evergreening signature: stress is being moved into a bucket that avoids NPL status.
  3. Uncollected interest. Interest receivable or interest suspense rising as a share of interest income means income exists on paper but not in cash.
  4. Provision coverage. Provisions held against bad loans, as a ratio, falling over several years while loans grow suggests the bank is not keeping up with real credit risk, even if the NPL ratio looks steady.
WATCH FOR A bank whose NPL ratio is flat or improving, while its provision coverage falls and its note on loans to related parties or group companies is silent or tiny, is showing the classic signature of an evergreened loan book: stress hidden by reclassification and restructuring, not resolved by recovery or write-off.

Lesson 28.3 — Related-Party Transactions and the Promoter Web

Where the stress starts

If evergreening is how a bank hides stress, related-party transactions (RPTs) are often where the stress starts — and how value leaks from a listed company to its promoters.

Under NFRS, parties are related when one controls, is controlled by, or shares control with another. In Nepal that usually means the promoter family, the companies they own separately, directors and their close relatives, and any entity where a director or key manager has significant influence. The disclosure rule is not the problem. The problem is the gap between the rule and its enforcement, and the ways promoter groups keep deals technically compliant but economically murky.

How it shows up by sector

  • Banks and finance companies: promoter-linked companies appear as large borrowers. NRB's single-obligor and group-lending limits are meant to cap this. But a determined group can borrow through several companies that are related in substance yet not disclosed as one group — "borrower splitting". It defeats the limits and hides the true concentration from anyone reading only the published accounts.
  • Manufacturing, trading and hotels: sales to or purchases from a promoter-owned private company at prices that are not clearly fair, or advances to a sister company labelled as trade receivables rather than related-party loans — which keeps them out of the closely read RPT note and buries them in an ordinary receivable balance.

The cooperative warning

The cooperative sector, though outside SEBON's listed market, is Nepal's starkest example of related-party lending left unchecked inside a weakly supervised deposit-taker. Several of the largest collapses hit depositors and shareholders, and one high-profile case became a political controversy involving a serving deputy prime minister — showing how deeply promoter-linked lending can become entangled with governance failure at the top.

Investigations found that cooperatives such as Oriental Cooperative and similar savings institutions had channelled deposits into loans to promoters, directors and their companies far beyond any prudent limit. A 2024 parliamentary probe reported about 40 cooperatives and roughly Rs 87 arba embezzled (Chapter 19). Ordinary households, who had treated cooperative deposits as safe as a bank, learned years later that much of the money had financed the promoters' own businesses, land speculation and, in some cases, personal spending.

CASE IN POINT The cooperative collapse of the mid-2020s is Nepal's clearest case study of unchecked related-party lending: borrowing linked to promoters and directors, concentrated in a few institutions and largely outside NRB's direct supervision, produced depositor losses in the tens of billions of rupees before regulators and prosecutors caught up. Licensed BFIs are far more tightly supervised. But the underlying temptation — promoters treating depositors' or shareholders' money as a captive source of funding for their own ventures — is the same disease in a more regulated body. Read every related-party note in a NEPSE bank's annual report with that history in mind.

Checking a non-bank company

Trace every named related party in the notes against the shareholding pattern filed with SEBON and against public company-registration records. Look for:

  • deals whose pricing or terms are not disclosed — a note that names the counterparty but not the value or pricing basis is itself a red flag;
  • receivables from, or advances to, related parties growing faster than total receivables;
  • guarantees given by the listed company for a promoter's private company — a contingent liability that may be off the balance sheet but should appear in the contingent-liabilities note;
  • related-party flows that change direction over time — a promoter company that was a customer one year becomes a major creditor later, often a sign that cash is being cycled around the group to hide a liquidity problem.
WARNING A related-party note that shows that deals exist but not their pricing, terms or how recoverable related-party receivables are gives you the minimum NFRS disclosure and nothing more. Minimum compliance is not transparency. A note that reads like boilerplate year after year, with round numbers and no ageing or recoverability comment, is a disclosure gap, not a clean bill of health.

Lesson 28.4 — Reading the Auditor's Signature: Opinions, Qualifications, and Turnover

The audit opinion is the densest piece of information in an annual report, and the most skipped, because its wording is formulaic. That formula is the point: any departure from the standard clean wording is a deliberate signal that the auditor — bound by ICAN's code and, for listed companies, within SEBON's oversight — found something serious enough to refuse a clean certificate. Watch four things: the type of opinion, the wording of any qualification or emphasis, how often auditors change, and the size of the audit firm relative to the client.

Qualified, adverse, disclaimer and emphasis of matter

  • A qualified opinion: the statements are fair except for a named matter.
  • An adverse opinion: the statements, taken as a whole, are not fair.
  • A disclaimer: the auditor could not get enough evidence to form any opinion — the most serious, because the auditor cannot vouch for the numbers at all.

In Nepal's insurance and finance-company sectors, qualifications and disclaimers have recurred around a few themes: whether general insurers' claims reserves are adequate, whether finance companies' and development banks' concentrated loans are recoverable and properly classed, and whether related-party disclosure is complete.

An emphasis of matter paragraph is softer. The auditor does not qualify the opinion but points you to a disclosure — often doubt about the company continuing as a going concern, a pending lawsuit, or a major later event. On its own it does not weaken the opinion. But the same emphasis repeated year after year — on litigation with a regulator, or on recovery of one large loan — is the auditor's polite way of saying the problem has not been solved.

Frequent auditor changes

Banks and financial institutions must rotate auditors (no more than three consecutive appointments under the Bank and Financial Institution Act), and the Insurance Authority sets rules for insurers. Ordinary listed companies face no hard limit. Long tenures are known to wear down independence.

Rotation itself can be a signal either way. A change exactly on schedule, with an orderly handover, is normal. A change mid-cycle, outside the required rotation — especially just after a qualified opinion or a public regulatory inquiry — is one of the strongest red flags in forensic accounting: opinion shopping, where management replaces an auditor who will not sign off with one who will.

The tell is the timing and the stated reason. "Due to expiry of tenure", in line with the legal cycle, is unremarkable. "By mutual agreement", or no reason at all, right after a qualified or adverse opinion, deserves a close look at what the departing auditor flagged.

PRACTICAL TOOL Before buying, build an auditor-history table for the company: audit firm, fiscal year, opinion type (clean, qualified, adverse, disclaimer) and any emphasis-of-matter theme, for at least the last five to seven years, from annual reports filed with SEBON and AGM disclosures. Changes that do not match the legal rotation cycle, or a qualification that reappears under a new firm in softer words for the same issue, tell you more about management's attitude to scrutiny than almost any balance-sheet ratio.

Firm size against client size

Many Nepali audit firms are small compared with the balance sheets they sign. A commercial bank with hundreds of billions of rupees in assets, hundreds of branches and loans across dozens of sectors needs an audit team with deep banking expertise, sound sampling methods for testing loan files, and the weight to resist management on a disputed provision.

When the same firm, or a small rotating group, appears across much of the listed BFI sector year after year, it is fair to ask whether its capacity matches the client's complexity. A two-partner firm auditing a bank with over a thousand large corporate loan accounts can only test so many files within a normal fee and timeline, however diligent it is.

CAUTION Do not treat a clean opinion as a guarantee. It means the auditor did not find — or was not shown — evidence that required a qualification. It does not promise that no problem exists, especially where audit capacity is stretched or the auditor's judgement on a debatable estimate such as loan-loss provisions was more cautious in form than in substance.

Lesson 28.5 — Case Studies: When the Numbers Finally Broke

Regulators, not markets, caught the problems

Pattern recognition improves with real examples. Nepal's last two decades supply several — in cooperatives, microfinance and the banks themselves.

None is the kind of headline "restated annual report" scandal seen in larger markets. In Nepal, problems have usually been caught by regulators — NRB special supervision, SEBON surveillance, criminal investigation — not by companies correcting their own accounts. That is a lesson in itself: the market has not been the first line of defence. By the time a regulatory action becomes public, prices and depositor confidence have often already collapsed.

Cooperatives

The largest episode. A cluster of savings and credit cooperatives — including Oriental Cooperative and similar institutions named in investigations — had depositor liabilities far beyond any prudent loan book, with much of the money channelled to promoters, directors and their companies. Investigation and parliamentary scrutiny reached senior political figures. The absence of a single prudential supervisor allowed the lending to compound for years. The lesson: Nepal's supervisory net has had holes, and concentrated related-party lending funded by public deposits can run for years before anyone stops it.

Microfinance

Microfinance shows a related but different pattern: stress driven less by theft and more by aggressive growth and over-borrowing households, hidden for a while by continued portfolio growth and refinancing of stressed micro-loans — a village-level echo of evergreening.

Sanjeevani Microfinance's well-documented path from acute distress towards a managed recovery shows both the risk and the eventual response. NRB's Microfinance Institutions Supervision Department has since taken direct action against a number of microfinance institutions for breaking rules. Wider reporting describes systemic over-indebtedness, with borrowers owing several lenders at once — a risk invisible in any one lender's accounts and visible only in sector-wide data.

CASE IN POINT NRB's action against several microfinance institutions, after years of concern about over-indebted borrowers and aggressive growth, shows a different kind of red-flag problem: not necessarily fraud in one company's books, but a system-wide weakness — the same borrower owing several lenders — that no single company's statements could reveal. For any Nepali lender, ask not only "are these numbers honest?" but "does this disclosure even capture the risk that matters?"

The banks' own review

Within licensed banking, the clearest evidence is NRB's approved loan portfolio review of roughly ten major commercial banks, reported to have found recurring malpractice in loan recovery and gaps between disclosed and actual credit risk. Because the sector's own prudential regulator conducted it, with legal inspection powers, it outweighs any outside ratio analysis. It is close to direct confirmation that reported loan quality across a meaningful part of the sector differed from reality.

SEBON's surveillance

SEBON's market surveillance has at times publicly named batches of NEPSE companies whose prices it judged detached from fundamentals and whose trading it flagged as irregular — roughly fifty in one review. That is a market-integrity flag rather than a misstated balance sheet. But the two connect: a company whose price was pushed up by coordinated trading gives insiders a strong reason to want good-looking accounts, because a fall driven by poor results would expose the earlier price as unsupported. If a share appears on a SEBON irregular-trading list, scrutinise its financial statements harder.

Lesson 28.6 — A Practical Red-Flag Checklist for the Nepali Investor

Everything in this chapter leads to a checklist you can run, company by company, before investing. No single item is reason enough to avoid a share, and a company that passes every item is not guaranteed clean. Red-flag analysis raises or lowers your estimate of the risk and the margin of safety you need. It does not replace valuation. But working through a list like this for every bank or company you seriously consider will catch many of the problems in this chapter before they appear as a price collapse or a regulatory action.

Treat a "High" flag as a reason to demand better answers before investing, not as proof of wrongdoing.

Two final habits

  1. Read several years together. Nearly every red flag here is a trend. One annual report rarely shows evergreening, related-party drift or opinion shopping; five to seven years side by side often do.
  2. Treat regulatory silence as information, not proof. No NRB or SEBON action against a company does not mean its numbers are clean, given the capacity limits described here. It only means nothing has become public yet. Your own checklist is the quicker and more reliable defence.

Chapter recap

  • A red flag is a probability signal, not proof of fraud. Use it to raise your margin of safety, ask for more, or walk away.
  • Nepal's setting makes red flags more likely: promoter-controlled groups, thin float, small audit firms and gaps between regulators (most clearly in cooperatives).
  • Evergreening and interest capitalisation keep bad loans looking good. NRB's own review of about ten major banks found under-reported credit risk, so treat reported NPLs as a floor. Watch restructured loans, uncollected interest and falling provision coverage.
  • Related-party transactions are where stress starts and value leaks: borrower splitting in banks, non-arm's-length sales and disguised advances elsewhere. The cooperative collapse is the unrestrained version.
  • Read the auditor's signature: opinion type, repeated emphasis of matter, auditor changes outside the rotation cycle (opinion shopping), and firm size against client size. A clean opinion is not a guarantee.
  • In Nepal, regulators rather than markets have usually exposed problems — often after prices had already collapsed.
  • Run the ten-point checklist on several years of reports for every company you seriously consider.
