WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Cash flow statement — a record of actual cash coming in and going out, like a household's cash book rather than its promises. Operating cash flow (CFO) — cash from the main business. Investing cash flow — cash spent on, or received from, long-term assets such as a new power plant. Financing cash flow — cash from loans, share issues and dividends. Cash conversion ratio — operating cash flow divided by net profit; it shows how much of the profit arrived as real cash. Capex — capital expenditure: money spent on long-lasting assets.

Ask a hundred NEPSE investors what a company earned last quarter, and most can quote the net profit in seconds — from a broker's app, a Sharesansar headline or a WhatsApp forward. Ask them what its operating cash flow was, and the room goes quiet.

Profit is the number that moves prices and makes dividend headlines. Cash flow is the number that would have warned you, months ahead, about many of this market's disappointments:

  • the hydropower company that could not repay its loan in its first monsoon of operation;
  • the manufacturer whose "record profit" was really a warehouse of unsold stock;
  • the finance company whose interest income existed mostly on paper, as dues never collected.

The cash flow statement is the one statement that is almost impossible to flatter for long. A company can change its depreciation policy, delay a provision or capitalise a cost, and the profit and loss account absorbs it quietly. Cash cannot. Either the money reached the bank account or it did not. That is why this chapter calls it the truth teller — the witness you call to cross-examine everything the income statement has just claimed.

Lesson 27.1 — Why Nepali Investors Skip the Cash Flow Statement

Three forces explain why Nepali investors skip it. Knowing them is the first step to correcting for them.

1. How results are published

NEPSE companies publish unaudited quarterly reports in a format set by SEBON. That format shows the profit and loss and a short balance sheet. The full cash flow statement, prepared under NAS 7 (Nepal's version of IAS 7), usually appears only in the audited annual report — once a year, often weeks or months after the year ends in mid-July. An investor who only opens quarterly reports, which is what most trading apps show, never sees a cash flow statement for three quarters out of four.

2. The trading culture

NEPSE's culture is still driven heavily by price momentum and rumour. When the question in a Facebook group is "yo share futai badhcha ki?" (will this share keep going up?), attention goes to the chart and the headline EPS, not to how profit turned into cash. Cash flow analysis needs patience and gives no instant verdict. It does not tell you to buy or sell; it tells you whether to trust the number that is telling you to buy or sell.

3. The sectors that dominate NEPSE

Much of NEPSE's value sits in banks and in hydropower companies. Their cash flow statements look nothing like the factory example used in most textbooks and YouTube tutorials. An investor who learns from a generic lesson and then opens a bank's or a half-built power plant's cash flow statement often decides it "doesn't make sense" and gives up. Lessons 27.4 and 27.5 deal with exactly this.

KEY CONCEPT The cash flow statement records only real cash moving in or out during the period, in three sections: operating activities (cash from the core business), investing activities (cash spent on or received from long-term assets and investments) and financing activities (cash from or to lenders and shareholders — loans taken and repaid, shares issued, dividends paid). Net profit is an accounting figure built on when income and costs are earned; cash flow is a record of money that actually changed hands. Most of the useful information lies in the gap between them.

Nepali investors can do this analysis perfectly well. The habit just has to be built on purpose. And in Nepal, a cash flow statement read without its sector's context is worse than not reading it at all: it produces confident, wrong conclusions.

Lesson 27.2 — The Three Rivers: Reading Operating, Investing, and Financing Activities

Think of the cash flow statement as three rivers flowing into and out of one lake — the company's bank balance. The skill is not memorising definitions. It is reading the signs (plus or minus) of all three together as one story about where the company is in its life and how honestly it is run.

Operating cash flow (CFO)

Cash from the everyday business: money received from customers, minus payments to suppliers, staff and for running costs, minus tax. Nearly all NEPSE companies use the indirect method: they start from net profit and adjust for non-cash items and working-capital changes. Over several years, CFO should track profit. When it keeps failing to, that is the most important signal in this chapter (Lesson 27.6).

Investing cash flow (CFI)

Cash spent buying property, plant, equipment, intangibles and investments in other companies, minus cash from selling them (and, where allowed, investment income). For a factory adding a production line, an insurer investing premiums in government bonds, or a hydropower company paying for a powerhouse, it is naturally negative. That is not automatically bad. It depends on the stage of investment and whether the spending is funded sustainably — which the third river shows.

Financing cash flow (CFF)

Cash from or to the providers of capital: loans taken and repaid, money from share issues (IPOs, FPOs and rights issues, all very common on NEPSE) and cash dividends paid. Bonus shares, so common on NEPSE, involve no cash and never appear here. This river shows how the company bridges any gap between what its business earns and what its investments need.

The four signatures

The meaning of any one sign depends on the other two. Learn to recognise four patterns on sight:

  1. Mature, self-funding: CFO positive and comfortably larger than CFI (modestly negative, for upkeep). CFF modestly negative as the company repays debt and pays dividends from real cash. You want this in an established dividend-paying manufacturer, or a hydropower company several years past starting operation with its construction loans largely repaid.
  2. Growing: CFO positive, CFI heavily negative as the company reinvests, CFF positive as it raises new debt or equity. Healthy if CFO is real and rising. Dangerous if a company claims to be "growing" while CFO is weak or negative — then lenders and shareholders, not the business, are paying for everything.
  3. Construction or pre-revenue: the normal pattern for a Nepali hydropower company still building. CFO near zero (only interest on idle project money), CFI deeply negative, CFF strongly positive from project loans and, near listing, IPO money. Normal during construction. The errors are mistaking it for distress — or failing to notice when it continues after the plant should be running (Lesson 27.4).
  4. Distressed: CFO negative or worsening while reported profit is flat or rising. CFI negative, or worse, positive because assets are being sold to survive. CFF positive from fresh short-term loans just to plug the gap — or negative because lenders refuse to roll over credit. Lesson 27.6 teaches you to spot this early.
WATCH FOR One quarter, or even one year, of negative CFO is not automatically alarming. Seasonal stock-building, a big one-off tax payment, or extra credit given to dealers before Dashain can all cause a negative period that later reverses. What should worry you is a pattern over three to five audited annual reports (not the noisier quarterly figures) in which CFO lags profit by a wide gap that does not close.

Lesson 27.3 — Reconciling Net Profit to Operating Cash Flow

The most skipped paragraph

The reconciliation from profit to operating cash flow is the most information-packed paragraph in a NEPSE annual report — and the most skipped. It looks like mechanical bookkeeping. It is not. Each line is a sentence about how the company's real economics differ from its reported profit.

Three kinds of adjustment

The reconciliation starts from profit before or after tax (reports differ, so check the first line before comparing companies) and makes three kinds of adjustment:

  1. Non-cash items are added back or taken off: depreciation and amortisation (always added back — they cut profit but used no cash), new provisions for bad debts and impairment (added back), unrealised exchange gains or losses, and changes in the value of investments (important for insurers and banks).
  2. Working-capital changes: a rise in receivables or stock is taken off (cash tied up, not yet collected); a rise in payables is added (the company is using suppliers' credit as a source of cash).
  3. Interest, dividends received and tax paid are placed according to the company's policy; NAS 7 allows some choice, especially for financial institutions.

A worked example

Take a made-up NEPSE manufacturer, "Himal Industries Ltd", to see how a strong profit can hide a weaker cash reality:

Reconciliation line (Rs million)Amount
Net profit after tax450
Add: depreciation and amortisation180
Add: provision for doubtful debts60
Less: increase in trade receivables(320)
Less: increase in inventory(210)
Add: increase in trade payables90
Less: interest income moved to investing activities(25)
Net cash flow from operating activities225

On the profit and loss account, Himal Industries looks healthy. But the reconciliation shows that of Rs 450 million profit, Rs 320 million was swallowed by money customers still owed and Rs 210 million by rising stock. Only Rs 90 million of extra supplier credit and Rs 240 million of non-cash add-backs offset this. Real operating cash was Rs 225 million — about half the reported profit.

That is not necessarily fraud. It may be a genuine sales push on generous credit to dealers before a festival — very common in Nepali consumer goods and cement around Dashain and Tihar. But it must be questioned, not celebrated. The key follow-up is whether those receivables are collected next quarter (confirming real, timing-driven sales) or keep piling up year after year (pointing to weak customers or, at worst, sales that never really happened).

PRACTICAL TOOL Work out the cash conversion ratio for any NEPSE company you study: operating cash flow divided by net profit after tax, from the audited annual report. A healthy, mature company usually averages 0.8–1.0 or more over three years. A ratio consistently below 0.5, especially if falling, means profit is increasingly built on paper — receivables, stock, capitalised costs — rather than cash. Read the notes on receivables ageing and related parties before paying the multiple the market gives that profit.

Two Nepali wrinkles

Related parties. Many NEPSE companies belong to promoter-controlled groups. Money owed by a related company is far more worrying than money owed by an independent dealer. The notes show related-party receivables separately — one of the few places where five minutes of reading directly protects your money.

Thin disclosure. Reporting quality varies among smaller non-financial companies. Some show little working-capital detail in the cash flow statement. Then you must work out the changes yourself from two years' balance sheets.

Lesson 27.4 — Hydropower's Cash Flow Signature: Construction Financing and the Capex Mountain

How Nepali hydropower is financed

No NEPSE sector punishes cash-flow illiteracy more than hydropower. Its construction-phase cash flow looks precarious to an untrained eye. It is actually the normal shape of project finance — like a farmer who spends on seed, fertiliser and irrigation for months before the harvest brings any income.

  • A typical project is funded 70:30 to 80:20 debt to equity.
  • The debt is shared among Nepali commercial and development banks — often a dozen or more lenders for a mid-sized project, because NRB limits how much one bank can lend to one borrower — and sometimes the Employees Provident Fund, Citizen Investment Trust and hydropower-focused debt instruments.
  • Under hydropower policy, a project must offer a share of its equity to the public and to local people affected by the project. That is why nearly every hydropower company on NEPSE held an IPO during construction or soon after starting operation — the source of the regular waves of hydropower IPOs.

The construction signature

Construction of a sizeable run-of-river project usually takes four to seven years from financial closure to commercial operation. During that time:

  • CFO is near zero — only interest on idle project money and minor other income;
  • CFI is deeply and increasingly negative — payments to civil contractors, imported turbines and electrical equipment, and the line to the grid;
  • CFF is strongly positive — loan instalments drawn as construction milestones are reached, promoters' equity, and IPO money.
CASE IN POINT Upper Tamakoshi and Chilime, now bellwether hydropower names, both went through long construction phases with exactly this signature: little or no CFO, CFI in the negative billions of rupees as the dam, tunnel, penstock and powerhouse were built, and strongly positive CFF from bank loans and, around commercial operation, IPO money. An investor who looked at either during construction and said "no operating cash — avoid" would have been right about the fact and wrong about the investment. A new power plant only starts earning when it starts selling electricity to the NEA under its power purchase agreement.

Three better questions

Before the plant runs, "is CFO positive?" is almost meaningless. Ask instead:

  1. Is spending tracking the budget? If CFI grows faster than the project report planned, costs are overrunning — from tunnelling surprises in Himalayan geology, contractor disputes, or a falling rupee raising the price of imported turbines.
  2. Is the funding mix holding? Compare total loans drawn (from CFF) with the debt ceiling stated in the IPO prospectus. Borrowing more than planned usually means covering overruns.
  3. Does CFO turn positive after commercial operation — and settle at a sensible level? It should match the installed capacity, expected output (which depends on river flow, lower in the dry season) and the PPA rates. A plant that keeps posting weak CFO well after its stated operation date is signalling overstated output, disputes with the NEA over purchases or payment, or a grid that cannot take its power.
WARNING A hydropower company can show positive profit while its plant earns little cash, because interest on construction loans is often added to the plant's cost instead of expensed, and other choices can flatter profit before operation. The financing section is the corrective: rising total debt with no matching rise in real operating cash after the stated operation date is the clearest hydropower red flag on NEPSE — more reliable than any ratio from the income statement alone.

The seasons matter

Most NEPSE hydropower plants are run-of-river, with little storage. Their output — and so their cash flow — is concentrated in the monsoon and after (roughly Asar to Mangsir) and much weaker in the dry months (roughly Poush to Chaitra), when river flows fall and many plants run at a small fraction of capacity. A weak cash quarter in Falgun is expected and tells you nothing on its own. The same weakness in a monsoon quarter is a real signal.

Lesson 27.5 — Banks and Financial Institutions: Why Their Cash Flow Statements Look Upside Down

Deposits and loans count as "operating"

Hydropower confuses investors by looking shaky when it is only pre-revenue. Banks confuse them the other way: their cash flow statements put under "operating" things a factory-trained reader expects under investing or financing.

The reason is in NAS 7 itself. Operating activities are the entity's main revenue-earning activities. For a bank, that is taking deposits and making loans. So a bank's operating cash flow includes:

  • the net change in customer deposits;
  • the net change in loans to customers;
  • interest received from borrowers and interest paid to depositors;
  • buying and selling securities held for liquidity and treasury needs.

For a bank, a deposit is not borrowed capital like a bond. It is raw material — like a kirana shop buying rice to sell. By contrast, a bank's own long-term subordinated debentures, issued to strengthen its capital (common on NEPSE because of NRB's capital rules), sit under financing, because that is capital raised by the bank as a company.

Why bank CFO swings wildly

So a growing bank's operating cash flow can swing hugely from year to year, driven by deposits and loans rather than profitability:

  • A bank whose deposits jump in a year of easy liquidity and branch expansion shows a big positive CFO, even if its margins and loan quality are flat or worse.
  • A bank losing deposits — which happens in Nepal during liquidity squeezes, when credit-to-deposit ratios press against the regulatory ceiling — shows negative CFO even if its lending is healthy and profitable.
REGULATORY DETAIL NRB sets the format of financial statements for commercial banks, development banks and finance companies. NFRS-compliant Nepali banks build operating cash flow from net interest and fee income, adjusted for changes in loans to customers, customer deposits, balances with other banks, and other operating assets and liabilities. This follows the standard international template for financial institutions and is consistent across nearly all NEPSE banks and finance companies, so comparisons within the sector are reliable even though the format differs sharply from non-financial companies.

Three better tests for banks

Because of this, the cash conversion ratio that works for a manufacturer is nearly useless on its own for a bank. A bank can show 3.0 in a year of strong deposit growth and 0.2 or negative in a year of deposit outflow, with the same earnings quality. Use three adapted tests instead:

  1. Interest collected versus interest booked. Compare interest actually received in cash with interest counted on the income statement. A widening gap — seen as growing "interest receivable" or a rising share of non-performing or restructured loans where interest is added to the loan instead of paid — is the bank's version of the receivables red flag.
  2. Repeated capital raising. In financing activities, look for debentures, subordinated debt and rights share money. A bank that keeps raising fresh capital to maintain its capital ratio is telling you something about its ability to generate capital internally that a smooth EPS trend will not.
  3. Cash dividends versus bonus shares. Nepali banks have often paid most returns as bonus shares, which involve no cash and never appear in the cash flow statement. A bank whose cash dividends stay thin relative to profit year after year is keeping nearly all its capital to support growth and capital ratios. That matters if you expect cash income rather than more shares.

Lesson 27.6 — Reading Earnings Quality: When Profit Growth Isn't Cash Growth

The core test

This lesson turns the chapter into one habit: use the cash flow statement to screen the quality of earnings across your watchlist, before a headline profit moves your money.

Compare the growth of net profit with the growth of operating cash flow over at least three audited annual reports. One year proves little either way.

  • Both grow together: profit is turning into cash at a steady rate — the most reassuring pattern available.
  • Profit grows while CFO stalls, falls or turns negative: success is increasingly an accounting matter. The burden shifts to management to explain why. A genuine, temporary reason — such as a deliberate credit-financed push into a new region, later confirmed by collections — is different from a gap that is unexplained and widening.

Four ways profit outruns cash

  1. Sales on credit not yet collected. Most common and often harmless, especially around festivals. Test: is it collected in the next one or two quarters? Track receivables across quarterly reports.
  2. Costs capitalised that should be expensed. Routine repairs called "improvements", or, at worst, running costs added to an asset under construction. Profit and assets both rise, while cash leaves through the investing section. Watch for capital spending growing in step with revenue in a business that should not need it.
  3. Related-party sales. Real in accounting terms, but the cash may never be collected on commercial terms, or may just circle within the promoter group.
  4. Interest booked but not received (banks). Interest on stressed or restructured loans added to the loan balance instead of paid in cash inflates profit. NRB watches this through the bad-loan and restructured-loan disclosures it requires.
CAUTION A negative or falling CFO trend is a reason to investigate, not an automatic sell signal — treating it as one is itself a common mistake. Open the notes, read the receivables ageing schedule (required under NFRS), check whether the increase is concentrated in related parties or spread across customers, and compare with the company's own history and its closest listed peers. Cash flow analysis sharpens your questions; it does not replace asking them.

Free cash flow

A second test is free cash flow: operating cash flow minus capital spending (purchases of property, plant and equipment, from the investing section). For a mature, non-hydropower NEPSE company, free cash flow that is positive and growing over several years is the clearest evidence that the business can fund its own growth, service its debt and pay real cash dividends without repeatedly going back to shareholders or lenders. That is the profile of a durable long-term holding, not a story stock that needs constant outside money.

PRACTICAL TOOL Keep a simple three-line table for each core holding, updated when each audited annual report comes out: net profit after tax, operating cash flow, and capital spending. From these you can work out the cash conversion ratio (CFO ÷ profit) and free cash flow (CFO − capital spending) in under five minutes per company. A five-year view of those two figures tells you more about how durable a company's profit is than any single year's EPS growth, P/E or price chart.

Do not over-mechanise

Cash flow ratios are diagnostic tools, not verdicts. A hydropower company mid-construction fails every generic cash test and may still be an excellent investment at the right price. A mature trading company can pass every test on paper while depending on money owed by one fragile related customer.

The habit this chapter teaches is not a blind scorecard. Read the income statement's story; then open the cash flow statement and ask whether the cash moved the way that story says it should — always allowing for the sector, the stage of life and the season. Few NEPSE investors build this habit, which is exactly why it is such a lasting advantage for those who do.

Chapter recap

  • The cash flow statement records only real cash, in three sections: operating, investing and financing. It is the hardest statement to flatter.
  • Nepali investors skip it because quarterly reports leave it out, trading culture is momentum-driven, and bank and hydropower statements look unlike the textbook.
  • Read the three signs together. Four signatures: mature, growing, construction and distressed.
  • The profit-to-CFO reconciliation shows where profit went. Work out the cash conversion ratio; consistently below 0.5 is a flag. Check related-party receivables.
  • Hydropower under construction normally shows near-zero CFO, deeply negative CFI and positive CFF. Check the budget, the funding mix and whether CFO turns positive after commercial operation. Allow for the dry season.
  • Banks count deposit and loan changes as operating cash, so CFO swings with deposits. Instead compare interest collected with interest booked, watch repeated capital raising, and compare cash dividends with bonus shares.
  • Over three or more years, compare profit growth with CFO growth, and track free cash flow. A weak trend is a reason to investigate, not an automatic sell.
