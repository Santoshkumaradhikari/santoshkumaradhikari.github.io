WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Inventory — stock of raw materials and finished goods. Net realisable value — what stock would fetch if sold now, less selling costs; stock must not be valued above it. Working capital cycle — the days money is tied up from buying materials to collecting cash from customers, like a dairy paying farmers today and collecting from shops next month. Occupancy — rooms sold divided by rooms available. ADR — average daily rate: room revenue per room sold. RevPAR — revenue per available room: occupancy times ADR.

Lesson 34.1 — The Sector Nobody Talks About, and Why That's Useful

A small corner of NEPSE

On any NEPSE sector dashboard, your eye goes where the money is: commercial banks, hydropower, insurers, microfinance. Manufacturing, trading and hotels sit at the bottom, usually lumped together as "the other stuff".

One widely cited snapshot of NEPSE's market value, from when about 219 companies were listed, put commercial banks at about 37.7 percent, insurance 15.3 percent, microfinance 9.5 percent and hydropower 8.4 percent — but manufacturing only about 3.7 percent, hotels about 1.5 percent and trading just 0.5 percent. Together, the three sectors in this chapter were under six percent of the exchange. NEPSE has since grown to 302 listed companies, mostly through new hydropower and microfinance issues, but the order has not changed.

This is not a flaw in the market. It is a fact about how Nepali business is owned, and understanding why so few real goods-and-services companies list tells you as much as the companies themselves.

Why the sector stays small

  1. No rule pushed them to list. NRB's capital rules forced banks and finance companies to raise public equity, and hydropower developers need public float for licensing and to reach retail investors. Nothing has ever pushed a noodle factory, a garment exporter or a family trading house onto the exchange.
  2. Family business houses prefer control. Groups such as Golyan, Khetan, Chaudhary and Dugar, and the promoter families behind Nepal Distilleries, built their businesses privately, funded growth with retained profit and bank loans, and have little wish to dilute control or open their books to public shareholders and SEBON.
  3. Some listed because they had to. Several manufacturers on NEPSE arrived not to raise growth money but because company law and government policy at times required public enterprises and some large private companies to offer shares to the public. That is how Bottlers Nepal (Balaju), Bottlers Nepal (Terai) and Unilever Nepal came to be listed decades ago — with promoters still holding almost everything and very few shares trading.
KEY CONCEPT A "thin sector" is not necessarily a low-quality sector. It is one where tradable shares, trading volume and the number of comparable peers are all small. Multiples here often sit far outside bank norms — Unilever Nepal and Bottlers Nepal have historically had some of the highest share prices on NEPSE — partly because so few shares change hands that scarcity itself becomes part of the price, like a rare variety of rice that sells high simply because so little reaches the market.

Few peers to compare

For a commercial bank, you have about twenty peers reporting in the same NRB format. For Ghorahi Cement Industry, your real domestic peers are Shivam Cements and Udayapur Cement Industry — three companies with three different owners (two private promoter-led, one fully government-owned) and three different capital histories. You must build your own sense of "normal" rather than borrow a crowded sector average.

Accounting in its plainest form

There is another reason to study this sector despite its small weight. Here you see core accrual accounting — inventory, cost of goods sold, depreciation of physical plant and working capital — without the regulatory layers (loan-loss rules, actuarial reserves, PPA-linked revenue) that dominate banks, insurers and hydropower. Part VI has shown how regulation reshapes accounting sector by sector. This chapter shows accounting close to its textbook form: read last, but understood first.

Lesson 34.2 — Inventory Accounting Under NFRS: NAS 2 in Practice

The key asset

For a bank, the key asset is the loan book. For a manufacturer or trader, it is inventory — stock of materials and goods. Nepal Accounting Standard 2 (NAS 2, matching IAS 2) governs how it is valued, and mistakes or aggressive choices here flow straight into gross margin.

REGULATORY DETAIL NAS 2 values inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV). Cost has three layers: (1) purchase cost — price, import duty, inward freight and other direct costs, minus trade discounts; (2) conversion cost — direct labour and a fair share of production overheads; (3) other costs of bringing the stock to its present place and condition. NRV is the expected selling price minus the costs still needed to finish and sell it.

Cement

For Shivam Cements, Ghorahi Cement or the state-owned Udayapur Cement, inventory comes in several types, each shown separately:

  • raw materials — limestone, gypsum, clinker (if not made in-house), fly ash, packaging;
  • work in progress — clinker in the kiln;
  • finished goods — bagged cement ready to dispatch.

Limestone from a company's own quarry is costed at what it takes to extract and process, not at market price. Udayapur Cement's limestone reserve is reported to last about two hundred years at current extraction, with only a small part mined so far. So a cement company with its own limestone can have a structurally lower raw-material cost than a rival without one — a real competitive advantage hiding in an inventory note.

Beverages

For Bottlers Nepal (Balaju or Terai), raw materials are imported concentrate and packaging (PET preforms, glass bottles, crown caps, labels); work in progress is syrup and unfinished batches; finished goods are cased drinks. Empty returnable glass bottles and crates are often carried as a mix of asset and stock, since they are reused many times yet also consumed as packaging.

Trading companies

For a trader such as Salt Trading Corporation, Nepal Lube Oil or Bishal Bazar Company, there is no conversion layer. A trader buys finished goods and resells them largely unchanged, so inventory cost is simply invoice price, customs duty and inbound freight. That is the cleanest inventory accounting on NEPSE — and the reason traders' gross margins are thin and steady, while manufacturers' swing with how full their plants run and with input prices.

FIFO versus weighted average

NAS 2 allows two main cost methods (specific identification is kept for goods that are not interchangeable, such as heavy machinery):

  • FIFO (first in, first out) — the oldest stock is treated as sold first;
  • weighted average — all stock is blended into one average cost each period.

The choice matters more than most investors think. Nepal's imported inputs — clinker, packaging resin, concentrate, fuel — have risen in price in recent years, as the rupee (pegged to the Indian rupee) weakened against the dollar and global commodity prices moved. When prices rise, FIFO charges the oldest, cheapest stock to cost of sales first, so gross margin looks better than under weighted average, even though the business is no more efficient. Think of a shopkeeper who still has last year's cheaper sugar in the back: margins look great until that stock runs out.

WARNING When comparing gross margin trends at two manufacturers, check the inventory cost-method note first. A FIFO company showing rising margins during input inflation may simply be using up cheaper old stock — a one-time effect that reverses when the next batch is costed at today's prices. This is not fraud; it is how FIFO works, and careless readers mistake it for better operations.

Write-downs and their reversal

The lower-of-cost-or-NRV rule forces a write-down whenever expected selling prices fall below cost — packaging made obsolete by a design change, drinks past their shelf life, cement that absorbed moisture and hardened, or slow-moving lines in a trader's warehouse. NAS 2 also allows a write-down to be reversed (up to original cost, never above) if NRV recovers. Watch for this: a reversal reduces cost of sales and can flatter a quarter's margin with no real change in the business.

Lesson 34.3 — The Working Capital Cycle: Manufacturing versus Trading

The cash conversion cycle

If inventory is the biggest asset, the working capital cycle is the clock showing how fast the company turns it into cash. It is the most useful single lens for telling manufacturers from traders in Nepal. Think of a dairy: it pays farmers today, holds milk and curd for a while, sells to shops on credit, and collects next month. The days between paying out and collecting in are the cycle.

The cash conversion cycle (CCC) has three parts, each in days:

Days inventory outstanding (DIO) = (average inventory ÷ cost of goods sold) × 365
Days sales outstanding (DSO) = (average trade receivables ÷ revenue) × 365
Days payable outstanding (DPO) = (average trade payables ÷ cost of goods sold) × 365
Cash conversion cycle = DIO + DSO − DPO

PRACTICAL TOOL Put three years of a manufacturer's and a trader's accounts side by side and work out DIO, DSO, DPO and CCC for each. A cycle that lengthens over time — stock piling up, receivables stretching, or suppliers being paid faster — is one of the earliest, least-noticed signs of working-capital stress, often visible two or three quarters before it shows in profit or in loan conditions.

Why manufacturers are slower

Manufacturers hold raw materials, work in progress and finished goods all at once. Cement adds heavy fixed capital, seasonal demand — the monsoon slows construction, so finished stock builds up and must be financed through the low season — and dealer credit that pushes receivables out. A cement maker's stock often amounts to several months of production once dealer stocking and the monsoon are counted.

Why traders are faster

Traders exist to shorten the cycle. Salt Trading Corporation and Nepal Lube Oil buy, distribute and collect with little added value. Their edge is working-capital efficiency: faster stock turns, tighter dealer collections (often backed by advance payments or dealer deposits) and good payment terms from suppliers. A well-run trader's cycle can be a fraction of a manufacturer's. That is why traders can earn respectable returns on equity from thin margins: the money turns over so many times a year that a small margin multiplied by high turnover still gives a good return.

Illustrative comparison

CASE IN POINT Shivam Cements came to market in Nepal's first cement IPO in 2017, at premium prices — Rs 200 for local people of the plant's district and Rs 300 for the general public, a two-tier structure then common for large industrial issues. It was a rare large private manufacturer choosing public money over purely family and bank funding, because cement expansion needs more capital than bank loans alone can supply. Ghorahi Cement, by contrast, listed its IPO shares only in 2023, in the same batch as two hydropower companies — a reminder that cement IPOs in Nepal still come as a trickle, not a wave.

Lesson 34.4 — Revenue Recognition and Cost of Goods Sold in Manufacturing

When a sale counts

Under NFRS 15, a manufacturer counts revenue when control of the goods passes to the customer — usually at dispatch from the factory or delivery to the distributor's warehouse, depending on the contract's shipping terms. For plain ex-factory cement or drinks, that is simple. The real complexity lies in the cost of goods sold beneath it, because that is where capacity utilisation — the biggest driver of manufacturing profit — shows up.

How full the plant runs

A cement kiln or bottling line has fixed costs — depreciation, maintenance, a core of skilled staff — that barely fall when output falls. Running at 85 percent of capacity instead of 60 percent spreads the same fixed cost over far more units, so the cost per bag or per case drops sharply and margin jumps. It is like a bus: the fuel and driver cost the same whether 20 or 50 passengers board, so every extra passenger is nearly pure profit.

That is why NEPSE manufacturers can show margin swings of several points from quarter to quarter that have nothing to do with input prices. Check them against disclosed capacity utilisation, or infer it from how revenue growth and margin move together.

Depreciation of factories and hotels

NAS 16 (Property, Plant and Equipment) governs depreciation for manufacturers and hotels alike, and the method and useful life chosen affect profit a lot, since buildings and plant are their biggest long-term assets.

REGULATORY DETAIL NAS 16 requires depreciation to follow how the asset's benefits are used up. Nepali manufacturers and hotels mostly depreciate buildings in equal yearly amounts, and plant, furniture and equipment either in equal amounts or by a reducing balance. Typical lives: 25–50 years for factory and hotel buildings, 10–20 years for major plant and machinery, and 5–10 years for hotel furniture, fixtures and soft goods. While an asset is being built — a new cement line, a hotel wing under renovation — NAS 23 requires the related interest to be added to its cost, so finance costs can look low during a long expansion and then jump once it opens.

For a hotel, much of the depreciating base is furniture, fixtures and equipment (FF&E) — mattresses, carpets, kitchen equipment, room technology — which must be renewed far more often than the building. A hotel that delays this to protect today's profit trades current earnings for a later problem: tired rooms, unhappy guests and eventually lower rates. A multi-year fall in FF&E spending relative to revenue is worth flagging even if profit looks fine.

Lesson 34.5 — Hotel Accounting: Occupancy, ADR, RevPAR, and Seasonality

The listed hotels

Hotels are a distinct problem. A hotel sells room-nights, food and drink, and extras (banquets, spa, laundry, business centre) all at once, each with different margins, and its revenue is more seasonal than any other NEPSE sector's.

NEPSE lists a small handful of hotel companies, most prominently Soaltee Hotel (five-star, Kathmandu), Oriental Hotels (the Radisson-branded property), Taragaon Regency Hotel (the Hyatt-branded property in Boudha) and, more recently, City Hotel (Hyatt Centric). Their combined weight is tiny, but their reports are unusually revealing because Nepal's tourism faces sharp, visible shocks.

Three kinds of hotel revenue

  • Room revenue — the highest-margin line. Rooms have largely fixed costs, so an extra occupied room-night drops close to its full rate into operating profit.
  • Food and beverage — banquets, restaurants, room service — lower margin, because food and labour costs rise with volume.
  • Other — spa, business centre, laundry, telephone, forex, transport.

Watch room revenue most closely. F&B growth without matching room growth often means a hotel is filling weak occupancy by pushing banquets and walk-in dining, not that its core business is stronger.

Three metrics to calculate

KEY CONCEPT Occupancy = rooms sold ÷ rooms available (a percentage). Average daily rate (ADR) = room revenue ÷ rooms sold (the average price actually received per occupied room). Revenue per available room (RevPAR) = occupancy × ADR, or room revenue ÷ rooms available. RevPAR is the most useful single measure because it captures both how full the hotel is and what it charges. A hotel can be nearly full at discounted rates or half-full at premium rates; RevPAR makes the two comparable.

Nepali hotel reports do not always give occupancy and ADR (unlike US or Indian hotel disclosures). You can often work out an implied RevPAR yourself: divide room revenue by available room-nights (rooms × days in the period, adjusting for rooms closed for renovation).

Seasons, and shocks

Nepal's tourism has two peaks — autumn trekking and festivals (about September to November) and spring trekking (March to May) — with the monsoon (June to August) and winter as troughs. Compare a hotel's quarter with the same quarter last year, not with the previous quarter. A fall from an autumn-peak quarter into a monsoon quarter is normal seasonality, not decline.

But seasonality is manageable and predictable. FY 2025/26 showed a far harder kind of risk: a sudden event shock on top of a strong tourism market.

CASE IN POINT Nepal recorded a record 1,209,357 foreign tourist arrivals in FY 2025/26 (FY 2082/83), and arrivals in the first seven months of calendar 2026 were up 6.84 percent — by every measure a strong tourism year. Yet eight listed hotel companies together swung from a profit of about Rs 1.02 arba in FY 2024/25 to a loss of about Rs 25.6 crore in FY 2025/26, with revenue down 14.5 percent from Rs 7.29 arba to Rs 6.23 arba. The cause was not tourism; it was the September 2025 "Gen-Z protests", which damaged and disrupted hotels.
  • Taragaon Regency (Hyatt) was targeted and stayed closed for nearly a year, with 133 staff on leave, and lost about Rs 70.57 crore. In September 2026 its operators said they aimed to reopen by December 2026, while Hilton Kathmandu was still being rebuilt (Onlinekhabar, 10 September 2026).
  • Hyatt Centric (City Hotel) lost about Rs 17 crore on revenue of Rs 53 crore.
  • Oriental Hotels (Radisson) lost about Rs 4.75 crore on revenue of just over Rs 1 arba.
  • Soaltee Hotel stayed solidly profitable: revenue about Rs 3.14 arba, profit about Rs 76.11 crore, EPS about Rs 6.48.
Within one shared shock, balance-sheet strength, brand position and physical exposure to the unrest produced very different results for hotels in the same city and the same tourism market.

This is one of the most important hotel lessons for a Nepali investor. High fixed costs — payroll, utilities and depreciation do not shrink when the building is empty or closed — combined with sudden shocks produce far more earnings swings than tourism data suggests. A hotel's occupancy and ADR from two years ago say almost nothing about how it will survive a week of unrest, a border closure or a travel advisory. Assess separately:

  • its cash cushion — how many months of fixed costs its cash and short-term investments can cover;
  • its insurance, including business-interruption cover;
  • the physical and political exposure of the property's location.
WARNING Never extend a hotel's latest RevPAR trend on its own. Nepali hotel earnings have shown — most recently in the September 2025 unrest — that they can swing from record-year profits to sector-wide losses within one fiscal year because of a single event unrelated to demand. Build a downside case into every hotel valuation, and check its insurance and business-interruption cover, not just its occupancy.

Lesson 34.6 — Related-Party Transactions and Governance in Family-Owned Conglomerates

The listed company is rarely independent

Nearly every manufacturer and trader on NEPSE sits inside a larger, mostly private family group:

  • Bottlers Nepal (Balaju and Terai) has long ties to the Khetan Group and the promoter families behind Nepal Distilleries and Khukri Rum;
  • Shivam Cements sits within a wider industrial holding structure, with Shivam Holdings — its rated parent — above the listed company;
  • Unilever Nepal is majority-owned by its multinational parent, with a small public float;
  • beyond the exchange, groups such as Chaudhary Group, Golyan Group and Dugar Group run manufacturing, trading, hotel and financial businesses side by side, with only a fraction ever reaching a listed company.

This is the most important governance fact in this sector: the company you analyse is often not economically independent of its promoters.

REGULATORY DETAIL NAS 24 (Related Party Disclosures) requires listed companies to disclose dealings with related parties — parents and subsidiaries, key managers, companies under common control and close family of controlling shareholders — including the relationship, the amounts, balances outstanding and any provisions for doubtful amounts. For Nepali groups, this note is often one of the most revealing parts of the annual report: related-party purchases of raw materials, sales of finished goods, loans and guarantees between companies, shared-service charges (management fees, brand royalties), and directors' and promoters' pay and holdings.

How profit can be moved

A manufacturer that buys much of its raw material from a related trading company, or sells much of its output through a related distributor, can shift margin between the listed company and the private affiliate through transfer pricing — the prices charged between them. Profit can be pushed to whichever side the family prefers, often for tax or dividend-timing reasons that have nothing to do with minority shareholders. It is like brothers who run a shop and a warehouse: the price the shop pays the warehouse decides which brother's books look rich.

This is not necessarily illegal or improper. NFRS and company law require disclosure, not prohibition. But it means margins at a related-party-heavy manufacturer need an extra layer of scepticism that a bank's regulated, standardised margins do not.

CAUTION Before valuing any Nepali manufacturer or trader on reported profit, read the related-party note in full and ask: (1) What share of purchases or sales goes through related companies, and has it changed much from last year? (2) Are related-party balances (loans, advances, guarantees) growing faster than the company's own operating cash flow — a sign it may be quietly financing private affiliates? (3) Do promoters' and directors' pay, brand royalties or management fees rise with profit in a way that leaves minority shareholders a shrinking share, even as revenue grows? There is no single right answer, but a company that does not answer these clearly should be valued more cautiously.

A governance checklist for this sector

  • Promoter holding and its trend: a rising promoter stake through preferential allotments dilutes public shareholders differently from open-market buying.
  • Independent directors: are they really independent of the family, or long-time associates?
  • Dividends against free cash flow: a family company with strong cash generation but a persistently low payout may be keeping cash for the private side of the group.
  • Auditor tenure and any qualified opinions: these carry more weight in a related-party-heavy structure than in a standardised bank audit.
CASE IN POINT Market commentary often compares Shivam Cements with premium-priced names such as Unilever Nepal, Bottlers Nepal and Himalayan Distillery. All are manufacturers, yet their multiples differ wildly — less because of product economics than because of scarce float, brand durability, steady dividends and, for multinational-linked names, confidence in governance standards brought from the parent. Cement earnings come from a cyclical, capital-heavy, commodity-like business; a branded drinks bottler's from repeat purchases with light working capital. Comparing their P/E ratios means nothing until you allow for these different business models.

Chapter recap

  • Manufacturing, trading and hotels are under a tenth of NEPSE's value, because no rule pushed family business houses to list. Few shares trade, scarcity lifts some prices, and there are few peers to compare.
  • This is where you see accounting in its plainest form: inventory, cost of goods sold, depreciation and working capital.
  • Inventory (NAS 2) is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value. Own limestone can be a hidden cost advantage. Under rising prices, FIFO can flatter margins temporarily; watch write-down reversals too.
  • Work out the cash conversion cycle (DIO + DSO − DPO). Manufacturers run slower and higher-margin; traders faster and thinner-margin, earning their return through turnover. A lengthening cycle is an early warning.
  • Manufacturing profit swings with capacity utilisation. Depreciation lives and capitalised interest (NAS 16, NAS 23) shape profit; for hotels, watch spending on furniture, fixtures and equipment.
  • For hotels, calculate occupancy, ADR and RevPAR, compare the same quarter year on year, and stress-test for shocks: the September 2025 unrest turned eight listed hotels from about Rs 1.02 arba profit to about Rs 25.6 crore loss despite record tourist arrivals, while Soaltee stayed profitable.
  • Most listed manufacturers sit inside family groups. Read the related-party note (NAS 24) for transfer pricing, growing balances and fees, and check promoter trends, independence, payout against cash flow, and the auditor.
