WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Cross-holding — one business family owning parts of several listed companies at once. Contagion — trouble spreading from one company to its sister companies, like fire running through joined houses in an old Kathmandu tole. Circular financing — the same rupee counted twice: lent by one group company and then shown as fresh capital in another. Single obligor limit — a cap on how much a bank may lend to one borrower or one group. D-SIB — a "domestic systemically important bank": one so large that its failure would hurt the whole economy, so NRB asks it to hold extra capital.

Ask a small investor in Kathmandu why they hold a commercial bank, a hydropower company, an insurer and a trading company, and they will say they have spread their money across four sectors. Now open the four annual reports and read the promoter lists and the directors' names. There is a real chance you will find the same three or four surnames on every board, the same family trust as the biggest promoter in each, and loans, guarantees and pledged shares connecting all four. What looked like four separate bets on the screen may be one bet on one family.

This is common in emerging markets, where family money built industry before stock markets grew up. In Nepal it has a particular shape, set by the order in which banking, insurance and hydropower were opened to private owners. For an investor, seeing that shape is the difference between pricing one company's risk and unknowingly taking on a whole family's balance sheet, one share at a time.

Lesson 22.1 — The Origins and Shape of Nepal's Promoter Webs

Nepal's big business houses mostly began as family trading firms — import-export houses, factories, food processing — that built up money over one or two generations. From the 1980s, when NRB began licensing private and joint-venture banks, it wanted a solid block of promoter money behind each bank, locked in for years, alongside public shareholders. The families that already had cash and no other way into regulated finance were the natural fit. Bank licences went to identifiable promoter groups, and those groups were largely the same trading and manufacturing houses that led the private economy.

The Chaudhary Group is a well-documented example. Besides its consumer-goods and hotel businesses, it is a promoter of Nabil Bank — Nepal's first private-sector commercial bank — and of Nabil Investment Banking, where CG Finco, a group company that also runs a remittance business, is a shareholder. Nothing is hidden; it is disclosed company structure. But it shows the mechanism: one family holding promoter positions across a bank, its investment-banking arm and a linked finance company, each of which a small investor meets as a separate name.

The same thing happened when hydropower opened to private investors from the 1990s. Hydropower needs large, patient money and carries construction risk — a natural next step for houses used to long industrial projects. Government incentives, power purchase agreements with the Nepal Electricity Authority and rules requiring hydropower promoters to sell part of the shares to the public created a second sector where the same family money reappeared as promoter shareholding. The Golyan Group, for example, spans textiles, food processing and hotels, and more recently a large hydropower and solar portfolio across more than a dozen projects.

Insurance followed the same path. As NRB and the insurance regulator (now the Nepal Insurance Authority) issued new licences in waves, much of the promoter money to meet minimum capital came from the same established houses.

Nobody planned an empire. The webs formed because Nepal's capital-hungry, licensed sectors — banking, insurance, hydropower — all needed the same kind of promoter: concentrated family money willing to stay locked in for years. The families with that money first kept taking promoter positions as each sector opened, and directors, once on one board, carried their contacts onto others.

WHY THIS MATTERS Owning promoter stakes across sectors is not wrongdoing. It is how Nepal's licensed sectors were built. Your job is not to treat it as a scandal but as a fact that changes how risk flows between the shares you own.

Lesson 22.2 — Mapping the Ownership Web: A Reader's Method

Before judging group risk, draw a simple map of who stands behind each company. It is slow work, but it uses only public documents, and it is the most valuable hour you can spend before buying a bank, insurer or hydropower company with a visible business house behind it.

  1. Read the related-party note in the annual report. Under Nepal Financial Reporting Standards, listed companies must disclose dealings with related parties — loans to or from linked companies, guarantees, pay of key managers, and deals with companies under common control. It names the counterparties, so it is often the richest page for this purpose. Read it every year; the links change as a group's money needs change.
  2. Cross-check directors. From annual reports, prospectuses and SEBON filings, list each director, the promoter they represent, and every other company where the same name appears. Directors' biographies usually list other directorships. One person on the boards of a bank, a hydropower company and an insurer is showing you the web directly; you just have to read three documents to see it.
  3. Use promoter shareholding pages. Financial portals and broker research pages break holdings into promoter and public and often name the largest promoters. Search for the same trusts, holding companies or people across every company you can find.
  4. For hydropower, read the IPO prospectus. It must describe the promoter group's other business interests — often the clearest one-page picture of a family's business footprint anywhere.
  5. Use SEBON's governance directive as the standard. The Listed Companies Corporate Governance Directive, 2074 sets what board make-up and governance disclosure should look like. Be more sceptical of any company whose filings fall clearly short of it.

None of this is difficult. It just means putting together documents that are each public but that nobody else assembles into one picture. That assembly is the value you create for yourself.

Lesson 22.3 — The Specific Risks: Contagion, Circular Financing, and Overstated Group Value

Once you can see the web, three risks appear that you cannot see when you look at one company alone.

1. Contagion

Contagion means trouble spreading from one company to its sister companies — like fire running through the joined houses of an old Kathmandu tole. If a group's finance company suffers a jump in bad loans, depositors may start doubting the group's bank too, even if the bank's own loans are sound, because people react to the family name faster than they can check legal separateness. If a group hydropower project runs over budget or its power agreement is delayed, the family may pull cash from, or pledge shares of, its other listed companies to keep it alive — moving stress from a company you do not own into one you do.

2. Circular financing

Circular financing means the same rupee counted twice. In its simplest form: Company A, a group bank or finance company, lends to Company B, a group hydropower or manufacturing firm. Company B uses part of that loan to buy rights or IPO shares in Company A or another group company, C. From outside it looks like fresh capital. In fact the same rupee of bank credit appears once as a loan on A's books and again as new paid-up capital on C's books. So when you hear that promoters "fully subscribed" a rights issue, ask where their money came from — and whether it traces back to another group balance sheet. A related version: promoters pledge shares of one group company to get margin loans, then use the money to meet calls in another. That chain is invisible from any single annual report but visible once you put the group's pledge disclosures side by side.

3. Overstated group value

When the same promoter money appears, directly or through cross-holdings, as equity in several listed companies, adding up their market values counts some of that money more than once. A family that seems to own a bank, an insurer and three hydropower companies may really be stretching one pool of money across all five, each partly depending on the others to keep performing. This can be fully legal and disclosed. But it means "a strong group" is not a blanket reason to trust every company carrying its name.

All of these come from connections, not from any one company's numbers — which is why they disappear if your analysis stops at the company you are buying.

Lesson 22.4 — Regulation: What Nepal Restricts, What It Doesn't (Yet)

Nepal's rules deal with parts of this problem. No single rule stops one business house from being a promoter of a bank, an insurer and a hydropower company at once, or from putting the same directors on all their boards. Knowing what is and is not restricted tells you how much checking the system does for you, and how much is left to you.

Lending to one borrower or group

A single obligor limit caps how much a bank may lend to one borrower or one connected group. NRB's traditional rules also required its prior approval for very large single loans (historically around Rs 25 crore and above). In 2025 NRB loosened these rules: it scrapped the Rs 25 crore per-customer cap on share-backed loans, and reports describe the prior-approval threshold for large loans as removed too, to help banks finance big infrastructure, industrial and hydropower projects. The wider limit linking a bank's exposure to one borrower group to its core capital — which Chapters 27 and 28 rely on — should be checked against NRB's current Unified Directives before you assume it still binds. The practical effect: more of the check on lending to a promoter's own group now rests on the bank's own board and risk managers rather than on central approval. So more of the checking has moved onto you.

Banks holding shares in other companies

NRB's first-quarter review of December 2025 also cut the minimum period for which a bank must hold listed shares and debentures from one year to six months, and removed the rule that a bank could sell no more than 20 percent of such holdings in a year. Banks can now build and unwind share positions more freely — potentially including positions in companies linked to their own promoters.

Separating bankers from borrowers

A 2024 BAFIA amendment proposed a direct answer to the cross-holding problem: anyone holding more than 1 percent of a bank's paid-up capital could not borrow from other banks, and large shareholders whose business debt exceeded 1 percent of paid-up capital could not serve as directors — in effect, separating "bankers" from "business owners". The Nepal Bankers' Association pushed back. Its leaders argued that the roughly Rs 750 billion (Rs 7.5 kharba) of bank capital belongs mostly to industrialists already, and that promoters cannot easily exit, so the rule would be unworkable without a long transition. This book could not confirm that the provision became law — many bills still before Parliament lapsed when the House of Representatives was dissolved in September 2025, so check the current text of BAFIA before relying on it. Either way, the practice it targets has not been fully closed off by law.

Recognising big, connected banks

NRB's Domestic Systemically Important Bank (D-SIB) framework, issued in November 2025, covers the ten largest banks and asks them to hold extra capital of up to 1 percent, phased in from 2027. A D-SIB is a bank so large and connected that its failure would hurt the whole economy. Reports describe "interconnectedness" as 30 percent of the assessment. So NRB does see connections between institutions as a system risk. But the framework does not turn that lens on promoter groups for ordinary investors.

Lesson 22.5 — A Practical Due-Diligence Framework Before You Buy

Because the rules leave gaps, the stress-testing is yours. Turn the map from Lesson 22.2 into a decision with these steps.

  1. Name the promoter group. Before the financials, name the family, trust or holding company behind the largest promoter block.
  2. List every connected company — listed and, where you can find them, unlisted — across banking, insurance, hydropower, manufacturing and trading. Do not stop at two; Nepali webs often run to five or more.
  3. Read the related-party note of each one and look for loans, guarantees and pledges running between them. Loans that recur and grow between the same two or three companies year after year are a stronger signal than one year's note.
  4. Check for pledged shares. Where disclosed, note whether promoter shares in any group company are pledged for loans, and whether the lender is itself inside the group. That is the clearest sign of a circular chain.
  5. Find the weakest link. Which group company has the most hydropower construction risk, the highest bad-loan ratio or the thinnest capital? Group stress is most likely to start there — not in the company you plan to buy.
  6. Trace the path. If the weakest link hit trouble — a stalled project, rising defaults, a failed capital raise — how would it reach your company? Check four paths: shared directors' attention and decisions, related-party loans that might need writing down, loss of depositor or customer trust, and margin calls on pledged shares.
  7. Do not add up the parts. Do not treat the group's combined market value or net worth as real, additive strength. Discount for the same money counted more than once.
VERIFICATION HABIT A group's strength in one sector is not proof of strength in another — it may be the same money stretched across both. Check each company's own solvency before letting the family name stand in for analysis.

Lesson 22.6 — The Diversification Illusion

Standard advice says spread your money across sectors — banking, hydropower, insurance, manufacturing — to reduce the risk of any one company. That advice assumes a bank share and a hydropower share are separate bets. In Nepal that fails whenever both sit under the same promoter group, because their fortunes are tied through contagion, circular financing and shared money.

Suppose you hold five shares: a commercial bank, a life insurer, a run-of-river hydropower company, a consumer manufacturer and one more. When you map the promoters, you may find that three or four of the five trace back to one family. In a real crisis for that family — a failed project, a liquidity squeeze at a linked finance company, a large default — those three or four could fall together, exactly when diversification was supposed to protect you. Your "diversified" portfolio was really one concentrated bet on one family's ability to handle trouble on several fronts at once.

This does not make group companies uninvestable. Many are well run, well capitalised and sound on their own. It means you must check diversification at the promoter-group level, not assume it from NEPSE's sector labels. Real diversification in Nepal means owning companies backed by different, unconnected groups.

ANALYTICAL HABIT Before buying any NEPSE share, map its promoter group across every other listed company you can find. Five different names on your screen may be one bet on one family.

Chapter recap

  • Nepal's promoter webs formed because banking, insurance and hydropower all needed concentrated family money; the same houses became promoters in each sector as it opened.
  • You can map a web from public documents: related-party notes, directors' other boards, promoter shareholding pages and hydropower prospectuses.
  • Webs create three hidden risks: contagion between sister companies, circular financing that counts the same rupee twice, and group value that is less than it looks.
  • Rules cover only parts of this. NRB eased share-loan and bank investment limits in 2025, a 2024 proposal to separate bank shareholders from borrowing is not confirmed as law, and the November 2025 D-SIB framework works at system level only.
  • Before buying, name the group, list its companies, read their related-party notes, check pledges, find the weakest link and trace how its trouble could reach you.
  • Diversify by promoter group, not by sector label: five shares from one family are one bet.
