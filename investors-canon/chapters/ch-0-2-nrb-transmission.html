NRB Policy → Equity Price Transmission: The Central Causal Chain

Nepal Rastra Bank does not tell anyone what the share market should do. Its job is monetary policy: setting the rules for interest rates, lending and the banking system. But in Nepal, those rules reach share prices faster and more directly than in most markets. A change NRB announces in July can show up in NEPSE within weeks.

This chapter traces each step from an NRB decision to a NEPSE price, so that you can use it as an investing tool, not just a theory.

Chapter Purpose By the end of this chapter, you should be able to take any NRB announcement and work out three things: which part of the market it will hit, how soon, and roughly how hard. That lets you adjust your portfolio before the market fully reacts — not with certainty, but with much better odds than guessing.

0.2.1 — The Full Transmission Map: Every Link in the Chain From NRB Instrument to NEPSE Price

NRB's decisions reach the market through several channels at the same time. A channel is simply a route — like the different canals that carry water from one river to many fields. The channels can strengthen or weaken each other, so you need to watch them together.

The Master Transmission Architecture

The chain runs like this: NRB decision → how banks respond → how much credit and spare cash there is → how businesses are affected → company profits and cash flows → the price investors will pay for those profits → NEPSE prices. Every link matters, and no link is instant. That delay is what gives a careful investor an edge. The market usually reacts quickly to the early links and only slowly notices the later ones.

There are four main channels:

How the Channels Interact

• They can add up. When NRB tightens several tools at once — raising the policy rate, tightening the CD ratio and cutting margin loan limits — each channel hits the market, and the effects pile on top of each other. The 2022 crash was this kind of episode.

• They can cancel out. If NRB tightens one tool but loosens another — say, it holds the policy rate but raises the CD ceiling — the overall effect is small. An investor who watches only one channel will misread NRB's overall stance.

• They take turns. Different channels lead at different stages. Early in a tightening, the margin loan channel usually hits first, because it is the fastest. Later, the credit channel slows company profits. Last, higher interest costs push up bad loans and provisions. Watch only one channel, and you will act at the wrong time.

0.2.2 — Interest Rate Channel: Policy Rate → Cost of Funds → NIM → Bank EPS → Bank Stock Price

The interest rate channel is the most discussed of the four, and the most misunderstood. Most retail investors watch the policy rate itself. What matters more is how that rate passes through banks' deposits and loans.

The Corridor as It Stands

NRB steers short-term (overnight) money through an interest rate corridor. Think of the banks of a river: the policy rate is the level NRB aims for, and the ceiling and floor are the banks that keep money rates in their channel. As of this chapter’s last check, the policy rate is 4.25 percent. The bank rate — the ceiling, at which banks borrow from NRB — is 5.75 percent. The deposit collection rate — the floor, at which banks park spare cash with NRB — is 2.75 percent. The average rate on loans between banks (the interbank rate) has been near 2.7 percent — below the floor. That tells you the system is not just loose. It is so full of spare cash that the corridor’s usual plumbing has turned upside down. When interbank rates fall under the floor, NRB stops adding money and starts soaking it up. In Poush 2082, for example, it issued Rs 200 billion of one-year NRB bonds just to absorb surplus cash, and bought large amounts of US dollars for the same reason.

For a share investor, an upside-down corridor is a strong signal. Every market force that depends on the price of money — deposit rates, margin loan rates, how attractive fixed deposits look compared with shares — is being pushed to the easy end of its range at the same time.

How Deposit and Loan Rates Adjust

When NRB changes rates, a bank's deposit costs and its loan income do not change at the same moment. To see why, you need to know how each is priced in Nepal.

• Deposits: the deposits that drive a bank's costs are mostly fixed deposits. A fixed deposit keeps its rate until it matures, often after one or two years. So deposit costs change only as old fixed deposits mature and are renewed at the new rate.

• Loans: most bank loans in Nepal are variable-rate. The rate is the bank's base rate plus a premium for the borrower's risk. The base rate is worked out from the bank's own cost of funds and is updated regularly, usually every quarter. So loan rates follow deposit costs, but a step behind.

• A limit on the gap: NRB also caps the average gap between a bank's lending and deposit rates — the interest spread, set at around 4 percentage points for commercial banks. So even in good times, margins cannot widen very far.

The result is a transition period of a few quarters in which margins can move in surprising ways. When rates fall, cheaper deposits often arrive before cheaper loans fully pass through, so margins can widen for a while. Then loan rates catch up through the base rate, and margins settle back. When rates rise, the reverse tends to happen. How big these swings are depends on each bank's mix of deposits and loans. So check each bank's reported net interest margin rather than assuming the textbook pattern.

The 2024–26 easing cycle shows this clearly. Fixed deposits written at 10–11 percent during the 2022/23 deposit shortage matured into renewals at about 5 percent. The average deposit rate fell to around 3.5 percent, and average lending rates settled near 7 percent. A bank's biggest cost fell quarter by quarter as the old deposits rolled over. But loan rates fell too, through the base rate, so margins did not widen much: unaudited results show commercial banks' net interest income rose only about 1 percent in FY 2082/83. Their profit still rose, to about Rs 69.8 arba, mainly because they recovered bad loans and needed fewer provisions. The lesson: in Nepal, cheaper deposits mostly protect bank margins during an easing cycle rather than widen them, and the bigger swing in bank profits often comes from provisions.

NIM to EPS: The Quantitative Relationship

For Nepal's commercial banks, net interest income is about 70–80 percent of total operating income. A basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point, so 10 basis points means 0.10 percent. A 10-basis-point change in NIM, held for a year, changes pre-provision operating profit — profit before money is set aside for bad loans — by roughly 3–5 percent, depending on the bank's costs and balance sheet. Operating costs and provisions usually move slowly, so most of that change reaches final profit. That is why bank shares react so strongly to margin news: a margin change of 0.20–0.30 percentage points can mean roughly a 10–15 percent change in profit. Think of a vegetable trader in Kalimati who earns Rs 3 profit on each kilo. A 30-paisa change per kilo sounds tiny, but it moves her total profit by 10 percent.

0.2.3 — Credit Channel: CD Ratio Ceiling → Lending Slowdown → Corporate Earnings → Broad Market

The credit channel works through NRB's bluntest tool: the credit-to-deposit (CD) ratio ceiling. It decides whether banks are allowed to lend more to Nepali businesses.

The Mechanism

NRB caps how much banks may lend compared with their deposits and core capital, at 90 percent. When banks hit that ceiling, they must refuse new loans, however many good borrowers are waiting. That is what happened in 2021/22. The CD ratio was pushed hard against 90 percent, interbank rates shot up, and lending stopped across the economy. Businesses could not get working capital, expansion plans stalled, and after the usual delay, company profits disappointed in almost every sector. Demand had not collapsed. The fuel line had been pinched — like a tractor that stops not because the field is finished but because its diesel supply was cut.

When the Ceiling Doesn’t Bind — and Why That’s Still Information

By mid-July 2026 commercial banks' CD ratio stood near 73 percent — about seventeen points below the ceiling. The credit channel has flipped. The ceiling no longer stops lending. What is missing is demand from borrowers and willingness from banks. Lending by banks and financial institutions to the private sector grew just 6.5 percent in FY 2082/83, while deposits grew about 13.9 percent. That is a gap of more than seven percentage points between money coming into banks and money going out as loans.

That gap is the single most useful number in Nepali macro-finance right now. It says that banks can lend and depositors are saving, but businesses are not yet borrowing at full speed. That fits an economy still repairing its balance sheets, with banks still careful after the bad-loan cycle. For a share investor, this credit gap is the upside option. The FY 2083/84 monetary policy includes revival measures: restructured loans, managing bad loans in struggling industries, and margin limits based on risk. If those measures and rising confidence turn even part of that idle lending capacity into actual loans, earnings across the whole market will grow — and today's prices assume little of that. Watch for monthly credit growth crossing 8–10 percent as the sign that it is happening.

0.2.4 — Margin Loan Channel: Collateral Cap → Forced Selling → Market-Wide Deleveraging

The margin loan channel is the most Nepal-specific of the four. It is the direct pipe from bank credit to share buying. It has also just been rebuilt, so older books and articles describe a channel that no longer works the way they say.

The Historical Mechanism

Many Nepali investors buy shares partly with borrowed money, using the shares themselves as security for the loan. When NRB and the banks loosen this channel — lending a larger share of the shares' value, offering bigger margin loans, charging lower interest — money flows into NEPSE within weeks. The 2021 boom was fed this way. In reverse it is harsh. When prices fall, the shares are worth less as security, so the bank demands more security or part repayment. This is called a margin call. Investors sell into a falling market to raise the cash, and prices fall further. The 2022 crash featured exactly this chain of forced selling.

The 2024–26 Rebuild

Four changes have reshaped this channel:

• The cap for institutions removed (July 2024): NRB scrapped the Rs 20 crore ceiling on loans against shares to institutional investors. It accepted that margin trading through brokers — a growing list of broker companies approved to run margin lending — was a better route than blunt caps.

• The cap for individuals removed (October 2025): NRB also scrapped the Rs 25 crore ceiling on share-backed loans to one person. Banks' general risk limits on lending to a single borrower still apply.

• Risk-based limits (FY 2083/84): the latest monetary policy said share-lending limits should depend on how strong each institution is. Expect well-capitalised banks and brokers to get more margin capacity than weak ones. The channel is being handed to the institutions least likely to break it.

• Cheap money: with the interbank rate near 2.7 percent and lending rates around 7 percent, borrowing to buy shares is cheaper than at any time since before the 2022 squeeze.

What to Watch

Track margin loan balances in banks' quarterly reports, the growth of broker margin lending, and the share of share-backed loans in total bank credit. Rising margin balances during a rising market make the rise stronger. Margin balances that stay high while prices fall are the classic warning: borrowed money meeting falling collateral. That combination came before the 2022 cascade. A note on discipline: this channel is fast in both directions. It is the first to pass on tightening and the first to pass on euphoria. That is why the position-sizing rules in Chapter 56 exist.

0.2.5 — Project Finance Channel: NRB Tightening → Hydro Debt Availability → COD Delay → Earnings Miss

The project finance channel is the slowest and least understood. But hydropower now makes up a large share of NEPSE's listed companies — nearly a hundred of them — so this channel decides the profits of a big and growing part of the market.

The Mechanism

A run-of-river hydropower project uses the river's natural flow rather than a large dam. It is usually financed with about 70 percent bank loans and 30 percent shareholders' money. It earns nothing until its commercial operation date (COD), when it starts selling electricity. That is often five to seven years after the loans are arranged. When NRB tightens and spare cash dries up, three things happen in order. Project loans become scarce or expensive. Construction slows to the pace at which shareholders can pay in their money. And the COD slips. A one-year delay on a 50-megawatt project means a year with no revenue while interest keeps piling up — a profit miss that reaches the listed company years after the policy that caused it. It is like building a house on a bank loan: if the loan money stops, the builders stop, but the interest clock keeps running.

The 2022–23 liquidity squeeze worked exactly this way. Hydropower developers could not find loans, CODs slipped across the sector, and the market marked hydropower shares down for missed deadlines as much as for low water. The 2024–26 flood of spare cash reversed this. With deposits growing about 13 percent a year and banks short of good borrowers, project loans became available again at the lowest rates in years. Cheap, plentiful hydropower loans are one of the quiet engines of the current recovery. Today's easy money becomes tomorrow's COD schedule.

What to Watch

Compare COD announcements with the dates promised in each prospectus (Chapter 72, the hydropower playbook, builds the full model). Watch project-loan growth in NRB's sector data, and hydropower bond issues — a revival of the corporate bond market is itself a sign that money is flowing. If you buy hydropower shares when credit is expanding, and treat COD slippage as a sign of tightening, you hold a slow-moving gauge of NRB policy.

0.2.6 — The Feedback Loop: How Falling NEPSE Prices Impair Bank Collateral and Amplify the Cycle

The four channels do not run in a straight line. They form a loop, because NEPSE prices themselves feed back into the banking system. This loop is what turns ordinary corrections into crashes, and ordinary recoveries into manias.

The Loop Mechanics

Loans against shares are only a modest slice of total bank lending, but they matter far more than their size suggests, because they tie bank credit directly to share prices. Many other borrowers — business owners and property investors — also hold shares, so their confidence and spending rise and fall with NEPSE too. When share prices fall, the collateral behind share-backed loans shrinks. Banks issue margin calls. Borrowers sell shares to meet them. Prices fall further, and the collateral shrinks again. On the way up, the same loop runs in reverse: rising prices raise collateral values, borrowing capacity and buying power. This loop is why NEPSE's swings overshoot what company profits justify, in both directions. It is like a crowded rope bridge: a little sway makes people grab the ropes and lean, which makes it sway even more.

The Mirror Image Operating Now

Right now the loop is running in the healthy direction. Policy is expanding margin lending capacity, and the banking system has so much spare cash that small shocks do not shake it. The market has tested this twice. After closing at 3,002 in July 2025, NEPSE fell to about 2,490 by October 2025, a slide that came alongside the September 2025 Gen-Z unrest. After rebounding to about 2,960 in late March 2026, it slipped to the mid-2,500s by mid-2026. Neither fall showed the clear marks of forced selling — spikes in margin calls, stress in interbank lending, panicked policy moves. Both looked more like prices cooling off in a system full of cash. Your job is to know which loop is running. A fall inside a loose system heals. A fall inside a tight system feeds on itself — like a small slip on a dry hillside compared with one on a hillside soaked by monsoon rain.

The 2024–26 Easing Sequence: A Worked Example

The latest cycle showed the table in action.

• Fast channel first. In July 2024, NRB removed the institutional margin-loan cap and cut the policy rate again (the easing had begun in July 2023). The market reacted quickly. By 15 August 2024, NEPSE had closed above 3,000 (at 3,000.81), up from the low 2,000s earlier that year.

• Medium channel next. Further cuts took the policy rate from 5.5 percent to 5.0 percent (July 2024), 4.5 percent (July 2025) and 4.25 percent (December 2025). As old fixed deposits renewed at lower rates, bank funding costs fell from early 2025. NEPSE closed at 3,002.07 on 29 July 2025, its highest close in four years.

• Slow channel last. Private-sector credit growth, the final link, reached only 6.5 percent in FY 2082/83 (7.3 percent year on year by mid-September 2026). The broad profit growth that would justify the higher prices has not yet arrived. Without it, the index fell back: to about 2,490 in October 2025, up to about 2,960 in late March 2026, and down to the mid-2,500s by September 2026, before recovering to about 2,650 by mid-November.

The lesson is in the order: tightening hits share prices before it hits profits, and easing lifts share prices before it lifts profits. An investor who knows the order acts when the policy changes — not when the profit reports confirm it a year or two later.

The One-Sentence Summary Read every NRB announcement with four questions: Which channel does it use? How fast will it work? What does it do to bank margins now? What does it do to lending later? The answers will not predict NEPSE perfectly, but they will put you well ahead of investors who never ask them.

Chapter recap

NRB policy reaches NEPSE through four channels: interest rates, credit ceilings, margin lending and project finance. Each moves at its own speed, and each affects the others. Here is where they stand now. The corridor is upside down and NRB is soaking up cash (policy rate 4.25 percent, interbank 2.7 percent, deposit rates falling as the high-rate 2022/23 fixed deposits renew). The CD ceiling has gone from a tight limit to unused room (about 73 percent against a limit of 90). The margin channel has been rebuilt: the fixed caps are gone and limits now depend on each institution's strength. And hydropower's slow project-finance channel is enjoying its easiest funding in years. In 2024–26 the fast channels lifted NEPSE above 3,000 twice, while the slow credit channel has still not caught up. Markets race ahead on the fast channels and ignore the slow ones. That is why the ordering in Table 0.2.2 is worth more to a Nepali investor than any earnings forecast.
