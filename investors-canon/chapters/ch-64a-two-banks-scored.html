Bishnu teaches mathematics at a school in Pokhara. Every month his son sends money home from Doha. After the wedding expenses and the new roof, Bishnu has about Rs 2 lakh left in his savings account. A cousin tells him: "Buy a bank share. Banks always make money."

Bishnu has two names in mind. Everest Bank's share costs about Rs 713. NIC Asia's costs about Rs 342. NIC Asia looks cheaper, because Rs 342 is less than half of Rs 713. But a lower price per share does not mean a better buy, just as a cheaper second-hand motorbike is not a better buy if the engine is broken.

So Bishnu does what Chapter 64 teaches. He gives each bank a report card with seven subjects. This page does the same, line by line, with every number shown. By the end you will see how the rubric works on real data — and where real data runs out.

Lesson 64A.1 — How These Scores Were Made

A provisional total moves the position limit one band lower. It is like a doctor's report that says "subject to the blood test": you may act on it, but carefully.

Three more choices were made, all from the rubric's own rules:

NIC Asia has subsidiaries, but its group profit series is incomplete in the sources read, so we use the bank's own series and say so. In every case where we checked, the row and the points stay the same.
  • Which year. The latest full year is FY 2082/83, which ended on 16 July 2026 (32 Asar 2083). Its Q4 figures are not yet audited. That is normal: a score uses the latest published figures, and changes if the audit changes them.
  • Which peers. Price comparisons use the 19 commercial banks listed on NEPSE. Rastriya Banijya Bank is left out because its shares are not listed.

Part 2 — Capital cushion (7 points)

A bank's capital adequacy ratio (CAR) compares its own money with its risky assets. NRB weighs each loan by its risk, so these are called risk-weighted exposures. Think of a bus: the capital is the brakes, and the risk-weighted exposures are the weight of the passengers and luggage. The heavier the bus, the stronger the brakes must be.

Capital has layers. Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) is the best kind: paid-up capital and retained profit, money that can absorb a loss on the day it happens. Tier 1 adds a few other permanent instruments. Tier 2 adds weaker items, such as subordinated debentures, which are loans to the bank that must be repaid later. For commercial banks, the Canon's minimum includes NRB's 2.5-point conservation buffer: 11% total, 8.5% Tier 1 and 7% CET1. Falling below the minimum in any one layer scores 0 and triggers the hard limit the rubric calls "capital below the regulatory minimum". On this page that phrase always means the Canon's buffer-inclusive minimum, which is stricter than NRB's base minimum of 4.5% CET1, 6% Tier 1 and 8.5% total.

BE PRECISE ABOUT WHAT "BELOW THE MINIMUM" MEANS HERE NRB's base minimums for commercial banks are 4.5% CET1, 6% Tier 1 and 8.5% total. The conservation buffer of 2.5 points sits on top. NIC Asia's 6.27% is above the 6% base, but inside the buffer. Under NRB's framework, a bank inside the buffer faces limits on distributions such as dividends; it is not in the same position as a bank below the base minimum.

The Canon's table deliberately counts the buffer as part of the minimum, because a bank that cannot pay dividends from its own core capital is not yet a safe holding for a small investor. So under the Canon the answer is 0 and the 54 cap applies. A reader who treats the buffer as separate from the minimum would score this part 3 instead (a total cushion of 0.12 point is the row "0 to under 0.5 points above") and would not apply the capital cap. The total would rise by 3 points to 36, the governance cap would still apply, and the band would not change.

The bank has proposed remedies: a 2:1 rights issue worth about Rs 7.45 billion, approved at its 28th AGM in June 2026 and sent to the Securities Board of Nepal, and Rs 5 billion of perpetual non-cumulative preference shares approved at its 27th AGM. Neither appears in the July 2026 capital table. When new capital arrives, this part must be scored again.

Lesson 64A.4 — Dimension 2: Governance & Promoter Behaviour (EBL 11, NICA 3, out of 15)

Part 1 — Promoter holding and pledging (6 points)

Promoters are the founding shareholders. In a Nepali bank their shares are a separate class that cannot be sold on the open market in the usual way. Two things are checked over three years: (A) did their share of the bank fall, or did they keep selling, without explanation, and (B) how much of their holding is pledged, meaning given to a lender as security for a loan? The score is the lower of A and B.

For both banks, the share capital grew only through bonus shares since 2021 (NIC Asia's 2:1 rights issue has not yet happened). A bonus share is given to every shareholder in the same proportion, like cutting every slice of a cake in half: the number of slices doubles, but each person's share of the cake stays the same. So the promoter percentage should not have moved, and no conversion of promoter shares to public shares was found. That supports "stable", but it is indirect, so A is labelled "estimated".

Pledging data comes from the bank's share register or annual report, from SEBON and NEPSE disclosures, or from CDSC, the central depository; none of these gave a figure for this book. When a figure is found, Chapter 64 measures it one way only: pledged promoter shares ÷ all promoter shares × 100. Dividing by all the bank's shares instead would make the same pledge look smaller, like measuring a farmer's mortgaged land against the whole village instead of against his own fields. The rubric's own rule caps an unverified pledge check at 4 of 6. That is why Everest gets 4. Read this carefully: actual promoter pledging — unknown; points the missing-data rule allows — 4 of 6. The 4 is a ceiling set by missing evidence, not a finding that pledging is low.

NIC Asia: 0. Its promoters published notices of promoter-share sales in two financial years: an auction of 714,950 shares (Chaitra 2081 to Baisakh 2082) and a sale to other promoters (May 2025), both in FY 2081/82; then an auction of 200,000 shares (January 2026), another sale (May 2026) and an auction of 45,000 shares (July 2026), all in FY 2082/83. The notices read for this page give no reason. Promoter shares bought this way stay in the promoter class, so the 51% does not change. But the table does not only look for falls. Chapter 64 defines repeated insider selling as promoters or directors selling shares, without a stated reason, in at least two of the last three years — and that row scores 0, whatever the percentage. The first version of this page scored 4 here by reading the table as a test of falls alone. That was a mistake, corrected in the review. If a stated reason for the sales is found (a family settlement, say, or a legally required sale), A goes back to 6 and the check to 4.

Two limits on what this shows. First, these are published notices of sale and auction; this page did not check that each sale was completed, so the 0 is labelled "estimated" (it was already inside the provisional count). Second, the rule scores a pattern, not a motive. Promoters sell for many reasons — personal cash needs, family settlements, transfers among themselves, a lender enforcing a pledge, or a genuine wish to reduce their stake. Keep four levels apart:

A farmer selling plots of family land may be in trouble, or may simply be paying for a daughter's wedding. You ask before you judge.

Dimension 4 — Valuation Reasonableness (EBL 7, NICA 4, of 15)

Two price measures are compared with the median of the 19 listed commercial banks on the same day, 13 Nov 2026:

  • P/E (price-to-earnings): how many years of current profit you pay for. A P/E of 20 means paying Rs 20 for every Rs 1 of yearly profit per share.
  • P/B (price-to-book): the price compared with the owners' money per share on the balance sheet. A P/B of 2 means paying Rs 2 for every Rs 1 of book value.
FIRST, PUT THE PRICE AND THE PROFIT ON THE SAME NUMBER OF SHARES Everest Bank's 5% bonus shares went "ex" on 17 Sep 2026 (1 Ashwin 2083). From that day the price is for a share that is one of 144.07 million, not one of 137.21 million. NEPSE adjusted the price from Rs 727.50 to a base of about Rs 693. Lesson 64A.11 — What Price Does the Quality Justify? A Residual-Income Cross-Check (Recorded, Not Scored)

The Canon Score answers one question: is this a business worth owning? It does not say what the share is worth. After this case study was published, a reader proposed four tools from professional bank analysis to answer that second question: hard "kill-switch" gates, a residual income valuation, a price-to-book regression across all banks, and a breakdown of ROE into its drivers. This lesson checks each proposal against the data, runs the numbers, and says what is right, what is wrong and what is already in the rubric. Nothing here changes a score.

The idea of residual income, in plain words

Suppose Bishnu's cousin owns a tea shop worth Rs 10 lakh. If he sold the shop and put the money in a fixed deposit and a few safe bonds, it would earn, say, Rs 1.2 lakh a year. That is his cost of equity — what his money could earn elsewhere for the risk he takes. If the shop earns Rs 1.5 lakh, the extra Rs 0.3 lakh is residual income: profit above what the money could earn anyway. A shop that earns only Rs 50,000 is not a "profitable shop" in this sense. It is losing Rs 70,000 a year compared with the bank.

The residual income model values a bank the same way: value = today's book value per share + the present value of all future residual income. It suits banks better than a cash-flow model, because a bank's borrowing (deposits) is its raw material, not its financing; and better than a dividend model when a bank pays no dividend, as NIC Asia does now. With a constant ROE it gives the same answer as the justified P/B formula already in Lesson 64.3.

The cost of equity: which number?

The proposal used a cost of equity of 13.5%, based on "5–10 year government bond yields around 7.5%–8.0%" plus a 5.5%–6% risk premium. The yield figure could not be confirmed. The latest figure found is a 5-year government bond yield of 6.50% in March 2026 (IMF data via CEIC), with the 91-day treasury bill at 2.47% and NRB's bank rate at 5.75% in the same month. Adding a 5.5-point premium to 6.5% gives about 12%. That is also the figure this book registered for its justified-value rival in Lesson 80.10 (12% cost of equity, 5% growth). So 12% is the base case below, and 9.5% and 13.5% are shown for comparison.

The theoretical ex-rights price (Lesson 64.4's TERP) from the 13 November close of Rs 342 is (342 + 0.5 × 100) ÷ 1.5 = Rs 261.33. Two cautions. TERP is a mechanical adjustment, not a forecast or a price target: the real price after the book closure will move with demand, sentiment and recovery hopes. And the rights shares have not yet been allotted, so every "post-rights" figure below is a scenario that assumes full subscription and ignores issue costs and any losses in the meantime.

What the numbers show — and two corrections to the proposal

  1. The bull cases were overstated. The proposal said Everest would be worth Rs 365–410 if its ROE stayed at 15.5%–16% forever. On the same model (13.5% cost of equity, 5% growth, 40% payout) the values are Rs 310 and Rs 328. Its NIC Asia bull case (Rs 161.50 at a 14% ROE) matches only if the 14% starts in year 1, and its claim that NIC Asia would be "near Rs 175" at a 9.5% cost of equity should read Rs 228. Its "about Rs 535" for Everest at 9.5% is right (Rs 534).
  2. The model does not separate the two banks the way the proposal's labels suggest. It called Everest "quality overpriced" and NIC Asia a "value trap trading 2.5 times above its value". But by the same arithmetic Everest trades even further above its value (2.7 times at 13.5%; 2.2 times at 12%). What the model shows is narrower than it looks: under these book values, these ROE paths and 5% growth, both prices are consistent with a required return of only about 8.4%–9% a year. Different paths would give different rates. It does not prove that NEPSE banks are mispriced; it shows how much optimism, or how low a required return, today's prices already contain — for both banks alike.

So where does the difference between the two banks come from? From the Canon Score, not from the valuation. A residual income value is only as good as its ROE forecast. Everest's 14%–15% path rests on five years of steady profit and the lowest bad loans in the industry. NIC Asia's path from 2.5% to 12% is a hope: it needs bad loans (9.26% gross) to fall and the Rs 14.87 billion accumulated loss to be worked off. The score tells you how much to trust the forecast; the valuation tells you what the price assumes. Like a land deal in Chitwan: the survey tells you whether the plot floods; the price per kattha tells you whether you are paying for a dry plot anyway.

How much rides on the long-run ROE?

The biggest assumption in any residual income model is the return the bank earns after the forecast years, for ever. A few good years do not prove a bank can keep earning 14% indefinitely. Keeping Everest's five-year path the same and changing only the long-run ROE:

At a 12% cost of equity, a long-run ROE of 10% would make Everest worth less than its own book value (Rs 243), because every rupee kept in the bank would earn less than shareholders require. At Rs 713, the price is above every value in this table: it needs a required return below 9.5%, or a long-run ROE above 14%, or both.

NIC Asia's path needs the same caution from the other side. Its rise from 2.5% to 12% ROE is not built from forecasts of bad loans, provisions, interest margins or loan growth; it is simply assumed. A proper recovery model would ask how fast gross NPL falls from 9.26%, how much more must be provided for, how long the Rs 14.87 billion accumulated loss takes to clear, and how much of the new capital loan growth would use up. None of that is modelled here, so NIC Asia's value range should be read as "what the price assumes", not "what the bank is worth".

WHAT THE RIGHTS MONEY DOES TO NIC ASIA'S CAPITAL If all Rs 7.46 billion arrives and the bank's risk-weighted exposures stay at the mid-July level (Rs 234,705 million), Tier 1 rises from 6.27% to about 9.4% and total capital from 11.10% to about 14.3%. That is above the Canon's 8.5% Tier 1 minimum. At the next quarter-end after allotment, the capital hard limit would lift, and the capital part would score 3 (not 7, because of the past breach). The governance cap from the promoter sales would stay. This is arithmetic on today's figures, not a forecast: the money may be lent out, which raises risk-weighted exposures, or partly lost to new provisions. It also depends on the shares actually being subscribed and allotted, on NRB counting the proceeds as core capital, and on issue costs. A rights issue can repair the capital shortfall; it does not repair the bad loans that caused it.

The price-to-book map of all listed banks

The proposal suggested plotting ROE against P/B for all banks, to find banks that look cheap for their returns. There are 20 commercial banks but only 19 are listed (Rastriya Banijya Bank is not), so the map has 19 points: FY 2082/83 ROE, and P/B at the 13 November 2026 close on matched share counts.

Three things stand out.

  • One year's ROE explains little. The fitted line explains only 16% of the differences in P/B. Prices also reflect expected recovery, dividend habits, ownership and float, and plain popularity.
  • Almost every bank sits above its justified P/B. At a 12% cost of equity and 5% growth, a bank needs an ROE of about 12% just to be worth its book value. Only four banks earned that in FY 2082/83 (KBL, EBL, SANIMA and SCB), yet every bank trades at more than 1.0 times book. This is the same low implied hurdle seen above.
  • The "cheapest" bank on the map is a warning, not a bargain. Kumari Bank (KBL) has the highest ROE (18.0%) and one of the lowest P/Bs (1.35). But its profit jumped from Rs 1.81 billion to Rs 7.38 billion in one year; a jump that size is exactly what the Canon's profit-record and write-back checks are built to question. A regression alone would call it mispriced. NIC Asia sits well above the line (P/B 1.98 on a 0.65% ROE): the market is paying for a recovery it has not yet seen.

The map is a good way to ask questions, and a poor way to answer them. It is recorded here and in the case-study file, not scored.

The hard gates: mostly already in the rubric

The proposal said an additive score has a blind spot: "a bank could score 65 while violating a central bank mandate". In version 1.5.0 it cannot. A lender below its minimum in any capital layer scores 0 on capital and is capped at 54 (Lesson 64.5) — which is exactly what happened to NIC Asia. CAMELS: close, but not the same

The proposal described the Canon as matching CAMELS, the supervisors' bank rating (Capital, Asset quality, Management, Earnings, Liquidity, Sensitivity to market risk). The overlap is real for C, A, M (partly, through governance) and E. But two letters are missing. The Canon's "Liquidity" dimension measures how easily the share trades on NEPSE, not whether the bank can meet withdrawals: the bank's own liquid assets, its credit-to-deposit ratio and its reliance on large or institutional deposits are not scored. And "Sensitivity" — how a change in interest rates or the exchange rate would hit profit — is not scored at all. Both are recorded for Chapter 65's bank table.

ROE broken into its parts

The proposal's last idea is a DuPont-style breakdown: split ROE into interest margin, fees, costs, credit cost (provisions as a share of loans) and leverage, so two banks with the same ROE are not treated as equally safe. That is a good habit, and it is recorded here as a diagnostic. One piece can be checked now: Everest's credit cost in FY 2082/83 was Rs 272.9 million on average customer loans of about Rs 224.5 billion — 0.12%, close to zero, which supports the proposal's description. The proposal's other two claims — that Everest also has a low cost-to-income ratio, and that NIC Asia's small FY 2082/83 profit came "solely" from lower impairment charges — were not checked here: the full-year income statements needed were not assembled. They stay unverified.

Where this leaves the method. The proposal's summary is right in spirit: use the Canon Score as the quality and safety filter, and a valuation such as residual income as the entry-price check. The book already keeps the two apart — the score never claims to be a valuation, and justified P/B is recorded, not scored. This lesson shows the fuller version on two real banks, with every input in the case-study file, so a reader can change the cost of equity or the ROE path and see the answer move.

Case study recap

  • Everest Bank: 74 (Business Quality 57, Share Attractiveness 17), provisional. High, steady ROE (15.08%), the lowest bad loans in the industry, every dividend paid in real cash — but a thin capital cushion, a full price (2.9 times book) and many items public data could not verify. Ceiling: Strong band, one band down because provisional — Adequate, 1.5%–3% at most, built in tranches.
  • NIC Asia Bank: 33 (Business Quality 19, Share Attractiveness 14), provisional and capped twice. A loss year, Tier 1 capital of 6.27% (below the Canon's 8.5%), gross NPL of 9.26%, a shrinking loan book, no real cash dividend in five years, two late AGMs and repeated published promoter-sale notices with no stated reason. Ceiling: no new money.
  • Read every rule's definition, not just its table row. This page's first version missed the definition of "repeated insider selling" and gave NIC Asia 4 points it had not earned. A review caught it.
  • A lower share price is not a cheaper share. Compare prices with profit and book value, on the same number of shares.
  • Labels matter. Fact, derived, estimated and unverified figures are scored differently, and too many unverified points make a total provisional.
  • Quality and price are separate questions. A residual income check shows the market prices both banks as if shareholders needed only about 8.4%–9% a year. The score says which ROE forecast deserves trust; the valuation says what the price assumes.
  • Real data found real gaps in the rubric. They are recorded for the next version, not patched in quietly.
  • A cap is a record, not just a number. NIC Asia's three flags each have a rule, dated evidence and a condition for lifting. The 54 never hides the reason.
