READ THIS FIRST This page scores two real listed banks, Everest Bank Ltd (EBL) and NIC Asia Bank Ltd (NICA), with the Canon Score rubric version 1.5.0 from Chapter 64. It is a teaching exercise. It is not investment advice, and it is not a recommendation to buy, hold or sell either share.

Every figure comes from public sources: the banks' own quarterly reports and capital disclosures, NEPSE price data and market news sites. Each one is listed, with its source and how sure we are of it, in canon-case-ebl-nica-v1.5.0.csv. The points come from the book's reference calculation, canon_case_ebl_nica_v1_5_0.py, so anyone can re-run them.

Dates. Data cut-off 23 Nov 2026 (7 Mangsir 2083). Share prices: close of Friday 13 Nov 2026 (27 Kartik 2083). Both scores will change when prices, reports or the rubric change. These scores are not part of the frozen forward record in Chapter 80.

Bishnu teaches mathematics at a school in Pokhara. Every month his son sends money home from Doha. After the wedding expenses and the new roof, Bishnu has about Rs 2 lakh left in his savings account. A cousin tells him: "Buy a bank share. Banks always make money."

Bishnu has two names in mind. Everest Bank's share costs about Rs 713. NIC Asia's costs about Rs 342. NIC Asia looks cheaper, because Rs 342 is less than half of Rs 713. But a lower price per share does not mean a better buy, just as a cheaper second-hand motorbike is not a better buy if the engine is broken.

So Bishnu does what Chapter 64 teaches. He gives each bank a report card with seven subjects. This page does the same, line by line, with every number shown. By the end you will see how the rubric works on real data — and where real data runs out.

Lesson 64A.1 — How These Scores Were Made

Before the numbers, four words. They tell you how much to trust each figure. Think of buying vegetables at the Kalimati market: some you weigh yourself, some you take on the seller's word, and some you cannot check at all.

LabelWhat it meansHow it is scored

UnverifiedA figure the rubric needs but this book could not check.Capped. The rubric's missing-data rule allows no more than the middle row of the table (the lower of the two middle rows when there are four), unless the table sets its own limit.

One more rule matters a great deal. If the points that rest on unverified or estimated figures add up to more than 20, the whole total is marked provisional. A provisional total moves the position limit one band lower. It is like a doctor's report that says "subject to the blood test": you may act on it, but carefully.

Three more choices were made, all from the rubric's own rules:

  • Which accounts. For a bank's capital, bad loans and loan growth, we use the bank's own figures as reported to Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB), the central bank. For profit and EPS the rubric prefers group (consolidated) accounts. Everest Bank has no subsidiary, so its group and bank figures are the same. NIC Asia has subsidiaries, but its group profit series is incomplete in the sources read, so we use the bank's own series and say so. In every case where we checked, the row and the points stay the same.
  • Which year. The latest full year is FY 2082/83, which ended on 16 July 2026 (32 Asar 2083). Its Q4 figures are not yet audited. That is normal: a score uses the latest published figures, and changes if the audit changes them.
  • Which peers. Price comparisons use the 19 commercial banks listed on NEPSE. Rastriya Banijya Bank is left out because its shares are not listed.

Lesson 64A.3 — Dimension 1: Financial Strength & Profitability (EBL 18, NICA 3, out of 20)

Part 1 — Three-year average ROE (8 points)

ROE (return on equity) is profit for the year divided by the owners' money in the business. If you put Rs 100 into a tea shop and it earns Rs 15 in a year, its ROE is 15%. The rubric uses the average of the opening and closing equity for each year, then averages three years.

A BOUNDARY CASE: EVEREST BANK IS ONLY JUST OVER THE LINE Everest's average, 15.08%, is only 0.08 of a point above the 15% line. If the audited FY 2082/83 profit came in about Rs 85 million lower (1.7%), the average would fall below 15% and the score would drop from 8 to 6. Write this down next to the score. A rule with a sharp line will always have companies sitting right on it, like a student who passes with exactly the pass mark.

Part 2 — Capital cushion (7 points)

A bank's capital adequacy ratio (CAR) compares its own money with its risky assets. NRB weighs each loan by its risk, so these are called risk-weighted exposures. Think of a bus: the capital is the brakes, and the risk-weighted exposures are the weight of the passengers and luggage. The heavier the bus, the stronger the brakes must be.

Capital has layers. Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) is the best kind: paid-up capital and retained profit, money that can absorb a loss on the day it happens. Tier 1 adds a few other permanent instruments. Tier 2 adds weaker items, such as subordinated debentures, which are loans to the bank that must be repaid later. For commercial banks, the Canon's minimum includes NRB's 2.5-point conservation buffer: 11% total, 8.5% Tier 1 and 7% CET1. Falling below the minimum in any one layer scores 0 and triggers the hard limit "capital below the regulatory minimum".

Everest Bank. Its total cushion is thin, 0.78 of a point at mid-October. That is why it does not get 7. Its Tier 1 has also slipped, from 10.87% in July 2024 to 10.42% in July 2025, 9.58% in April 2026 and 9.98% in July 2026. The October quarter's Tier 1 was not found, so the 5 is labelled "estimated". At 9.98% it has 1.48 points of Tier 1 room, so a breach in three months would be a surprise.

NIC Asia. This is the most important finding on the page. Its Basel III disclosure for mid-July 2026 shows Tier 1 capital of Rs 14,711 million against total risk-weighted exposures of Rs 234,705 million. That is 6.27%. It has no other Tier 1 instrument, so its CET1 is also 6.27%. The total of 11.10% passes only because Tier 2 — mainly Rs 7,221 million of subordinated debt and Rs 2,522 million of general loan-loss provisions — fills the gap. In bus terms, the main brakes are weak and the hand-brake is doing a lot of the work.

Why did Tier 1 fall so far? Retained earnings in the July 2026 capital table are minus Rs 14,865 million. Losses and provisions have eaten into the bank's own money. The same table adds an extra 4% of risk-weighted exposures under NRB's Pillar II, on a line that reads "overall risk management policies and procedures are not satisfactory". That add-on makes the required capital larger.

BE PRECISE ABOUT WHAT "BELOW THE MINIMUM" MEANS HERE NRB's base minimums for commercial banks are 4.5% CET1, 6% Tier 1 and 8.5% total. The conservation buffer of 2.5 points sits on top. NIC Asia's 6.27% is above the 6% base, but inside the buffer. Under NRB's framework, a bank inside the buffer faces limits on distributions such as dividends; it is not in the same position as a bank below the base minimum.

The Canon's table deliberately counts the buffer as part of the minimum, because a bank that cannot pay dividends from its own core capital is not yet a safe holding for a small investor. So under the Canon the answer is 0 and the 54 cap applies. A reader who treats the buffer as separate from the minimum would score this part 3 instead (a total cushion of 0.12 point is the row "0 to under 0.5 points above") and would not apply the 54 cap. The total would rise by 3 points to 40; the band would not change.

The bank has proposed remedies: a 2:1 rights issue worth about Rs 7.45 billion, approved at its 28th AGM in June 2026 and sent to the Securities Board of Nepal, and Rs 5 billion of perpetual non-cumulative preference shares approved at its 27th AGM. Neither appears in the July 2026 capital table. When new capital arrives, this part must be scored again.

The rubric marks a company's total "provisional" while its accounts have been materially restated, until a full year of new audited figures exists. Strictly, these are audit-stage changes to published unaudited results, not a restatement of earlier audited accounts. This page treats changes of this size as "material" — a judgement, written down here so you can disagree with it. NIC Asia's total is provisional for another reason as well (Lesson 64A.9), so the judgement does not change the result.

Lesson 64A.4 — Dimension 2: Governance & Promoter Behaviour (EBL 11, NICA 7, out of 15)

Part 1 — Promoter holding and pledging (6 points)

Promoters are the founding shareholders. In a Nepali bank their shares are a separate class that cannot be sold on the open market in the usual way. Two things are checked over three years: (A) did their share of the bank fall without explanation, and (B) how much of their holding is pledged, meaning given to a lender as security for a loan? The score is the lower of A and B.

BankPromoter / publicA: change in 3 yearsB: pledgedPoints

NICA51.0% / 49.0%No fall found (estimated) → 6Not found → at most 44

For both banks, the share capital grew only through bonus shares since 2021 (NIC Asia's 2:1 rights issue has not yet happened). A bonus share is given to every shareholder in the same proportion, like cutting every slice of a cake in half: the number of slices doubles, but each person's share of the cake stays the same. So the promoter percentage should not have moved, and no conversion of promoter shares to public shares was found. That supports "stable", but it is indirect, so A is labelled "estimated".

Pledging data comes from CDSC, the central depository, and was not available to this book. The rubric's own rule caps an unverified pledge check at 4 of 6. That is why both banks get 4.

NIC Asia's promoters published several notices of promoter-share sales between April 2025 and July 2026, including an auction of 200,000 shares in January 2026 and of 45,000 shares in July 2026. The notices read for this page do not give a reason. Promoter shares bought this way stay in the promoter class, so the bank's 51% promoter share does not change, and the rubric's holding test (which looks for falls) is not triggered. A stricter reader might call repeated sales "insider selling" and score 0. Either way, ask the question: why are founders selling while the bank is short of core capital?

Dimension 3 — Liquidity & Tradability (both 10 of 10)

Liquidity asks: can you sell when you need to? A shop on New Road sells faster than one in a back lane. The measure is the average value traded each day over six months — here 14 May to 13 Nov 2026, which had 115 NEPSE trading days.

Dimension 4 — Valuation Reasonableness (EBL 7, NICA 4, of 15)

Two price measures are compared with the median of the 19 listed commercial banks on the same day, 13 Nov 2026:

  • P/E (price-to-earnings): how many years of current profit you pay for. A P/E of 20 means paying Rs 20 for every Rs 1 of yearly profit per share.
  • P/B (price-to-book): the price compared with the owners' money per share on the balance sheet. A P/B of 2 means paying Rs 2 for every Rs 1 of book value.
FIRST, PUT THE PRICE AND THE PROFIT ON THE SAME NUMBER OF SHARES Everest Bank's 5% bonus shares went "ex" on 17 Sep 2026 (1 Ashwin 2083). From that day the price is for a share that is one of 144.07 million, not one of 137.21 million. NEPSE adjusted the price from Rs 727.50 to a base of about Rs 693. Lesson 64A.6 — Dimension 5: Sector & Business Model Durability (EBL 10, NICA 7, out of 15)

Part 1 — Moat (8 points)

A moat is the water around a fort: something that keeps competitors out. For a bank, the rubric asks for evidence, not a story. The top row needs ROE at or above the sector median in at least four of the last five years, and gross bad loans (NPL, non-performing loans) at or below the class average in those same years.

Everest's NPL was the lowest, or second-lowest, of all commercial banks in each of these years, so the NPL test is met. The ROE test passes in four of five years. But only the last year uses the exact method (a median of 19 banks, with average equity). NIC Asia fails both tests: its ROE was below the median in FY 2080/81, 2081/82 and 2082/83, and its gross NPL at mid-July 2026 was 9.26% against an industry average of 5.44%. The table's text then offers "moderate barriers, or licence/regulated position without proof", worth 5, because a banking licence is still a barrier to entry. So NIC Asia scores 5.

A GAP IN THE RUBRIC, REPORTED — NOT FIXED HERE The moat table gives the same 5 to a bank with no evidence and to a bank whose evidence shows it has lost its advantage. That cannot be right. A doctor would not write the same note for "not yet tested" and "tested and failed". This page follows the rule as written and records the gap for the next rubric version (Lesson 64A.10). With a 2 instead of 5, NIC Asia's total would be 34.

Part 2 — Credit concentration (7 points)

This asks how much of the loan book sits in one sector, segment or region — like a farmer who plants only tomatoes. Neither bank's sector-wise loan table could be read from the public files used here. The missing-data rule allows at most the lower of the two middle rows: 2 points each, unverified. This is the biggest single gap on the page, worth up to 5 points for each bank. Both banks' annual reports contain the table; reading it is the first job for anyone improving these scores.

Lesson 64A.7 — Dimension 6: Growth Trajectory (EBL 12, NICA 4, out of 15)

For a bank, fast growth is not automatically good. Lending too quickly is how bad loans are born. So the rubric compares each bank's five-year loan growth with the whole commercial-bank system and rewards staying close to it. Growth is measured as CAGR (compound annual growth rate), the steady yearly rate that would take the first figure to the last.

The adjustment steps, in order:

NIC Asia's loan book shrank by more than a fifth while the system grew. Some of that is deliberate repair, but the table does not ask why: 2 points.

Lesson 64A.8 — Dimension 7: Dividend & Capital Return Discipline (EBL 6, NICA 2, out of 10)

Nepali banks pay two kinds of dividend. A bonus share dividend gives you more shares but no money. A cash dividend puts money in your bank account. When a bank gives bonus shares, it must also pay a small cash amount to cover the 5% tax on the bonus. The rubric counts a year as "paid" only when the cash is more than that tax amount — real cash, not tax cash.

Everest Bank — consistency 4 of 6. Every year had real cash. But the cash payout — cash dividend divided by distributable profit — was inside the book's preferred 30%–70% range in only one year. The row "every year with payout outside 30%–70% in 2 or more years" gives 4.

WHICH "DISTRIBUTABLE PROFIT"? A SECOND GAP IN THE RUBRIC The rubric says to use the NRB-format distributable profit. But the figure printed in that statement is a running total: Sharesansar's report on Everest's FY 2078/79 results says its distributable profit "stands at Rs 3.89 arba till now". It includes profit kept from earlier years, like a savings box that is never emptied. Measured against one year's new distributable profit instead (roughly 79% of net profit after the 20% general reserve and the 1% social fund), Everest's payout would be 36%, 42%, 22%, 47% and 34% — inside the range in four of five years, which would score 6. The rule does not say which to use. This page uses the printed figure, because that is what the rubric names, and records a 2-point uncertainty.

Everest Bank — source and reason 2 of 4 (unverified). The money comes from genuine profit, and reserves grew every year, which fits the top row. But the top row also needs a stated reason for keeping profit back, and none was found in the sources read. (Capital is the obvious reason: Everest's cushion is thin, and bonus shares turn reserves into permanent paid-up capital.) Unverified gives the middle row, 2. Total: 6.

NIC Asia — consistency 0 of 6.

  • FY 2078/79: no dividend, although the printed distributable profit was positive (Rs 3,427 million).
  • FY 2079/80: 29% bonus and 1.5% cash. The 1.5% is the tax on the bonus (29 × 5 ÷ 95 = 1.53%), so it is not real cash.
  • FY 2080/81 and 2081/82: no dividend. The printed distributable figure was negative: minus Rs 2,304 million and minus Rs 10,678 million.
  • FY 2082/83: none declared so far, with the printed figure at minus Rs 14,865 million. By the rubric's rule, "no profit and no dividend" is a year without a real cash dividend.

Zero real-cash years gives 0. The rubric has a softer rule, "retained by design" (4 and 2 points), for a bank a regulator stops from paying. It is not used here. Its purpose is to avoid punishing a healthy bank caught by a rule. NIC Asia cannot pay because of its own losses, and the rubric's specific rule for negative distributable profit already covers that.

NIC Asia — source and reason 2 of 4. No dividend was drawn from capital, so the 0 row ("drawn from capital or reserves") does not fit. The top row does not fit either, because reserves fell sharply. The middle row lists other failures. Following the rubric's rule for a case that fits no row, the score is the lower of the two nearest rows (4 and 2): 2. This is a third small gap: the table has no row for "paid nothing, and reserves shrank". Total: 2.

Why both totals are provisional

The rubric adds up the full points of every part that rests on unverified or estimated figures.

  • EBL: 46 points. Capital cushion 7 (October Tier 1 not read), promoter 6 (pledging), audit 5 (latest report not read), board 4 (quarterly dates partly checked), free float 5, moat 8, concentration 7, dividend source 4.
  • NICA: 23 points. Promoter 6, audit 5, free float 5, concentration 7 — plus the audit-stage changes to published profit.

Both are above 20, so both totals are provisional. This is an honest result, not a failure of the exercise. Public web data can show profit, capital, prices and dividends well. It cannot easily show pledged shares, locked-in shares or the sector split of the loan book. Those sit in the annual report and in CDSC's records. Most banks scored only from public websites will come out provisional — and that is exactly what the rule is for.

What the Score Tells Bishnu

The score does not tell Bishnu to buy Everest Bank. It tells him three things. First, Everest is the better business by a wide margin: 57 of 75 against 23 of 75. Second, he would be paying a full price for that quality: 2.9 times book value. Third, until the open items are checked, the Canon's ceiling for Everest is a small satellite holding, 1.5%–3% of his share money, not a large core position. For NIC Asia, the Canon says no new money until its capital is rebuilt and the score is redone. The cheaper price per share told him nothing.

Lesson 64A.10 — What This Case Taught Us About the Rubric

Scoring real companies is the best test of a rulebook. These points came out of this exercise. None changes rubric 1.5.0 today, because a rule should not be changed to suit two examples. They are recorded for the next version, which Lesson 80.10 says must be tested before it is adopted.

  1. Moat table: "no proof" and "proof against" both score 5. A bank that fails the ROE and NPL tests should probably score lower than one with no record.
  2. Distributable profit: say whether the payout ratio uses the printed running total or one year's new distributable profit. For Everest the answer moves the score by 2 points.
  3. Source-and-reason table: add a row for a company that paid nothing while its reserves shrank.
  4. "Restatement": say whether large audit-stage changes to published unaudited results count as a material restatement.
  5. Per-share figures after bonus shares: state in the rubric that price, EPS and book value must use the same share count. The frozen Chapter 80 record (version 1.2-P, 17 Nov 2026) used FY 2082/83 per-share figures before the bonus shares that seven banks issued after mid-July. Its P/E median was 19.54; on matched shares it is 20.52, and the P/B median (1.535) is unchanged. The record stays frozen, as its rules require; this note is the disclosure.
  6. Capital minimum: the Canon counts NRB's 2.5-point buffer as part of the minimum. That is a deliberate choice, but it should be stated beside every score it decides, as it is here.
  7. Data access: pledged promoter shares and loan-sector concentration decide up to 13 points and are rarely on public websites. A score built only from websites will usually be provisional.

Case study recap

  • Everest Bank: 74 (Business Quality 57, Share Attractiveness 17), provisional. High, steady ROE (15.08%), the lowest bad loans in the industry, every dividend paid in real cash — but a thin capital cushion, a full price (2.9 times book) and many items public data could not verify. Ceiling: Adequate, 1.5%–3%.
  • NIC Asia Bank: 37 (Business Quality 23, Share Attractiveness 14), provisional and capped. A loss year, Tier 1 capital of 6.27% (below the Canon's 8.5%), gross NPL of 9.26%, a shrinking loan book, no real cash dividend in five years and two late AGMs. Ceiling: no new money.
  • A lower share price is not a cheaper share. Compare prices with profit and book value, on the same number of shares.
  • Labels matter. Fact, derived, estimated and unverified figures are scored differently, and too many unverified points make a total provisional.
  • Real data found real gaps in the rubric. They are recorded for the next version, not patched in quietly.
