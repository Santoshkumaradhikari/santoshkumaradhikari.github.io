Nepal’s Credit Cycle: The Hidden Equity Cycle

Why Part 0 Comes Before Part I

Most investing books begin with the share market itself. This one begins with what drives the share market. In Nepal, the link between the wider economy and share prices is unusually direct. Once you understand that link, you can read the market far better than most investors do.

In big markets like the United States or India, prices respond to many forces at once: company profits, global fund flows, investor mood, interest rates and more. No single force decides the direction.

NEPSE is different.

On NEPSE, one institution — Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB), the central bank — and one variable — how much money is available to lend and borrow — explain more of the index's big moves than any other single force. Look at the record. The 2014–16 bull run followed a lending boom, when banks lent freely. The 2021 boom came from a flood of cheap money after COVID-era easing. The 2022 crash came when NRB tightened its lending rules. And the 2024–26 rise was driven by deposits and falling interest rates. Remittances poured in faster than ever before, and NRB cut its policy rate (the main interest rate it sets) from 5.5 to 4.25 percent, even though bank lending stayed weak. Where that money comes from — remittances, the trade balance and foreign exchange reserves — is the subject of Chapter 4.

Think of the Bagmati in monsoon. Its level depends far more on the rain upstream than on any single boat floating on it. NRB policy is the rain. Individual companies are the boats.

But rain is not the only thing that moves boats, and the other forces have grown stronger. In 2024–26, hydropower shares, waves of new IPOs and the end of promoter lock-in periods (when founders become free to sell their shares) also pushed the index hard — sometimes in a different direction from bank lending. So treat NRB as the strongest current in the river, not the only one.

Why is the NRB link so strong? Four features of Nepal's market explain it.

• Financial firms are a big part of the market. Market capitalisation means the total value of all of a company's shares at today's price. In mid-March 2026, banks, development banks, finance companies, microfinance institutions and insurers together made up about 52 percent of NEPSE's total market capitalisation. Commercial banks alone were about 24 percent. Their weight used to be even bigger: in 2007, commercial banks alone were about two-thirds of the market. As of mid-July 2026, NRB licensed 20 commercial banks, 17 development banks, 16 finance companies and 51 microfinance institutions. These numbers keep shrinking as mergers continue, so always check NRB's latest list. Bank profits depend mainly on three things: how fast loans grow, the gap between what banks earn on loans and pay on deposits (the net interest margin), and how many loans go bad. NRB policy shapes all three.

• There is no short selling and there are no derivatives. Short selling means betting that a price will fall; derivatives are contracts such as futures and options. Without them, a NEPSE investor cannot profit directly from a falling share price. The return on an ordinary shareholding comes from dividends received and from a rise in the share price; in a falling market, the only protection is to sell or to hold cash. Most trading is done by ordinary retail investors. When loans are cheap and easy to get, they buy. When credit tightens, they sell or stop buying.

• Nepal's economy runs on remittances and imports. Money sent home by workers abroad fills bank deposits, and much of what people buy comes from abroad. So NRB's decisions travel quickly through the economy: NRB rules → bank behaviour → spending and business activity → company profits → share prices.

• Margin lending magnifies every move. Margin lending means borrowing from a bank, against shares you already own, to buy more shares. It makes rises bigger on the way up and falls sharper on the way down.

Part 0 does not replace Parts I to XVIII. It provides the big framework inside which all the other analysis sits. Learn three things here: Nepal's credit cycle, the channels through which NRB policy reaches the market, and the four liquidity regimes. Then every ratio, financial statement and valuation in this Canon will make more sense to you. A reader who skips this Part will never fully get that context.

Core Thesis Share prices follow liquidity — how much spare cash is looking for a home. Liquidity follows deposits and credit. NRB has the biggest say over both. To understand why NEPSE does what it does, start here.

Introduction to the Credit Cycle

The credit cycle is the repeating pattern of lending booms and lending squeezes. It is the master cycle in Nepal's economy. The earnings cycle, the valuation cycle and the liquidity cycle all follow from it. An investor who reads it alongside the liquidity signals — deposit growth and the interbank rate — can usually tell which phase the market is in. As the next lesson shows, it is the liquidity signals that tend to give the earliest warning.

This chapter lays the foundation, using both data and ideas. It tests the link between credit, liquidity and the market against eleven years of NRB data, and explains how the link works. It shows you exactly where to find the data. It is honest about what the evidence does and does not prove. And because this book is meant to be used, not just admired, it ends by reading the latest data, as of November 2026.

0.1.1 — Bank Lending Growth vs. NEPSE Index: What the Data Show and Why the Link Exists

The most useful chart a Nepali investor can study puts a few lines side by side: NRB's figures for how fast loans to the private sector are growing, how fast deposits are growing, the interbank rate, and the NEPSE index. These lines are connected. But they are not connected in the simple way most market talk assumes, so this lesson starts with what the data actually show.

What the Data Actually Show (2015–2026)

To test the link, this book used 44 quarter-end readings, from mid-October 2015 to mid-July 2026, taken from NRB's Current Macroeconomic and Financial Situation reports. It is the same dataset used to test the cycle settings in Chapter 80, Lesson 80.8. For each quarter it records private-sector credit growth, deposit growth, the interbank rate and the NEPSE index.

The test uses a correlation. A correlation is a number between −1 and +1 that shows how closely two things move together. +1 means they always move together, −1 means they always move in opposite directions, and 0 means there is no link. As a rough guide, anything below about 0.3 either way is weak.

Three lessons come out of this table.

• Credit growth is not an early warning. It tends to move with the market or after it. Look at the two biggest credit booms. Credit growth hit 32 percent in mid-January 2017, six months after NEPSE's July 2016 peak, when the index was already falling. It hit 31.7 percent in mid-October 2021, two months after the August 2021 peak. In both cases, record credit growth was a sign that the boom was late, not early.

• Liquidity leads. Deposit growth and the interbank rate — measures of how much spare cash is in the system — gave earlier signals than credit growth. That is why this book's core rule says price follows liquidity first.

• Direction matters more than level. Whether credit growth is speeding up or slowing down tracked the market more closely than the growth rate itself.

Read These Numbers Carefully Eleven years of quarterly data hold only about two and a half full market cycles, and each 12-month change overlaps the next. So these correlations are evidence, not proof. Correlation also does not show cause: a link between two lines does not tell you which one moved the other. Treat the table as a guide to which signals deserve your attention, and re-check it as new data arrive.

When NRB tightens, share prices often react within weeks — long before the yearly figure falls. So do not wait for the credit number to confirm a squeeze. Watch NRB's policy announcements and the interbank rate. By the time the yearly credit figure drops, the market has usually already moved.

Why the Link Exists: The Structural Explanation

Credit, liquidity and share prices are tied together by three chains.

1. Financial firms are a big part of the index. When lending grows faster, bank profits grow, through more interest income and more fees. When lending slows, bank profits shrink. Because financial firms make up about half of NEPSE's value, the index moves a lot with the banking cycle — the way a trailer follows wherever the tractor pulling it goes.

2. Margin lending creates a loop. NRB allows banks to lend against listed shares. When credit is easy, loan limits are generous and interest rates are low. This creates a loop: cheap credit → investors borrow to buy shares → share prices rise → the shares are worth more as collateral → investors can borrow more → they buy more. When credit tightens, the same loop runs backwards.

3. Retail investors follow credit. Most NEPSE trading comes from ordinary investors whose finances depend on easy credit. When banks lend freely, a salaried worker can take a loan and buy shares. When banks stop lending, the same worker becomes a seller.

These chains also explain why credit growth peaks late. Loans keep growing for a while after a boom starts to fade, because borrowers draw down loans they arranged earlier, and investors borrow more to hold on to falling shares. So the credit figure keeps climbing even as the market turns.

The Stress Test: What 2024–26 Added to the Evidence

The latest cycle tested this link in a surprising way. It is worth studying closely, because it sharpens the rule rather than breaking it. First, here is what the index actually did:

• From the low 2,000s in early 2024, it rose to just above 3,000, closing at 3,000.81 on 15 August 2024 (31 Shrawan 2081) — a gain of nearly half.

• It fell back, then climbed again to 3,002.07 on 29 July 2025 (13 Shrawan 2082), its highest close in four years.

• It then slid to about 2,490 in October 2025, rebounded to about 2,960 in late March 2026 (2,960.40 on 24 March), and ended the fiscal year at 2,597.80 in mid-July 2026.

Through these years, bank lending grew slowly. In FY 2082/83 (2025/26), the year to mid-July 2026, lending by banks and financial institutions to the private sector grew only 6.5 percent. (NRB's broader monetary survey, which counts credit a little differently, shows 6.6 percent.) Lending that weak would not normally come with a market near 3,000. So was the link broken?

No. It was overtaken. Deposits grew 13.9 percent in FY 2082/83 (2025/26), driven by record remittance inflows of roughly Rs 2,363 billion (Rs 23.63 kharba). Demand for loans was weak, and the banking system's credit-to-deposit ratio — the share of deposits that banks have lent out — sat near 74 percent. So a flood of fresh deposits had nowhere to go. Deposit rates collapsed. Fixed deposits made at 10–11 percent in 2022/23 (2079/80) matured and were renewed at near 5 percent. All that money went looking for a better return. In Nepal, the only large, easy-to-trade, nationwide place for such money is the share market. The index rose on liquidity — plenty of spare cash — not on credit. It is like a village where every family suddenly has extra savings but nobody is building a house: the money ends up chasing whatever is for sale.

That is why the second half of the story matters. By September 2026 the index had eased back to the mid-2,500s, and it stood near 2,650 in mid-November. A rally driven by spare cash can lift prices quickly. A lasting rise usually needs company profits to grow as well, and in Nepal broad profit growth usually needs credit growth. The market ran ahead of the credit cycle and has been digesting the gap. The refined rule is this: liquidity can start a rally; whether it lasts depends on profits, and in Nepal that usually means credit. Watch both, and treat any gap between them as stored energy — it can be released in either direction.

0.1.2 — Remittance → Deposit → Lending Multiplier: How External Inflows Become Domestic Credit

Nepal's credit does not begin in Nepal. It begins in Kuala Lumpur, Doha, Dubai, Riyadh and Seoul — in the wages of several million Nepali workers. Every month that money arrives home as remittances. Remittances become bank deposits, and deposits give banks money to lend. Understand this pipeline and you understand where Nepal's credit cycles come from.

The Multiplier, Step by Step

The mechanics are simple enough to follow over tea. A worker in Malaysia sends home Rs 25,000. His family deposits Rs 15,000 in the bank and spends Rs 10,000 at the local sabzi mandi. The shopkeeper deposits her takings too. The bank now holds new deposits. NRB's credit-to-deposit (CD) ceiling, in use since it replaced the older CCD ratio in 2021–22, says a bank's loans may not exceed 90 percent of its deposits. (The exact calculation includes some adjustments set out in NRB's directives.) Roughly speaking, then, each Rs 100 of new deposits allows up to about Rs 90 of new loans. But the ceiling is a maximum, not a target. Banks must also meet NRB's rules on capital, cash reserves and risk, and they need borrowers who want to borrow. Those loans are paid to contractors, traders and suppliers, who deposit the money again, and that supports more lending. This chain is called the deposit-to-credit multiplier. Its fuel comes from outside Nepal.

In real life the multiplier is smaller than this simple chain suggests, for two reasons. First, part of every loan leaks out of Nepal. When a trader borrows to import goods from India or China, that money goes abroad. It does not come back as a new deposit — like water seeping out of an irrigation canal before it reaches the next field. Second, banks rarely lend right up to the 90 percent ceiling. In mid-July 2026, commercial banks' CD ratio was only about 73 percent, because borrowers were not asking for loans. So treat 90 percent as the upper limit, not as what actually happens.

The 2025/26 (2082/83) Surge: The Multiplier at Record Speed

In the eight months to mid-March 2026, workers' remittances rose 37.7 percent in rupee terms (31.0 percent in US dollars) to Rs 1,449.65 billion (Rs 14.5 kharba). The full fiscal year closed at roughly Rs 2,363 billion (Rs 23.63 kharba). Three things helped: Malaysia's labour market reopened, wages in the Gulf rose, and more workers sent money through official banking channels instead of informal hundi. The balance of payments — the total of all money flowing into and out of Nepal — showed a surplus of about Rs 658 billion (Rs 658 arba) over those eight months. Foreign exchange reserves grew enough to pay for about 18.5 months of imports by mid-March 2026, and 19.6 months by the end of the fiscal year. That is nearly triple the seven-month level NRB treats as safe.

Then came the historic part. For most of Nepal's modern history, the problem was too few dollars. In Poush 2082 (December 2025–January 2026), NRB did the opposite of its usual job. It issued Rs 200 billion (Rs 200 arba) of one-year NRB bonds to soak up spare cash from the banking system, and it bought large amounts of US dollars to absorb the flood of foreign currency. The central bank was no longer pumping money in. It was mopping money up. Pause on that, because it reverses what most Nepali market commentary assumed for twenty years.

The Two Gears Think of the remittance multiplier as a machine with two gears. The first gear is deposit growth, driven by money from abroad. The second gear is lending speed, driven by how much borrowers want loans and how much banks may lend. In 2025/26 (2082/83) the first gear spun at record speed while the second barely turned: deposits grew 13.9 percent, loans only 6.5 percent. When the first gear is fast and the second is slow, the market is full of stored energy. Watch the second gear.

0.1.3 — Credit Expansion Lag to EPS Growth: Why Bank Earnings Follow the Credit Cycle With a Delay

Lending growth drives bank profits, but with a delay. A common rule of thumb puts the delay at six to twelve months. That rule comes from how loans earn interest, explained below. This book has not yet tested it on bank-by-bank data, and as the 2025 example shows, other forces can move bank profits too. So treat six to twelve months as a rough guide, not a law. Knowing why the delay exists is more useful than memorising its length.

The Lag Mechanism

A loan made today earns interest for years. So when lending growth speeds up from 10 to 20 percent, banks earn interest on both the old loans and the new ones. Their net interest income keeps rising for six to twelve months. Fee income — commissions, guarantees, trade finance — follows lending almost at once, but it is the smaller part of profit. The reverse also holds. When lending stalls, old loans keep paying interest, so profits fall slowly at first. Then they fall faster, as funding costs catch up and bad loans appear. It is like a mango orchard. Trees planted today bear fruit only after some years, and an orchard you stop tending keeps fruiting for a while before the harvest drops.

What the 2025 Earnings Recovery Actually Proved

The 2024–26 period showed a subtler point: bank profits can start rising before lending growth does. Through 2025 and into 2026, lending growth stayed weak (6.5 percent in FY 2082/83 or 2025/26), yet bank profits began to recover. Here is what actually did the work, according to banks' unaudited results for FY 2025/26 (2082/83).

• Cheaper funding protected margins, but did not lift income much. The average interbank rate (the rate banks charge each other for short-term loans) spent 2025/26 (2082/83) near 2.7 percent, and old high-rate fixed deposits were renewed at rates five to six percentage points lower. But loan rates fell too, so commercial banks' net interest income rose only about 1 percent, to about Rs 192.4 arba.

• Lower provisions did most of the work. Provisions are money banks set aside to cover bad loans. Late in the year, banks recovered many bad loans. That let commercial banks cut their provisions from about Rs 44.7 arba to Rs 37.3 arba. The cut drove almost all of the rise in their combined net profit, to about Rs 69.8 arba. This happened even though non-performing loans (NPLs — loans that borrowers have stopped repaying) kept rising across the system. They went from about 1.3 percent in mid-July 2022 to 3.9 percent in 2024, 4.6 percent in 2025 and about 5.7 percent in mid-2026. Recoveries on old bad loans can lift profit before the NPL ratio itself turns down. When the ratio does peak, bank earnings per share (EPS — profit divided by the number of shares) can bounce back further from a low base.

• Policy help is promised. The FY 2083/84 (2026/27) monetary policy, announced in July 2026, committed to managing bad loans in struggling industries and helping stressed borrowers revive their loans. In effect, the regulator is helping to ease the provisioning burden.

Here is the lesson for an investor who wants to be six to twelve months ahead of the crowd: bank profit cycles bottom when bad-loan provisions peak, not when lending growth is at its lowest. In 2025, bank earnings recovered while lending grew at barely half its usual pace. Be careful, though: provisions can also fall because of loan-restructuring relief or because collateral is written back, not only because loans have truly got healthier. Part VI looks at how to tell the difference. The two cycles are related but not identical. Track them separately.

0.1.4 — Credit Contraction and NPL Spike: The Inevitable Phase-Shift and Its Equity Consequences

Every boom ends. In Nepal, the end usually comes not as a slow fade but as a sudden change of phase. Tighter policy turns comfortable borrowers into struggling ones after a delay of three to five quarters. Then the accounting system, which is slow to recognise bad loans, spreads the pain across years. This section explains that shift. Chapter 0.3 turns it into a framework for positioning your portfolio.

The Phase-Shift Mechanism

During booms, Nepali borrowers rarely repay term loans. They renew them instead. Because of this renewal habit, the true quality of a bank's loans shows only when new lending dries up. When NRB tightens — a lower CD ceiling, costlier money, stricter margin rules — banks stop renewing. A borrower who was never really paying the loan from his business income must now repay from somewhere. Usually that means borrowing from another bank, then another. It is like a family that keeps borrowing from the next moneylender to repay the last one: it works until no one will lend. The stress finally shows up as an NPL months later.

The 2022–24 episode was the textbook case. Tightening began in 2021/22 (2078/79). The CD ratio was pushed toward its 90 percent ceiling, interbank rates shot up, and margin lending was squeezed. After the usual delay, the system-wide NPL ratio climbed from about 1.3 percent in mid-July 2022 to 3.9 percent by mid-July 2024, and kept rising to about 5.7 percent by mid-July 2026. Provisions cut into earnings for two years in a row. Price-to-book multiples (share price divided by book value per share) across the banking sector fell to multi-year lows. Microfinance suffered most, because its borrowers have the thinnest cushion of formal income.

The Recognition Lag You Can Trade

Here is the detail worth real money. Nepali loan accounting recognises trouble late, because renewing or restructuring a loan delays calling it bad. So the reported NPL ratio peaks well after the real stress has peaked. Many investors trade on reported numbers, so selling tends to be heaviest around the time reported bad loans look worst — after the real credit turn has already happened. That was the pattern in 2022–24; it is an observation from one cycle, not a law. You can get ahead of this by tracking the early-warning signs that move before the NPL ratio. Watch renewal and restructuring requests. Watch fixed-deposit maturity walls — a borrower refinancing from 11 percent to 5 percent is healthier than his accounts suggest. Watch sector-specific signs of stress, and the policy language around loan revival. Then you can position for the turn in earnings while the reported NPL ratio still looks ugly. In 2024–25, that was exactly the window in which the re-rating began. It is like a patient whose fever has already broken while the last lab report still says "sick".

0.1.5 — Reading the NRB Credit Growth Data: Where to Find It and What Signal to Extract

None of this framework is usable unless you can read the data yourself, at the source, for free. This lesson shows you where to look.

The Documents That Matter

• NRB’s Current Macroeconomic and Financial Situation — published every month, about six weeks after the month ends, with a full-year edition each August. This is the most important regular document in Nepali investing. It puts remittances, deposit growth, private-sector credit growth, interbank rates and reserves in one place.

• The Monetary Policy Statement — announced each July for the coming fiscal year. NRB reviews it around Mangsir (November–December), again around Falgun (February–March), and once more before the year ends. Every change to the CD ratio, margin lending or the interest rate corridor discussed in this Canon was announced in one of these. The interest rate corridor is the band NRB sets around its policy rate, inside which it tries to keep the interbank rate. At present the floor is 2.75 percent and the ceiling 5.75 percent — like the two banks of a river that keep the water in its channel.

• NRB’s banking statistics and the quarterly results of listed banks — for NPL ratios, provisions and net interest margins, bank by bank.

Here is how to read it. Spare cash is still plentiful, and deposits are still growing fast. Lending is slow but has started to pick up. Bad loans are high and have not yet clearly peaked. The market has given back part of a large gain driven by spare cash. Put together, this says recovery phase with repair-phase scars. One new warning light has come on: inflation has climbed above 6 percent, driven largely by dearer fuel after the 2026 war involving Iran pushed oil above $100 a barrel (Chapter 6 traces that shock). Rising prices are the main reason a central bank stops cutting rates and starts raising them, so watch NRB's next policy reviews closely.

Two numbers will decide what comes next. If lending growth climbs past 8–10 percent while deposits stay strong, company profits should broaden and the recovery can continue. If inflation keeps rising and NRB turns cautious, or deposit growth slows while lending stays weak, the spare cash that powered the rally starts to drain — and the stored energy works in reverse.

Chapter recap

Nepal's share market sits downstream of its banking system, and its banking system sits downstream of remittances and NRB policy — like fields that depend on a canal, which depends on the river. Eleven years of NRB data sharpen that picture. Liquidity — deposit growth and the interbank rate — tended to move first. Credit growth tended to move with the market or after it, and record credit growth was a late-cycle warning, not a buy signal. Bank profits follow lending with a delay, but other forces, such as recoveries of bad loans and lower provisions, can lift them sooner. Financial firms make up about half of NEPSE's value, and margin loans magnify every move, so this cycle moves the index strongly. Other forces — hydropower, IPO supply and lock-in expiries — also matter, and their weight has grown. The 2024–26 episode showed both sides: spare cash carried NEPSE just above 3,000 twice, but without credit growth the gains did not hold. Keep track of how sure each claim is, re-test the evidence every year, and use the dashboard to know which phase the data describe. Let that guide your positioning, rather than guesses.
