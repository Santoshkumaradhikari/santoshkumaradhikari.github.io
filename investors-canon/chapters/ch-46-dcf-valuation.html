WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Present value — what a future rupee is worth today, given a chosen rate. Discount rate — the yearly return you require, used to shrink future cash to today's money. Free cash flow — cash left after running and maintaining the business. WACC — weighted average cost of capital: the blended cost of debt and equity, used as a discount rate (not the tax term in Chapter 35). Beta — how much a share's price tends to move with the market. Terminal value — the value of all cash flows after the forecast years.

Lesson 46.1 — The Logic of Discounting: Why a Rupee Tomorrow Is Worth Less Than a Rupee Today

Today or next year?

Every DCF rests on one simple human feeling: money promised in the future is worth less than the same money in your hand now.

Say a friend offers you NPR 100,000 today or NPR 100,000 in exactly one year, and you trust the friend completely. You would almost certainly take it today. Why? Because you could put it in a fixed deposit, earn a year's interest, and have more than NPR 100,000 by the time the friend's payment would have arrived. Money today can be put to work; money promised for later cannot work until it arrives.

This is the time value of money: a sum is worth more the sooner you get it, because of what it can earn in the meantime.

DCF: the same idea, applied to a whole business

Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) valuation is that everyday feeling applied carefully, year by year, to a whole business. Instead of "what is NPR 100,000 next year worth today?", it asks "what is this company's whole stream of future cash — this year, next year and on into the distant future — worth to an investor today?" The analyst does two things:

  1. Forecast the spare cash the business should produce each year. This is free cash flow (FCF): the cash left after paying for operations and reinvesting what is needed to keep running and growing.
  2. Convert each year's cash into today's rupees using a discount rate — the yearly return an investor could reasonably earn elsewhere for taking a similar risk. It works like an exchange rate between a rupee tomorrow and a rupee today.

The result is the present value of that future cash: what it is worth today, once you allow for both the passing of time and the risk that the cash might not arrive, or might arrive in a different amount.

The fixed-deposit example

Suppose a Nepali fixed deposit pays 8% a year. If you were promised NPR 108 in one year, that promise is worth exactly NPR 100 today — because NPR 100 in the deposit today grows to NPR 108 in a year. NPR 100 is the present value of NPR 108 at an 8% discount rate.

Now suppose the promise is riskier — it comes from a private company, not a government-backed bank, and there is some chance the money never comes. You would not pay NPR 100 today for it. You would want a higher return for the extra risk, so you would pay something less than NPR 100 for the same promised NPR 108.

That is why a DCF's discount rate is not simply "whatever the bank pays". It must reflect the risk of the particular cash being valued. A government treasury bill and a small unlisted hydropower company's shares are different kinds of promise, and cannot be discounted at the same rate.

KEY CONCEPT Present value is the amount of money today that equals, at a chosen discount rate, a specific amount at a specific future date. Discounting is simply the arithmetic of shrinking a future rupee amount to today's equivalent, using the formula PV = FCF ÷ (1 + r)^n, where r is the discount rate and n is the number of years ahead.

The full recipe

  1. Forecast free cash flow for each year of an explicit forecast period (often five to ten years).
  2. Discount each year's cash back to today at the right rate.
  3. Add up those present values.
  4. Add the present value of everything the business should produce after the forecast period — one lump sum called terminal value, explained in Lesson 46.4.

The total is the DCF's estimate of what the business, or one share of it, is truly worth today. Compare it with the market price. If the DCF value per share is clearly above NEPSE's price, the share may be undervalued; if clearly below, it may be overvalued.

Chapter 45 warned that "may be" is doing a lot of work there. A DCF is an estimate built on assumptions, not a certificate of truth. This chapter comes back to that warning often, because in Nepal's market the assumptions matter even more than usual.

PRACTICAL TOOL A quick way to sanity-check any DCF: work out what discount rate and growth rate the current market price already implies, then ask whether those implied assumptions are more or less reasonable than your own. If a share's price only makes sense with 20% growth forever, that is useful information whatever your own model says.

Why DCF matters for Nepali investors

Nepal's market runs mostly on relative valuation — comparing one bank's price-to-book with another's, or one hydropower share's P/E with the sector average. That is quick and easy. But it only tells you whether something is cheap or dear compared with its neighbours. It says nothing about whether the whole neighbourhood is fairly priced.

DCF lets you step outside the crowd's mood and ask a deeper question: based on the cash this business is likely to make over its life, and what that cash is worth in today's rupees, what should I be willing to pay? It is slower and harder. It is also the discipline that separates serious investing from following tea-shop tips about which hydropower counter is about to "run".

Lesson 46.2 — Free Cash Flow: The Real Number a DCF Runs On

Profit is not cash

Before discounting, you need a number to discount — and it is not accounting profit. Net profit, the "bottom line" most Nepali retail investors focus on, is not the same as cash.

Net profit can be lifted by non-cash accounting entries. And it ignores the cash a company must spend on new equipment, extra working capital or loan repayment just to keep going and growing. Free cash flow strips all that away and asks a blunter question: after running the business and reinvesting what it needs, how much real, spendable cash is left for the people with a claim on it?

Think of a farmer whose harvest is worth Rs 5 lakh on paper. If Rs 2 lakh must go back into seed, fertiliser and a new pump, only Rs 3 lakh is truly free for the family.

Two kinds of free cash flow

DCF models use two versions. Mixing them up is one of the most common mistakes, so define both carefully.

Free Cash Flow to the Firm (FCFF) is the cash the business makes for all providers of money — shareholders and lenders — before any interest or loan repayments. It is what the operating business throws off, whatever way it is financed. It usually starts from operating profit and:

  • adds back depreciation — the accounting charge that spreads the cost of a long-lived asset, such as a hydropower plant's turbines, over its useful life, even though the cash was spent up front;
  • subtracts capital expenditure (capex) — cash spent buying, building or upgrading long-term assets such as power plants, factory machines or bank branches;
  • subtracts the change in working capital — cash tied up in day-to-day needs such as stock, money owed by customers, and money owed to suppliers.

Free Cash Flow to Equity (FCFE) is the cash left for shareholders alone, after the company has paid its lenders — after interest and after any net loan repayments (or plus any net new borrowing). It is what shareholders could in theory receive as dividends without harming the company's ability to keep operating and growing. In formulas:

FCFE = FCFF − interest expense × (1 − tax rate) − net debt repayments

or, built up from net profit:

FCFE = Net Profit + Depreciation − Capex − Change in Working Capital + Net Borrowing

KEY CONCEPT FCFF is discounted at the Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) — a blended rate reflecting the cost of both debt and equity — to give the value of the whole firm (enterprise value), from which debt is then subtracted to reach equity value. FCFE is discounted directly at the cost of equity to give equity value per share. Never discount FCFF at the cost of equity, or FCFE at WACC. Mismatching the type of cash flow and the discount rate is the single most common DCF error, and it quietly distorts the answer.

Which one to use

For most non-financial NEPSE companies — manufacturers, hotels, hydropower developers, traders — either can work. FCFE is often more practical for a share investor because it gives equity value (and value per share) directly, without subtracting net debt at the end.

FCFE is also more natural when the company's capital structure — its mix of debt and equity — should stay fairly stable. That is reasonable for a mature manufacturer. It is a poor assumption for a hydropower project in its debt-heavy early years, when loans are being repaid from operating cash and the mix shifts every year. There, FCFF with WACC can be cleaner: it separates the plant's cash-earning power from the shifting debt on top, and only at the end subtracts the (shrinking) net debt to get equity value.

The big exception: banks

For banks and financial institutions in Nepal, FCFE is close to mandatory. For a commercial or development bank, customer deposits are not "debt" in the normal sense. They are the raw material of the business, not a financing choice on top of it. Interest paid to depositors is an operating cost of banking, not a financing cost to add back. An FCFF for a bank is a distorted, almost meaningless number.

So for banks, practitioners use a version built on FCFE — often approximated as net profit minus the capital the regulator requires the bank to keep to support its loan growth. Nepal Rastra Bank's capital adequacy rules effectively force banks to reinvest part of profit as regulatory capital instead of paying it all out. This matters a lot, because bank and financial shares make up one of the largest blocks of NEPSE's market value.

The data problem

A second Nepal-specific issue is the quality of the past figures you start from. Forecasting future free cash flow begins with understanding past free cash flow, built from several years of audited accounts.

Many Nepali companies outside the tightly regulated banking and insurance sectors have had uneven disclosure:

  • working capital movements scattered inconsistently across the notes;
  • related-party transactions (dealings with companies linked to the owners) not always shown separately;
  • depreciation policies that sometimes change between years without clear flagging.

For a Nepali manufacturer or trader, expect to spend real time rebuilding a clean, consistent five-year history of FCFF or FCFE before forecasting a single year. Skipping this and forecasting straight from one reported "net profit" figure is one of the fastest ways to build a DCF that looks precise but is quietly wrong.

WARNING A DCF is only as reliable as the free cash flow figures feeding it. If the historical rebuild is sloppy — inconsistent working capital, unexplained swings in capex, related-party flows that distort real operating cash — every later number in the model inherits the error, however carefully it is discounted.

Lesson 46.3 — Building the Discount Rate: Cost of Equity in a Frontier Market

If free cash flow is the top of the DCF fraction, the discount rate is the bottom. In a market like Nepal's, the bottom is where most of the real difficulty — and most of the judgment — lies.

The cost of equity is the yearly return shareholders need to be paid for the risk of owning a particular share. (The cost of debt is simpler: the interest rate lenders charge.) The standard tool for estimating the cost of equity is the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM):

Cost of Equity = Risk-Free Rate + Beta × Equity Risk Premium

Each of the three pieces needs real judgment for a NEPSE company, so take them one at a time.

Piece 1: the risk-free rate

The risk-free rate is the return on an investment with practically no risk of default — usually the yield on a government security, since a government that prints its own currency is assumed (rightly or wrongly) never to default on debt in that currency.

In Nepal, the natural stand-ins are Government of Nepal Treasury Bills (short-term, under a year, auctioned regularly by Nepal Rastra Bank for the government) and Development Bonds (longer-term government securities).

The trouble is that these yields have swung wildly in recent years. Not because the government's credit risk changed much, but because of swings in bank liquidity (how much spare cash banks hold). When banks are flush with deposits and have few borrowers, they pile into treasury bills and push yields right down. When cash tightens, yields jump.

Nepal's weighted average treasury bill rate went from double digits in the 2022 liquidity crunch to a much lower single-digit range in the 2024 and 2025 cash glut, and stayed low into 2026 — the 91-day bill was about 2.6 percent around mid-2026, as reported with NRB's Monetary Policy for FY 2083/84, and 1.01 percent by mid-October 2026 (NRB). A "risk-free rate" that can move five or more percentage points in two years, for reasons unrelated to the government's real creditworthiness, is not a clean input.

REGULATORY DETAIL Nepal Rastra Bank regularly publishes the weighted average treasury bill rate from its auctions, along with 91-day, 182-day and 364-day figures. For a DCF, use a longer-term government yield (or an average T-bill rate over several recent quarters) rather than the latest single auction, because one data point can be distorted by a temporary cash shortage or glut rather than showing a lasting "risk-free" rate.

Piece 2: the equity risk premium

The equity risk premium (ERP) is the extra return, above the risk-free rate, that investors as a whole demand for holding a spread of shares instead of the risk-free asset. It pays for the general riskiness of shares.

Mature-market ERPs, built from many decades of US and other developed-market history, are commonly put at around 4–6%. Nepal is not a mature market, and NEPSE has nothing like that long, high-quality return history.

So practitioners in frontier and emerging markets usually start with a mature-market ERP and add a country risk premium — an extra slice for the added risk of that particular country. A widely used approach, popularised by NYU professor Aswath Damodaran, takes the country's credit rating or bond default spread and scales it up by how much more volatile its share market is than its bond market.

Nepal is unrated or low-rated by the big international agencies, has capital controls limiting foreign portfolio investment, and has fairly low market liquidity. So its country risk premium is large, often several percentage points on top of the mature ERP. In practice, a Nepali company's cost of equity, built honestly this way, tends to land well above a similar company's in a developed market — often in the low-to-mid teens, even before the company's own particular risks.

Piece 3: beta

Beta measures how much a share's returns move compared with the whole market's:

  • beta 1.0 — moves in line with the market;
  • above 1.0 — exaggerates market moves;
  • below 1.0 — steadier than the market.

In deep, liquid markets, beta comes from a regression (a statistical comparison) of a share's past returns against the index over several years of daily or weekly data.

NEPSE has a serious problem here. Many listed shares trade thinly, some going days or weeks without a trade. Daily circuit breakers (limits on how far a price may move in one session) further squash and distort the price record. A beta regression on such a share is working on prices that often do not reflect a real, continuously trading market — only whatever price cleared on the few days a trade happened, inside the circuit limits.

So raw betas for many NEPSE shares are unreliable. They can look falsely low (a share that barely trades seems not to move much, hiding its true risk) or jump about from one period to the next.

CAUTION Do not trust a raw regression beta from NEPSE price history without scrutiny, especially for thinly traded counters. A beta of 0.3 for a heavily indebted hydropower developer, taken from a share that traded on only forty days last year, almost certainly understates its true sensitivity to economic and sector risk. It is measuring illiquidity, not safety.

Two workarounds

Practical Nepali analysts usually use one or both of these:

  1. Bottom-up beta. Instead of using the Nepali company's own erratic prices, start from the betas of similar listed companies in markets with better data (for example, listed hydropower or manufacturing companies in India or other South Asian markets). "Unlever" each one to remove the effect of that company's own debt (more debt makes shares riskier). Average them to get an industry beta. Then "relever" it using the Nepali company's own debt-to-equity mix.
  2. Sensible judgment brackets. Treat regulated, essential-service businesses with steady demand — established commercial banks, operating hydropower plants with signed long-term PPAs — as lower beta (perhaps 0.7–0.9). Treat cyclical, optional-spending or debt-heavy businesses — hotels and tourism, construction, early-stage hydropower still facing river and construction risk — as higher beta (perhaps 1.1–1.4). Defend the choice openly in your write-up instead of hiding behind a falsely precise regression number.

Putting it together

With rough, illustrative figures:

  • a risk-free rate of about 7% (a smoothed, multi-quarter average of longer-term Nepali government yields, deliberately ignoring the recent cash-driven swings);
  • a mature-market ERP of about 5%;
  • a Nepal country risk premium of about 4–5%;
  • a beta of 1.0 for a mid-risk operating business.

Together these give a cost of equity of about 16–17%. A lower-risk regulated utility with beta 0.8 might land nearer 14–15%; a riskier, debt-heavy cyclical business with beta 1.3 might be 18% or more.

These are illustrative ranges, not fixed rules. The discipline is to build the number openly from its parts, state every assumption, and then test the final value against a sensible range of discount rates (Lesson 46.6) — rather than presenting a falsely precise figure like "14.73%" as if it were an observed fact rather than a judgment.

When you use FCFF: WACC

For FCFF valuations, the discount rate is WACC rather than the cost of equity alone. WACC blends the cost of equity with the after-tax cost of debt (the interest rate the company really pays, reduced because interest is tax-deductible), weighted by how much of the company is financed by equity and by debt at market value.

The cost of debt is easier to find for a Nepali company: it can usually be read from the interest rates on its bank loans and debentures in its accounts. For heavily indebted hydropower projects, look harder. Some hydropower loans carry cheap or subsidised rates (sometimes through targeted refinancing schemes), which can understate what the company would pay for new debt today.

Lesson 46.4 — Terminal Value: Valuing the Business Beyond the Forecast Horizon

No analyst can believably forecast a company's cash line by line, year by year, forever. So most DCFs forecast a limited number of years — often five, sometimes up to ten for a business still growing towards a steady state, such as a hydropower plant in its early operating years. Everything after that is squeezed into one figure, the terminal value, calculated at the last forecast year and then discounted to today like any other cash.

There are two standard ways to calculate it. A careful analyst works out both and uses each to check the other.

Method 1: the Gordon Growth Model

The Gordon Growth Model (or perpetuity growth method) assumes that after the forecast, free cash flow grows at a steady, modest rate forever:

Terminal Value = FCF in the first year after the forecast period ÷ (Discount Rate − Perpetual Growth Rate)

The growth rate must be cautious and sustainable. No company can grow faster than the whole economy forever — it would end up bigger than the economy, which is impossible. So the rate should sit near the country's expected long-run nominal GDP growth (real growth plus inflation). For Nepal, given its growth record and inflation history, about 4–6% is a commonly defensible anchor, to be revisited as the outlook changes.

Using much more — 8% or 10% forever — is one of the commonest ways, deliberate or not, to inflate a DCF. Terminal value is extremely sensitive to the gap between the discount rate and the growth rate, as the next lesson shows in numbers.

Method 2: the exit multiple

The exit multiple method assumes the business could be sold at the end of the forecast at a price based on a multiple seen among similar companies — such as EV/EBITDA (enterprise value divided by earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, a common stand-in for operating cash power) or a P/E.

It has the advantage of resting on something closer to real market prices. But in Nepal it has a specific weakness. It needs a decent set of comparable companies with believable, liquid prices. NEPSE's comparables in any narrow sub-sector — a handful of hydropower developers, a few hotels, a cluster of similar banks — are few, and their multiples swing with the same illiquidity and mood discussed throughout this book. An exit multiple taken from a thinly traded, mood-driven peer group can smuggle back into the "intrinsic" value exactly the market mispricing a DCF is meant to see past.

WARNING In most real DCFs — and especially in Nepal, where forecast periods are short compared with the very long life of assets like hydropower plants — terminal value often makes up 60% to 80% or more of the total value. So the most influential number in the whole model is often the one built on the least certain, longest-dated assumptions. Scrutinise the terminal value assumptions at least as hard as the near-term forecast, not less.

Why hydropower needs special care

This has a clear lesson for the hydropower and cyclical businesses covered earlier. A hydropower plant's cash is not a smooth, rising line. It depends on:

  • hydrology — the seasonal pattern of river flow, high in the monsoon months (roughly Shrawan to Ashwin) and much lower in the dry winter and pre-monsoon months;
  • the PPA tariff structure agreed with the Nepal Electricity Authority — the near-monopoly state utility that buys power from most of Nepal's independent producers, usually under long-term, fixed or season-based tariffs;
  • the loan repayment schedule.

A well-built hydropower DCF models these year by year through the forecast: cash rising as construction loans are repaid, wet- and dry-season swings, and a step-change when — often twenty to thirty years from commissioning — the PPA tariff or the licence itself may reset or expire. It does not smooth all this into one constant growth rate forever.

Nepali hydropower licences are usually issued for a fixed number of years, after which ownership arrangements can change. For an asset with a limited licensed life, a Gordon Growth terminal value assuming cash forever is not just imprecise — it can be conceptually wrong. A careful model may value only the remaining licensed life, plus a reasoned view (not an assumption) of what happens at expiry. It is like valuing a shop on a 30-year lease: you count the years you can trade, not forever.

CASE IN POINT Nepal's operating hydropower companies — long-established plants of the kind tracked by market data providers — show the terminal value problem clearly. Once a plant is built and its PPA is running, its yearly cash becomes fairly predictable within a wet-season/dry-season band. But the plant's total value depends hugely on assumptions about tariff renewal, licence extension and major refurbishment spending decades ahead — exactly the long-dated, hard-to-forecast judgments that terminal value is forced to squeeze into one number.

Lesson 46.5 — A Worked Example: Discounting a Nepali Company Step by Step

The company

The mechanics are easiest to absorb through a full example. Take an imaginary company, Himalaya Hydro Power Ltd. (HHPL): a mid-sized run-of-river hydropower company, like the counters on NEPSE's hydropower sub-index, with 3 crore shares and a long-term PPA already signed with the NEA.

Its plant has run for several years. Its construction loan is being steadily repaid, so its free cash flow to equity (FCFE) should grow as loan payments fall, before settling into slow, steady long-run growth once the loan is mostly repaid and output stabilises.

The inputs

Suppose the analyst has done the work in Lessons 46.2 and 46.3:

  • rebuilt HHPL's past FCFE from several years of audited accounts;
  • forecast FCFE five years ahead, reflecting falling loan payments;
  • built a cost of equity of 14.5% with CAPM — a risk-free rate of about 7%, a combined equity and country risk premium for Nepal's frontier status, and a beta of about 0.8 for HHPL's moderate business and debt risk;
  • chosen a terminal growth rate of 4%, anchored to a cautious view of Nepal's long-run nominal GDP growth.

Reading the table step by step

  1. Discount each year. Multiply each year's FCFE by its discount factor, 1 ÷ (1.145)^n for year n. For example, Year 3's NPR 18 crore is worth NPR 11.99 crore today after three years of discounting at 14.5%.
  2. Add the five years. The five present values sum to NPR 56.22 crore.
  3. Work out the terminal value. Take Year 5's FCFE of NPR 21 crore and grow it one more year at 4%: NPR 21.84 crore. Divide by the gap between the discount rate and growth rate (14.5% − 4% = 10.5%). That gives NPR 208.00 crore, as of the end of Year 5.
  4. Discount the terminal value. It is a Year-5 amount, so discount it back five years at 14.5%: NPR 105.69 crore today.
  5. Add up. NPR 56.22 crore + NPR 105.69 crore = NPR 161.91 crore total equity value.
  6. Per share. Divide by 3 crore shares: about NPR 54.00 per share.

What to notice

As Lesson 46.4 warned, the terminal value's present value (NPR 105.69 crore) is about 65% of HHPL's total estimated value. Most of what this valuation says the company is worth rests on one assumption about growth forever after Year 5, discounted through one assumption about the long-run discount rate.

This is not a flaw in this particular example. It is built into DCF for almost any ongoing business. That is exactly why Lesson 46.6 now tests how sensitive the NPR 54.00 figure is to reasonable changes in those two assumptions.

Lesson 46.6 — Sensitivity, Fragility, and the Practical Limits of DCF in Nepal

One number is not enough

The HHPL example produced one clean number: NPR 54.00 a share. On its own, it carries an unearned air of precision, as if the analyst had found a hidden truth.

A disciplined DCF never stops at one number. It immediately asks how much the answer moves when the two most powerful assumptions — the discount rate and the terminal growth rate — are changed within a reasonable range. This is sensitivity analysis, and it is arguably more useful than the base case itself, because it shows how much of the conclusion comes from the analyst's judgment rather than the business.

Keeping HHPL's FCFE forecast fixed and changing only those two rates gives these values per share:

Discount Rate ↓ / Terminal Growth Rate →2%4%6%
12.5%NPR 57.5NPR 67.3NPR 83.1
14.5% (base case)NPR 47.8NPR 54.0NPR 63.1
16.5%NPR 40.7NPR 44.9NPR 50.7

The spread is striking

The discount rate varies by only four percentage points (12.5% to 16.5%, both defensible depending on how you estimate beta and the country risk premium). The growth rate varies by only four points (2% to 6%, both defensible depending on your view of Nepal's long-run growth). Yet HHPL's value per share swings from about NPR 40.7 to about NPR 83.1 — more than double from bottom to top.

An investor who took only the NPR 54.00 base case at face value could be lulled into false confidence. The honest way to present the result is not "HHPL is worth NPR 54.00 per share" but "under reasonable assumptions, HHPL appears to be worth somewhere between roughly NPR 40 and NPR 85 per share, with NPR 54 as a central estimate". Then compare that whole range, not just the middle, with NEPSE's market price.

CAUTION A DCF can be made to give almost any answer simply by nudging the discount rate down half a point and the terminal growth rate up half a point. The changes look trivial on a spreadsheet but add up to a huge swing in the final value. Always run and report a sensitivity table with any DCF conclusion, and be especially suspicious of a DCF — yours or anyone's — that conveniently confirms a price target the analyst was already hoping to justify.

Beyond these two rates, three Nepal-specific challenges affect how much weight to put on any DCF at all.

Challenge 1: data quality

As Lesson 46.2 said, a DCF looks forward but stands on a backward-looking foundation. You must first understand several years of a company's real, cash-based history. For many NEPSE companies outside banking and insurance, that foundation is harder to build cleanly than for a similar company in a market with longer-established, more consistently enforced disclosure and audit standards.

Challenge 2: currency and inflation

Nepal has had periods of clearly higher inflation than the developed economies on which most valuation textbooks and default assumptions are based. And the Nepali rupee, though pegged to the Indian rupee, is indirectly exposed to wider currency and external pressures — import dependence, remittance inflows and foreign exchange reserves, discussed elsewhere in this book.

A DCF must be built consistently in either:

  • nominal terms — cash flows and discount rate both include expected inflation; or
  • real terms — both exclude it.

Mixing them — for example, discounting inflation-adjusted "real" cash flows at a nominal, inflation-inclusive rate — is a subtle but common error that quietly and substantially understates value. Double-check it in any Nepali DCF, given how much inflation assumptions have moved in recent years.

Challenge 3: cyclical and project businesses

The hardest problem, for the businesses this book has covered, is forecasting cash for cyclical and project-based companies:

  • A hydropower developer's cash depends on river flow that varies year to year, on monsoon timing, and on PPA terms that may not be fully public or fully understood by outsiders.
  • A hotel or tourism business depends on tourist arrival cycles, which are sensitive to regional politics, global travel and domestic political stability.
  • A trader or manufacturer can be exposed to volatile import costs, exchange-rate effects and changing government duties.

In each case a DCF is still a useful discipline — it forces you to think, year by year, about what really drives the cash. But the honest output is a range of possible values under different scenarios (a strong monsoon versus a weak one; a tourism recovery versus stagnation), not one confident number.

PRACTICAL TOOL For cyclical or project-based Nepali businesses, build at least three scenarios into the DCF — a base case, a downside case (weaker river flow, fewer tourists, thinner margins) and an upside case — and report the range of per-share values for each, rather than presenting one base case as the final answer.

Use it like an engineer, not a fortune-teller

None of this means abandoning DCF for pure multiples on NEPSE. It means using DCF the way a careful engineer uses a calculation with known error margins, not the way a fortune-teller uses a crystal ball. It is a disciplined way to make assumptions visible, test how much each one matters, and form a reasoned view of value — always with humility about how much of the final number is real insight into the business and how much is simply your own assumptions echoing back.

Not every statement in this chapter is equally certain. A careful investor keeps track of which is which, the way a farmer separates what the soil test says from what the neighbours expect of the monsoon.

Chapter recap

  • A rupee today is worth more than a rupee tomorrow (the time value of money). A DCF forecasts free cash flow, discounts each year with PV = FCF ÷ (1 + r)^n, and adds a discounted terminal value. Riskier cash deserves a higher discount rate.
  • Free cash flow, not profit, drives a DCF. Discount FCFF at WACC or FCFE at the cost of equity — never mix them. Banks need an FCFE approach; rebuild a clean cash history first.
  • Cost of equity = risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium. In Nepal, T-bill yields swing with bank liquidity, a country risk premium must be added, and raw betas from thin trading are unreliable — use bottom-up betas or sensible brackets. Illustrative result: about 14–18%.
  • Terminal value often supplies 60–80% of the total. Keep perpetual growth near nominal GDP (about 4–6% for Nepal); for hydropower, value the licensed life rather than assuming cash forever.
  • In the HHPL example (3 crore shares, 14.5% cost of equity, 4% growth), equity value is NPR 161.91 crore, about NPR 54 a share, with 65% from terminal value.
  • Small changes in the two rates move HHPL from about NPR 40.7 to NPR 83.1. Always report a sensitivity table and scenarios, keep nominal and real consistent, and present a range, not one number.
