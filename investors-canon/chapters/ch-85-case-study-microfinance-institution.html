Case Study 3 — A Microfinance Institution

Anjana Koirala opened her notebook to a fresh page and wrote two words at the top: "small loans." She had spent Chapter 83 inside the balance sheet of a commercial bank, learning to read capital adequacy ratios and net interest margins the way a mechanic reads an engine's vital signs. Chapter 84 had taken her up a hillside penstock, learning to price a hydropower company's water year by water year, discounting a river the way you would discount a bond. Now she was looking at a business built on loans of twenty, forty, sixty thousand rupees each, made to women running vegetable stalls, buffalo sheds, and tailoring shops in villages she had never visited. Her bank case study had had a market capitalization measured in tens of arba (one arba equals ten billion rupees, roughly the scale at which Nepal habitually counts its largest listed companies). Her hydropower case study had had a single, physical, inspectable asset: a dam, a tunnel, a powerhouse. This company had neither a single site to visit nor a single number that fully captured its risk. It had, instead, hundreds of thousands of tiny, unsecured promises, scattered across dozens of districts, each one no bigger than a mid-sized grocery bill back home in Kathmandu.

That, she reminded herself, was the whole point of doing this as her third case study. The Canon Score — the 100-point, seven-dimension scoring framework this book has built chapter by chapter — is only as good as an investor's willingness to apply it honestly to businesses that do not look like each other. A bank borrows short and lends long against collateral and a regulatory capital cushion. A hydropower company sells a single physical commodity, electricity, under a long-term contract, and lives or dies by rainfall and interest rates. A microfinance institution (an MFI — a company whose entire business is making small, typically collateral-free loans to low-income borrowers, often organized in groups) does something different again: it manufactures trust, at scale, in places the formal banking system finds too expensive to reach. Getting the Canon Score right for an MFI meant testing whether the framework's discipline survived contact with a genuinely different kind of risk. This chapter follows Anjana through that test, using a real, currently NEPSE-listed microfinance institution: Chhimek Laghubitta Bittiya Sanstha Limited, trading under the ticker CBBL.

Lesson 85.1 — Setting Up the Case: A Different Kind of Bank

Chhimek Laghubitta was established in 2058 BS (2001 AD) as one of Nepal's earliest Grameen-model microfinance replicators — meaning it borrowed its founding method from the Grameen Bank of Bangladesh, the institution credited with popularizing group-based microlending to poor, largely rural women. Over two and a half decades it grew from a single-district pilot into one of Nepal's largest laghubitta ("small finance," the Nepali term used for microfinance institutions) networks, with a branch footprint spanning well over a hundred offices across the country and a borrower base numbering in the hundreds of thousands, concentrated among women in rural and semi-urban households who use small loans to finance livestock, retail trade, agriculture, and cottage-industry work. It is one of the older, larger, and more closely watched names in a sector that, unusually for Nepal, has an enormous number of separately listed companies: roughly fifty microfinance institutions trade on NEPSE, a level of sector fragmentation with no real parallel among Nepali banks or hydropower companies.

Anjana's first task, before she touched a single number, was to understand why an MFI needs its own scoring lens rather than a copy of the bank playbook from Chapter 65. Three structural facts drove this.

The first is the joint-liability-group (JLG) lending model itself. Rather than lending to individuals against collateral, as a commercial bank does, most Nepali MFIs organize borrowers into small groups — typically five to seven women — who meet regularly, often weekly, and who each guarantee the others' loans informally through peer pressure and shared reputation rather than through a mortgage or a fixed deposit.

KEY CONCEPT Joint liability group (JLG) lending replaces collateral with social collateral: a small circle of borrowers who know each other's businesses and families, and who each have a stake in the others repaying, because default by one member can affect the group's — and sometimes the whole center's — access to future credit.

This is a powerful mechanism when it works, because it turns a village's own social fabric into an underwriting tool a bank could never replicate. But it is also a mechanism that can fail all at once: when a whole village's cash flow is hit by the same shock — a bad monsoon, a remittance slowdown, a local market collapse — the very thing that made repayment reliable (shared local conditions) turns into a common point of failure. A bank's thousands of individually underwritten mortgage borrowers are far less correlated with each other than a microfinance center's thirty group members farming the same watershed.

The second structural fact is the dual mandate. An MFI is chartered, in part, on a social mission — expanding financial access to households the formal banking system has historically ignored — alongside its obligation, as a NEPSE-listed company, to deliver a commercial return to shareholders. These two mandates usually pull in the same direction, but not always: a purely profit-maximizing lender might raise loan sizes and interest rates faster than borrower repayment capacity can bear, while a purely mission-driven one might resist raising loan-loss provisions during a stress cycle for fear of looking like it is retreating from its social purpose. An investor reading Chhimek's numbers has to keep both mandates in view.

The third is regulatory. Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB, the central bank), which regulates banks, hydropower financing, and microfinance institutions all under one roof but with very different rulebooks for each, has historically applied tools to microfinance that it does not apply to commercial banks at all.

REGULATORY DETAIL For years, NRB capped the effective interest rate microfinance institutions could charge borrowers at a flat ceiling (around 15 percent all-in), and separately capped the interest rate spread — the gap between an MFI's cost of funds and what it charges borrowers — at a fixed number of percentage points. Effective from Shrawan 2082 (mid-July 2025), NRB moved away from that flat ceiling toward a base-rate-plus-premium framework, similar in spirit to how commercial banks already price loans: each MFI publishes its own base rate, reflecting its actual cost of funds and overheads, and then adds a regulator-bounded premium on top, rather than being handed one uniform number for the whole sector.

This single regulatory change matters enormously for an MFI's future profitability, because it determines how much room an institution has to reprice its loan book as its own funding costs move — a lever a commercial bank takes almost for granted but that microfinance institutions in Nepal have only recently been given in a more flexible form.

Anjana's note to herself at the end of this lesson: everything from here on has to be read through these three lenses — social collateral that can fail correlated, not independent; a dual mandate that can quietly trade off against itself in a downturn; and a regulatory regime for pricing that has just changed shape under the sector's feet.

Lesson 85.2 — The Qualitative Dimensions: Liquidity, Governance, and Moat

Chapter 65 established that liquidity and market structure — the first Canon Score dimension — asks a simple question: if you needed to sell this stock in a hurry, at a fair price, could you? For microfinance names on NEPSE, the honest answer is usually "with difficulty." Even judged against NEPSE's own famously thin liquidity (a market where many otherwise solid mid-cap names can go days without a meaningful trade), most laghubitta stocks sit toward the thinner end: free float (the portion of shares actually available to trade, once promoter holdings, staff allotments, and other locked-up blocks are excluded) tends to be a smaller slice of total shares outstanding than at a large commercial bank, and average daily traded volume (ADV) can be modest even for a company the size of Chhimek.

WARNING Do not assume that "large, well-known MFI" means "liquid stock." A microfinance institution can have hundreds of thousands of borrowers and still be a stock that moves ten percent on a single mid-sized order, because so few of its shares change hands on an ordinary day. Anjana treated this as a hard constraint on position size, not a detail to note and move past — the same discipline Chapter 65 asks of every NEPSE investor, applied here with extra force.

Governance, the second qualitative dimension, asks about board composition, ownership concentration, and the presence or absence of related-party dealing — loans or contracts that flow to people connected to management or the board rather than at arm's length. For an MFI this question has a distinctive local flavor: over-indebtedness. Because JLG borrowers can, in principle, take loans from more than one microfinance institution at once (a practice regulators call multiple lending, or borrower overlap), a poorly governed MFI's growth can be built partly on the same borrowers' capacity being double- or triple-counted across competing lenders in the same village. Nepal's 2021–22 microfinance stress episode — covered in Chapter 73's sector playbook — was in large part a story of exactly this: rapid branch expansion, aggressive loan-officer targets, and borrower overlap across institutions that, when a liquidity squeeze and rising rates hit simultaneously, left many households holding more debt across more lenders than their cash flow could service. Anjana's governance check on Chhimek looked for the antidotes to that pattern: a board with a meaningful share of independent, non-promoter directors; publicly disclosed policies on credit information sharing with other MFIs to catch overlapping borrowers before disbursement; and a loan-officer growth rate that has not dramatically outrun the institution's ability to actually supervise those loan officers (a theme she would return to with numbers in Lesson 85.3).

CASE IN POINT The 2021–22 stress episode is the clearest natural experiment Nepali investors have for governance quality in microfinance: institutions that had kept branch and staff growth roughly in line with their internal audit and supervision capacity came through the subsequent NPL cycle with far smaller increases in portfolio-at-risk than institutions that had expanded fastest in the two years before the shock. Growth speed alone told you almost nothing in 2019; growth-versus-oversight ratio told you a great deal by 2022.

The third qualitative dimension, competitive advantage or "moat," takes an unusual shape for an MFI. A bank's moat might be its deposit franchise or its branch network in the country's commercial centers; a hydropower company's moat is its river and its power purchase agreement. A microfinance institution's moat is best understood as geographic and product diversification against localized shocks. An MFI concentrated in a handful of districts — however deep its penetration there — is effectively making a bet that no single weather event, remittance disruption, or local market failure will hit its whole book at once. An MFI spread across dozens of districts, multiple ecological belts (Tarai, hills, mountains), and a mix of agricultural and non-agricultural borrower livelihoods is diversified against exactly the correlated-failure risk described in Lesson 85.1. Chhimek's long operating history and broad multi-district branch network give it a real edge here relative to smaller, more regionally concentrated laghubitta peers — a genuine, if unglamorous, form of competitive advantage that shows up not in pricing power but in a lower amplitude of bad years.

Lesson 85.3 — The Quantitative Dimensions: Applying the Chapter 80 Recalibration

This is the lesson Anjana had been most looking forward to, because it let her use a tool this book itself had had to build under pressure. Chapter 80 described how backtesting the Canon Score against Nepal's 2021–2026 microfinance stress period produced an uncomfortable finding: the financial-risk dimension, originally worth only 10 of the Canon Score's 100 points, was too thin to do its job. A 10-point dimension simply could not create enough separation between a strong, well-provisioned institution like Chhimek and a weaker peer carrying a much worse portfolio-at-risk trend, because so many points were "spent" elsewhere — on liquidity, governance, and moat — that a genuinely dangerous credit trend could only ever move the total score by a few points either way. The book's calibration response, also from Chapter 80, was to reweight financial risk up to 15 points for lending institutions specifically, funded by trimming two points each from the growth and moat dimensions, on the reasoning that for any lender — bank or microfinance institution alike — the quality of the loan book is close to existential in a way that, say, five-year revenue growth simply is not.

Applying that 15-point revised financial-risk dimension to Chhimek meant looking hard at three things.

The first is the portfolio-at-risk trend.

KEY CONCEPT Portfolio at risk over 30 days (PAR30) and over 90 days (PAR90) measure the percentage of an MFI's total outstanding loan portfolio that has at least one installment overdue by 30 or 90 days respectively — critically, the entire remaining balance of a late loan counts as "at risk," not just the missed installment. This is stricter than how a commercial bank often reports non-performing loans, and it is the standard the microfinance sector itself uses worldwide, because a borrower who has missed one weekly installment on a JLG loan is a meaningfully worse credit than the raw missed-installment amount alone would suggest.

A rising PAR30 that has not yet become a rising PAR90 is an early warning: borrowers are missing payments but many are still curing before the 90-day mark. A rising PAR90 is the language of a genuine, sector-wide credit cycle turning, which is exactly what Nepal's microfinance sector experienced in 2021–22 and, per more recent trade reporting, has continued to grapple with into 2025 and 2026 — one 2026 sector report found eighteen separately listed microfinance institutions had crossed the 10 percent non-performing loan threshold, even as the sector overall posted a headline profit increase, because roughly forty of the sector's fifty-odd listed names still grew earnings while a smaller cohort deteriorated sharply.

That bifurcation is precisely the pattern the original, thin financial-risk dimension would have struggled to price correctly, and precisely why the recalibrated 15-point dimension exists.

The second input is loan concentration by district. An MFI that discloses its outstanding portfolio broken down geographically lets an investor check the moat argument from Lesson 85.2 against hard numbers: what share of the book sits in the single largest district, and how has that share moved over time as the institution has expanded or consolidated its branch network? A falling concentration ratio over several years — the largest district's share of the book shrinking as new districts are added — is a genuine risk-reducing trend and earns real points inside the recalibrated financial-risk dimension; a rising concentration ratio, even at an institution with an otherwise clean PAR trend, is a flag that its apparent strength may be a single good district's luck rather than a structural advantage.

The third input, and the one Anjana found most specific to microfinance, is the growth-versus-oversight ratio: comparing the rate at which an MFI has added branches and loan officers against the rate at which it has grown its internal audit, credit-supervision, and risk-management staffing.

PRACTICAL TOOL A rough proxy any retail investor can compute from an MFI's disclosed employee and branch counts across two or three annual reports: divide the growth rate in branches (or loan officers) by the growth rate in internal audit or supervision staff, where disclosed. A ratio comfortably near or below 1 suggests oversight capacity has kept pace with the frontline; a ratio of 2, 3, or higher — branches multiplying much faster than the people meant to check them — is a leading indicator of the kind of overextension that preceded the 2021–22 stress episode, often visible a year or two before it shows up in the PAR numbers themselves.

This is exactly the kind of forward-looking, sector-specific signal the recalibrated financial-risk dimension was built to reward, and it is not a number most bank-focused investors would ever think to compute.

Anjana's assessment of Chhimek across all three inputs was that its longer operating history had given it time to build supervisory infrastructure alongside its branch network, rather than bolting supervision on after a growth sprint — precisely the pattern that let it weather the 2021–22 episode with a PAR trend that stayed materially better than many younger, faster-growing laghubitta peers, consistent with why this book's own calibration work in Chapter 80 used it as the reference case for what a "strong" MFI looks like under stress.

Lesson 85.4 — Valuation for a Business With No NIM

Chapter 65's bank framework leans heavily on net interest margin (NIM) — the spread a bank earns between what it pays depositors and what it charges borrowers — as the central measure of fundamental earning power. An MFI does not run on deposits from the general public in the same way (most fund themselves through wholesale borrowing from banks and development finance institutions, plus, for the larger ones, limited public deposit-taking), so Anjana needed a different fundamental-strength anchor. The sector's own preferred metric, inherited from microfinance's roots as an NGO-driven, donor-funded activity before it became a commercial NEPSE sector, is operating self-sufficiency (OSS).

PRACTICAL TOOL Operating self-sufficiency (OSS) is calculated as operating revenue divided by the sum of financial expense, loan-loss provisioning expense, and operating expense. An OSS reading above 100 percent means the institution is covering its own full cost of doing business — funding costs, expected credit losses, and overhead — from its lending income alone, with no reliance on grants or subsidized capital to stay afloat. An OSS below 100 percent, sustained over several quarters, is a genuine red flag: it means the institution's core lending business is not paying for itself. Alongside OSS, portfolio yield — total interest and fee income earned on the loan book, divided by the average outstanding portfolio — plays the role NIM plays for a bank: it tells you how richly the institution is pricing its loans relative to its funding cost, and how that pricing power is likely to be squeezed or expanded by the new base-rate-plus-premium regime described in Lesson 85.1. A well-run MFI with an OSS comfortably above 100 percent and a portfolio yield that has held up even as the regulatory pricing regime shifted underneath it is displaying real fundamental strength in the specifically microfinance sense of the term.

On valuation itself, Anjana ran into the same problem this book flagged for smaller hydropower names in Chapter 84: thin analyst coverage. Most Nepali brokerage research desks devote far more attention to the handful of largest commercial banks and hydropower developers than to any of the fifty-odd microfinance names on NEPSE, which means there is rarely a well-populated consensus earnings estimate or discounted cash flow model to lean on for a name like Chhimek. In that environment, the pragmatic valuation anchor is book value per share (net worth divided by shares outstanding) and the price-to-book (P/B) multiple the market is willing to pay for it — the same fallback this book recommends for any under-covered NEPSE name. Nepali microfinance stocks as a group traded at rich P/B multiples, often several times book value, during the sector's fast-growth years in the mid-2010s; that multiple compressed sharply during and after the 2021–22 stress episode as investors repriced the sector's credit risk, and it has remained a useful gauge of whether the market is, at any given moment, still pricing in stress-era caution or has moved back toward growth-era optimism. Reading Chhimek's current P/B against both its own multi-year history and against sector peers carrying worse PAR trends gave Anjana a sense of whether the stock's price already reflected its comparative strength, or whether the market was still tarring it with the same broad brush it applied to the eighteen institutions crossing the 10 percent NPL threshold.

Lesson 85.5 — The Full Worked Canon Score

Anjana laid out the complete tally the way Chapters 83 and 84 had taught her: dimension by dimension, using the recalibrated 15-point financial-risk weighting from Chapter 80, with the two points trimmed from growth and two from moat exactly as that chapter specified for any lending institution.

A 69 is neither a screaming buy nor a name to avoid — it lands in the range this book has consistently described as "own it in a properly sized position, and watch it closely," rather than either extreme. Anjana was honest with herself about where the genuine uncertainty in that 69 lived.

CAUTION Three sources of uncertainty are specific to microfinance and do not have clean equivalents in banking or hydropower scoring. First, rural credit cycle timing: unlike a bank's diversified urban-and-rural book, an MFI's repayment capacity is tied closely to agricultural seasons, so a single poor monsoon or a delayed harvest can move PAR30 by more in one or two quarters than a comparable shock would move a bank's NPL ratio. Second, the newly implemented base-rate-plus-premium regime is simply too recent, as of this writing, for its full effect on sector-wide portfolio yields to be visible in more than a few quarters of data — meaning the fundamental-strength score carries real forward uncertainty that will only resolve with time. Third, weather and remittance flows interact in ways specific to rural Nepali households: a bad harvest that would otherwise strain loan repayment is sometimes cushioned by remittance income from family members working abroad, and sometimes is not, depending on which countries those remittances are coming from and what is happening to their own labor markets — a variable almost entirely outside the institution's own control and barely visible in any of its disclosures.

None of this made the 69 wrong; it made it a number that deserved to be held with an open hand rather than treated as a settled verdict.

Lesson 85.6 — The Decision, and What to Watch

A Canon Score of 69, applied to a microfinance institution with Chhimek's profile, implied to Anjana a modest, properly sized position — meaningful enough to matter to her portfolio's return if the sector's current bifurcation continues to reward stronger institutions over weaker ones, but small enough that she would not be undone if the rural credit cycle turned against even a well-run name faster than its disclosures could signal. She resisted the temptation to treat the score as more precise than the uncertainty in Lesson 85.5 actually allowed; a 69 built partly on a regulatory regime only months old, and partly on a rural repayment cycle she could not see up close, was a score to act on with humility rather than conviction.

The decision, though, was only half the job. Chapter 82 established this book's drift-monitoring discipline — the idea that a Canon Score is a snapshot, not a permanent verdict, and that an investor's real ongoing work is tracking the specific inputs most likely to move the score, rather than re-running the whole seven-dimension process from scratch every quarter. For a microfinance holding, three monitoring items matter more than any others.

The first is the quarterly PAR30 and PAR90 trend, read against the sector-wide pattern rather than in isolation — a PAR uptick that merely tracks the whole sector's cycle is a different signal than a PAR uptick that outpaces peers who share the same regulatory and macro environment.

WARNING A single quarter's PAR spike, especially right after harvest season or during a remittance slowdown, is not automatically a reason to sell — but two or three consecutive quarters of PAR90 rising faster than the sector median is exactly the kind of drift Chapter 82 asks investors to treat as a trigger to re-score, not merely to note and forget.

The second is district-level loan concentration, watched for whether the diversification trend that supported this chapter's moat score continues, holds steady, or reverses as branch expansion resumes or slows. The third is any further change to NRB's microfinance interest-rate regime — since the base-rate-plus-premium framework is still new, a future tightening of the allowed premium, or a reintroduction of a flatter cap, would move the fundamental-strength score directly and should be treated as a scheduled check-in item rather than a surprise.

Anjana closed her notebook on this third case study with a different feeling than she'd had after the bank or the hydropower plant. Those two had rewarded her for reading a single dominant risk clearly — capital adequacy for the bank, hydrology and PPA terms for the hydropower company. The microfinance case had rewarded her, instead, for holding several smaller, correlated risks in mind at once — social collateral, geographic concentration, growth-versus-oversight discipline, and a regulatory regime in the middle of changing shape — none of which alone would have told her much, but which together, run through the same disciplined seven-dimension structure, produced a score she could actually stand behind, and a set of specific things to watch that would tell her, quarter by quarter, whether that score still held.

Chapter recap

This chapter applied the Canon Score's full seven-dimension framework to a real, currently NEPSE-listed microfinance institution, Chhimek Laghubitta Bittiya Sanstha, and in doing so tested whether the framework's discipline holds up against a business built on an entirely different foundation than a commercial bank or a hydropower company: thousands of small, largely collateral-free loans, underwritten through joint-liability groups rather than mortgages or fixed deposits, serving a dual social-and-commercial mandate, and regulated through microfinance-specific tools — including Nepal Rastra Bank's recent shift from a flat interest-rate ceiling to a base-rate-plus-premium pricing regime effective from Shrawan 2082. The chapter walked through the qualitative dimensions — liquidity and market structure (typically even thinner for microfinance names than the NEPSE norm), governance (with particular attention to over-indebtedness and related-lending risk), and competitive advantage (recast, for an MFI, as geographic diversification against correlated local shocks) — before turning to the quantitative core of the case.

The centerpiece of this chapter was putting Chapter 80's calibration finding to direct use: the recalibrated 15-point financial-risk dimension, up from the original 10, applied to Chhimek's portfolio-at-risk trend, district-level loan concentration, and a growth-versus-oversight ratio comparing branch and loan-officer expansion against internal supervisory capacity. Against a sector backdrop where eighteen listed microfinance institutions had crossed a 10 percent non-performing-loan threshold even as roughly forty of the sector's fifty-odd listed names kept growing profits, the wider financial-risk dimension did the job the original 10-point version could not: it created real separation between a well-managed institution and a stressed one, rather than letting that difference get diluted across six other dimensions. The chapter also introduced operating self-sufficiency and portfolio yield as the fundamental-strength inputs appropriate to a lending institution that does not run on a bank-style net interest margin, and leaned on book value and price-to-book multiples as the valuation anchor appropriate to a thinly covered NEPSE name.

The full worked score — 69 out of 100 — landed in this book's "own it in a properly sized position, and watch it closely" range, with the chapter's most important honest admission being how much genuine, irreducible uncertainty sat inside that number: a rural credit cycle tied to agricultural seasons and remittance flows well outside the institution's control, and a brand-new regulatory pricing regime not yet old enough to show its full effect. The chapter closed by tying the decision back to Chapter 82's drift-monitoring discipline, naming three specific ongoing items — the quarterly PAR30/PAR90 trend read against sector peers, district-level concentration changes, and any further NRB adjustment to the microfinance interest-rate framework — as the concrete triggers that should prompt a re-score rather than a shrug.

Across three case studies now, Anjana Koirala has applied the same seven-dimension structure to three businesses that share almost nothing at the level of how they actually make money — a bank's balance sheet, a hydropower plant's river, a microfinance institution's village-level social collateral — and found that the framework's discipline, including its willingness to recalibrate itself openly when the data demanded it, held up in all three. That is itself a demonstration worth taking seriously: a scoring framework is only trustworthy once it has been stress-tested against genuinely different businesses and shown willing to change its own weights when the evidence says so.

Chapter 86, Case Study 4 — A Manufacturing Company, takes this same worked-example discipline into a fourth, again fundamentally different terrain: a real, NEPSE-listed manufacturer, where the central risks shift again — to raw material input costs, capacity utilization, import dependence and customs duty exposure, and a competitive landscape shaped by both domestic rivals and cross-border imports, none of which map cleanly onto the bank, hydropower, or microfinance lenses built so far. Readers who have followed Anjana through all three case studies so far should expect Chapter 86 to ask the same foundational question this chapter asked of microfinance: which of the Canon Score's seven dimensions need a sector-specific lens, and which, if any, need actual recalibration, once real manufacturing-sector numbers are put through the same disciplined process.
