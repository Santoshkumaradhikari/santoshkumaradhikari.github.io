Case Study 4 — A Manufacturing Company

Kabita has now sat through three full Canon Score workups in this Part — a commercial bank in Chapter 83, a hydropower developer in Chapter 84, a microfinance institution in Chapter 85 — and something has changed in how she works. The first case study took her most of a weekend, with a printed copy of the 100-point framework next to her laptop and sticky notes marking which dimension she was on. By the third case study, the microfinance one, she could do a rough pass in an evening and knew, almost from memory, which questions the framework wanted answered under each of its seven dimensions. She arrives at this fourth case study, a manufacturing company, expecting the same rhythm: profile the business, walk the seven dimensions, apply the sector adjustment from Chapter 65, tally the score, decide. What she does not expect is how much of that confidence the manufacturing sector will take back from her within the first hour of research.

The company is Arghakhanchi Cement Limited, traded on NEPSE under the symbol ARGCL, one of Nepal's cement manufacturers and — as she will discover — one of the harder companies in this book to pin down with a clean set of numbers. Chapter 75 warned her this was coming: manufacturing is NEPSE's smallest, thinnest-covered sector cluster, without the swarm of brokerage notes and comparison sheets that follow banks, and without even the specialist attention hydropower and microfinance get from their own dedicated NRB circulars and IPO cycles. She had briefly considered Bottlers Nepal, the Coca-Cola bottler chapter 75 uses as its FMCG example, but decided a cement name would put her through the sharper version of the sector's core tension — the import-substitution story chapter 75 introduced, playing out against a real domestic capacity race that is happening as she writes.

Lesson 86.1 — Setting up the case: a cement company in a construction cycle

Arghakhanchi Cement Limited runs its production plant in Siyari Rural Municipality, Rupandehi district, in Nepal's southern plains near the Indian border, with a corporate office in Thapathali, Kathmandu. It makes ordinary Portland cement and Portland pozzolana cement — OPC and PPC in industry shorthand, the two most common cement grades used in Nepali construction, differing mainly in the proportion of clinker (the kiln-fired intermediate product that gives cement its strength) blended with other materials. The plant's clinkerization unit runs at roughly one million metric tons of annual capacity, with a grinding unit capacity of about 800,000 metric tons a year, and it uses Danish vertical roller mill technology along with a waste heat recovery system generating about 2.5 megawatts, which lets the plant claw back some of the enormous energy cost that a cement kiln otherwise burns straight into the atmosphere.

The board is chaired by Pashupati Murarka, a former president of the Federation of Nepalese Chambers of Commerce and Industry, alongside directors from the Siddhartha Group and the Kedia Organization — three separate, well-established Nepali business houses sitting on one board. That detail matters more than it might first appear, and Kabita flags it for herself early, because it will resurface directly in the governance dimension.

Why does a manufacturing company need its own distinct reading of the Canon Score, on top of the general framework she has now applied three times? Three reasons, and Chapter 75 had already named all three before she opened a single filing.

The first is cyclicality of a specific and identifiable kind. A bank's fortunes track the whole economy in a diffuse way; a hydropower company's fortunes track rainfall and a fixed power purchase agreement; a cement company's fortunes track the construction cycle almost directly — how many buildings are going up, how much road and bridge work the government is funding, how confident households feel about starting a home addition. When construction slows, cement demand slows with it, often before the slowdown shows up anywhere else in the economy. An FMCG manufacturer like a bottler faces a gentler version of the same idea, tracking consumer demand and festival-season spending rather than construction, but the principle is the same: manufacturing revenue rises and falls with a cycle that is visible and, to some degree, predictable, rather than moving in a straight line.

The second is import-substitution economics. Nepal imports enormous quantities of finished goods from India, and for decades a lot of its cement did come across the border too. Domestic cement manufacturers have grown mainly by displacing those imports — hence import substitution, the general economic pattern of a country producing at home what it used to buy abroad. That displacement is protected less by tariffs than by simple geography: cement is heavy, and heavy things are expensive to move long distances. A tonne of cement carried three hundred kilometres by truck picks up a transport cost that a tonne made forty kilometres from the construction site does not. That transport-cost cushion is a real competitive advantage for Nepali producers close to the markets they serve, but it is not a permanent, unbreakable moat — a change in Indian export incentives, a new road, or a large new domestic competitor can erode it, which is exactly the sector-specific risk Lesson 86.4 will dig into.

The third reason is the one that will occupy much of Kabita's time on this case: thin coverage. For the bank, she had NRB circulars, quarterly disclosures in a standardized format, and half a dozen brokerage comparison sheets. For the hydropower company, she had the power purchase agreement itself, a public document she could read clause by clause. For the microfinance institution, NRB's microfinance-specific disclosure regime gave her portfolio-quality data down to the district level. For Arghakhanchi Cement, several of the numbers she wants simply are not sitting in one clean place. Dividend history, an item she pulled up in thirty seconds for the bank, took real digging here and still came back incomplete.

KEY CONCEPT Import substitution is what happens when a country starts producing domestically what it used to import — and for a heavy, low-value-per-kilogram product like cement, the natural transport cost of moving it long distances acts as a built-in tariff protecting local producers, without any government policy required.

Lesson 86.2 — The qualitative dimensions: liquidity, moat, and governance

The Canon Score's first qualitative dimension is liquidity and market structure — how easily a real investor could buy or sell a meaningful position without moving the price against themselves. Two figures matter most here: average daily traded value, or ADV (the rupee value of shares changing hands on a typical day, which tells you how much you could trade without your own order swamping the market), and free float (the portion of total shares that is actually available to trade, as opposed to sitting locked up in promoter or strategic hands).

This is where the sector's thin coverage bites for the first time. For the bank case study, third-party sources gave Kabita a free-float percentage to two decimal places. For Arghakhanchi Cement, she could not find a verified, current free-float figure from a source she trusted enough to cite with confidence. What she can observe directly is the ownership structure: three named business houses — the Murarka family, the Siddhartha Group, and the Kedia Organization — hold board seats, which in a small-cap Nepali industrial company almost always means a promoter block large enough to meaningfully reduce the free float available to ordinary investors like her. Peer manufacturing names show a similar pattern with very different absolute liquidity: Ghorahi Cement, for instance, carries a market capitalization in the neighborhood of seventeen billion rupees, larger and probably more liquid day to day than a number of its smaller manufacturing peers, illustrating chapter 75's warning that liquidity is "highly variable across manufacturing names" rather than following one sector-wide pattern the way it roughly does for banks.

PRACTICAL TOOL Before scoring liquidity on any small Nepali manufacturer, pull the board of directors list first. Three or more distinct family or group names on the board is a fast, free signal that the free float is probably concentrated — a useful stand-in when a clean free-float percentage is not available anywhere.

The second qualitative dimension is competitive advantage — sometimes called a moat, the everyday word for whatever durable feature of a business keeps competitors from copying its profits away, the way a literal moat kept attackers from an easy walk into a castle. For Arghakhanchi Cement, the moat has two layers. The outer layer is the sector-wide transport-cost protection against Indian imports described in Lesson 86.1. The inner layer, specific to this company, is a genuinely interesting recent development: Arghakhanchi Cement has been growing its exports to India, not just defending against Indian imports but reversing the flow, with its plant's location near the border in Rupandehi working in its favor for outbound as well as inbound freight economics. Company disclosures describe exports rebounding to about 12 percent of total trade in the current fiscal year, up sharply from around 5 percent the year before, and back near the 12 percent level the company had reached two years earlier — in other words, a dip and a recovery, not a steady climb, which matters for how confidently Kabita should extrapolate it. The company's use of a Danish vertical roller mill and a waste heat recovery system also point to a genuine cost-position advantage: energy is one of the largest costs in cement production, and recovering waste heat to generate roughly 2.5 megawatts is the kind of operational detail that shows up in margins rather than headlines.

CASE IN POINT A cement plant in Rupandehi, near the Indian border, illustrates both sides of import-substitution economics at once: the same proximity that once let Indian cement undercut Nepali producers on price near the border now lets a competitive Nepali plant sell into the Indian market when its own cost position is strong enough.

The third qualitative dimension is governance — how well-aligned the company's insiders are with ordinary shareholders, and how much the numbers can be trusted. For Nepali industrial groups, the single biggest governance question is usually related-party transactions: deals between the listed company and other companies controlled by the same promoter families, which can be entirely legitimate and still create a conflict of interest if pricing or terms are not arm's-length. Arghakhanchi Cement's board includes representatives of three separate business houses, each of which runs other ventures — the Siddhartha Group's footprint, for instance, extends well beyond cement into banking and other sectors. That is not, by itself, a red flag; established Nepali industrial families sitting on multiple boards is closer to the norm than the exception, and their industrial experience is arguably an asset. What it does mean is that a careful investor should look for related-party transaction disclosures in the annual report and ask whether procurement, distribution, or lending arrangements with affiliated entities are priced fairly. Kabita was not able to verify the granular related-party transaction detail with the same confidence she managed for the bank, where NRB's disclosure regime forces a specific related-party exposure schedule. That gap does not automatically mean something is wrong — it means the governance dimension carries a wider error bar here than it did in her first three case studies, and she scores it accordingly rather than guessing at false precision.

Lesson 86.3 — The quantitative dimensions: growth, risk, and price

With the qualitative work behind her, Kabita moves into the numbers — fundamental strength, financial risk, and valuation appeal, the three dimensions built directly on the financial statements.

Fundamental strength starts with revenue growth, and here the recent trend for Arghakhanchi Cement is genuinely strong: turnover for the first nine months of the current fiscal year came in around 4.53 billion rupees, a 14 percent increase over the comparable period, continuing off a full prior fiscal year in which the company's turnover was roughly 5.87 billion rupees — itself a recovery from 6.06 billion rupees two years earlier and 5.77 billion rupees three years earlier, meaning the company's revenue line has not been a smooth upward slope so much as a dip and rebound pattern that tracks the broader construction cycle. Alongside the growth, gross operating margin has expanded from about 22 percent to about 25 percent over the same comparison, a meaningful improvement that the company attributes to the export recovery and operational efficiency gains rather than a one-off. For a capital-intensive manufacturer, margin expansion of that size, sitting on top of double-digit revenue growth, is a legitimately strong quantitative signal — but Kabita notes she is looking at essentially one clean comparison period, not the multi-year, quarter-by-quarter series she had for the bank, because that is what is publicly available for this name.

Financial risk asks how much debt sits on the balance sheet relative to the company's capacity to service it — an especially important question for manufacturing, where a cement kiln and grinding plant represent a huge upfront capital cost financed mostly with borrowed money, unlike a bank whose entire business model is built around debt in a different, regulated way. Three figures anchor this for Arghakhanchi Cement. The gearing ratio — total debt divided by equity, a basic measure of how much of the company is financed by lenders versus shareholders — sits at about 0.72 times, a moderate level for a capital-intensive manufacturer. Total debt relative to operating profit has fallen from about 2.85 times to about 2.13 times, a real deleveraging trend (paying down debt faster than profit is growing, so the debt burden shrinks in relative terms). The debt service coverage ratio, or DSCR — operating cash flow divided by the debt payments due, essentially a measure of how many times over the company could make its scheduled loan payments from its own cash flow — has improved from about 2.05 times to about 2.95 times, comfortably above the 1.0 threshold where a company would be living payment to payment. Credit rating agency ICRA Nepal rates the company's long-term bank facilities at LBB+ and its short-term facilities at A4+ — investment-adjacent but not top-tier grades on the Nepali rating scale, where long-term ratings run from LAAA down through LBB and lower, and short-term ratings run from A1+ down through A4 and lower. Put plainly: the company is deleveraging in the right direction, comfortably covering its debt payments, but is rated by an independent agency as a moderate, not a top-tier, credit risk — a fair and specific picture rather than a simple "safe" or "risky" label.

REGULATORY DETAIL ICRA Nepal's short-term rating scale runs from A1+ (the strongest) down through A2, A3, and A4, with plus and minus modifiers at each notch. Arghakhanchi Cement's short-term facilities carry an A4+ rating — comfortably investment-grade adjacent, but well below the top of the scale, and worth checking against a peer's rating rather than reading in isolation.

Valuation appeal is where the thin-coverage problem hits Kabita hardest. For the bank and the hydropower company, she had a current price-to-earnings ratio, or P/E — market price per share divided by annual earnings per share, the most common shorthand for how expensive a stock is relative to its profit — pulled straight from a data provider, plus half a dozen peers to compare it against. For Arghakhanchi Cement, the data providers she checked returned incomplete or unpopulated fields for current price, EPS, and P/E. What she can build instead is a peer comparison using the manufacturing names that do have visible numbers. Ghorahi Cement, a close peer, is currently loss-making — its trailing EPS is negative, at roughly minus 6.13 rupees for the fiscal year just closed — yet its shares still trade around 338 rupees, more than twice its book value per share of about 165.73 rupees. That is a market pricing a temporarily unprofitable cement producer as though its earnings power will return, which is itself a useful cyclical read chapter 65's framework asks for. Shivam Cements, the first cement company to list on NEPSE back in 2019, posted earnings per share in the high twenties to high thirties of rupees in its strongest recent years, though its most recent full-year dividend of 2.5 percent in bonus shares plus 10 percent cash suggests profitability well off those earlier peaks. Without a clean, current multiple for Arghakhanchi Cement itself, Kabita cannot honestly assign it a precise valuation score — and she decides, correctly, that manufacturing the number would be worse than admitting she does not have it.

CAUTION When a reliable current P/E or EV/EBITDA figure is not available for the company itself, do not substitute a peer's multiple and present it as the company's own. Score the valuation dimension conservatively and say explicitly that the score reflects incomplete data, not a judgment that the price is fair.

Lesson 86.4 — Reading cyclicality without mistaking it for decline

This is the lesson where Chapter 65's sector-adjustment logic earns its keep. The Canon Score's market sentiment dimension asks how the market currently feels about a stock — is it in favor or out of favor — and the business model dimension asks how durable its competitive position is. For a cyclical manufacturer, these two dimensions are the easiest in the whole framework to score wrong, because a bad year and a broken business look identical from the outside if you only glance at the headline numbers.

Ghorahi Cement's current loss is the clearest example sitting in front of Kabita right now. A cement producer reporting negative earnings per share looks, at a glance, like a company in real trouble. But construction activity across Nepal has been soft, a cyclical condition that affects essentially every cement producer at once rather than singling one company out — and the market's own pricing of Ghorahi Cement, still valuing it above twice book value despite the loss, is effectively telling Kabita that other market participants read the situation the same way: a rough patch in the cycle, not a broken company. Chapter 65's guidance is direct on this point — a good long-term Canon Score should look through genuine cyclical softness rather than punishing a company for the part of a downturn that is shared by the whole sector.

But "look through the cycle" is not the same as "ignore everything." Two developments in this same sector are not cyclical at all — they are structural, meaning they change the underlying competitive landscape rather than just moving along an existing cycle, and a good score should react to them differently. The first is any change in Indian trade or export-incentive policy toward cement and clinker; if India were to subsidize cement exports or Nepal were to change import duties, the transport-cost protection this whole sector leans on could erode quickly, and that is a real threat to the moat described in Lesson 86.2, not a mood swing. The second, and the one already visible in Kabita's research, is new domestic capacity. Shivam Cements holds a stake in a joint venture, Hongshi Shivam, alongside a Chinese cement group, and that joint venture has been expanding its own plant from roughly 6,000 tonnes of daily capacity toward a doubled 12,000 tonnes, an investment reported in the tens of billions of rupees. A large block of new domestic supply coming fully online does not care what the construction cycle is doing — it adds capacity to the whole Nepali cement market regardless, and if demand does not grow to absorb it, prices and margins across every producer, including Arghakhanchi Cement, come under structural pressure that a cyclical recovery will not fix.

WARNING A large domestic competitor doubling its own production capacity is a structural risk to every other producer in the same market, whether or not the broader construction cycle is currently improving. Do not let a cyclical upswing in the score mask a capacity threat that is unrelated to the cycle.

The discipline Kabita is practicing here — distinguishing "the whole sector is having a rough year" from "this specific company or sector is losing ground it will not get back" — is the same discipline the framework asked of her with the hydropower company's rainfall variability and the microfinance institution's portfolio stress, but the diagnostic tools are different every time. For hydropower, she checked rainfall records against the plant's design flow. For microfinance, she checked portfolio-at-risk trends against the sector-wide loan cycle. For manufacturing, the diagnostic is capacity utilization and competitor capacity announcements — is a company running a smaller share of its plant than usual because customers aren't buying (cyclical, and likely to recover), or because more competing plants are running at the same time and splitting the same demand (structural, and not something that recovers just because the cycle turns)?

Lesson 86.5 — The full worked score

Kabita assembles her tally. The Canon Score's seven dimensions and her assigned weights, unchanged from the previous three case studies, are: liquidity and market structure out of 10, business model and competitive advantage out of 20, governance and ownership out of 15, fundamental strength out of 20, financial risk out of 15, valuation appeal out of 10, and market sentiment and cyclical position out of 10.

Sixty-five out of one hundred lands in the same broad range Kabita has now come to recognize as "worth owning, not worth loading up on" — meaningfully above the bank case study's threshold for a pass, but well short of the conviction score the hydropower case study earned. What's different about this case, and worth being honest about, is why the score landed where it did. For the bank and the microfinance institution, every dimension was backed by a verifiable, current number. Here, two of the seven dimensions — liquidity and market structure, and valuation appeal — are scored below what the underlying business might actually deserve, purely because Kabita could not find the data to score them with confidence. That is a materially different kind of caution than "the business itself has weaknesses," and conflating the two would be a mistake she is careful not to make. A future version of this same case study, done once better third-party coverage exists for Nepali manufacturing names, might legitimately score five to ten points higher on data availability alone, without the business changing at all.

CAUTION A score depressed by missing data is not the same signal as a score depressed by a weak business. Keep a private note next to any dimension you scored conservatively for lack of information, so a future update to the case does not confuse "we didn't know" with "it was bad."

Lesson 86.6 — The decision, and what would move the score next

A score of 65 out of 100, for Kabita, implies a modest position rather than a full-conviction one — the kind of holding she would be comfortable adding to on a clear dip driven by sector-wide sentiment, but not one she would build into a large position on the strength of this research alone, given how much of the governance and valuation picture remains genuinely uncertain rather than merely unfavorable. That is a different flavor of caution than the one she reached with the hydropower company, where every number was knowable and the caution came from the business itself; here the caution comes partly from the limits of what she could verify, and she treats that as a reason to keep the position modest and revisit it as better information becomes available, rather than as a reason to walk away entirely.

Three catalysts stand out as the things most likely to move this score in either direction from here, and Kabita writes all three down as items to watch rather than trying to predict which will happen. The first is any change in Indian trade or export policy toward cement and clinker — a shift that could either widen or narrow the transport-cost protection the whole domestic industry leans on, moving every Nepali cement name's competitive-advantage score at once. The second is the pace at which the new domestic capacity she flagged in Lesson 86.4 actually comes online and gets absorbed by demand; if construction activity recovers fast enough to absorb the new supply without a margin hit, the sentiment score should recover with it, but if the new capacity lands into continued softness, the score should fall further, and this is not a cyclical judgment call but a specific, checkable fact she can track through company announcements and construction-sector data. The third is simply better disclosure — if Arghakhanchi Cement's own reporting, or third-party coverage of it, improves enough for her to fill in the liquidity and valuation gaps with real numbers instead of conservative placeholders, the score could move meaningfully in either direction once she actually knows what those numbers say.

PRACTICAL TOOL For any thinly covered manufacturing name, keep a short watchlist of three things: the next fiscal year's construction-sector growth figures, any announced capacity additions from named competitors, and any change to Indian cement or clinker export incentives. These three items move a cement company's score more reliably than almost anything else you can track quarter to quarter.

Chapter recap

This chapter applied the full Canon Score — the same seven dimensions, the same 100-point structure, and the same sector-adjustment logic from Chapter 65 that shaped the bank, hydropower, and microfinance case studies — to a real, currently listed manufacturing company, Arghakhanchi Cement Limited. The case surfaced three features that distinguish manufacturing from every sector examined so far in Part XVI: a cyclicality tied directly and visibly to the construction cycle rather than the broader economy, a competitive dynamic built on import-substitution economics and transport-cost protection against Indian producers that is real but not permanent, and — the theme that ran through nearly every lesson — a level of third-party coverage thin enough that several dimensions of the score had to be built on incomplete information rather than clean, verified figures.

The qualitative dimensions showed a company with a real but hard-to-quantify concentration of ownership among established Nepali industrial families, a genuine competitive advantage built on both transport-cost protection and a specific, verifiable recent export recovery to India, and a governance picture that was creditable on paper but impossible to fully verify at the related-party-transaction level given what was publicly available. The quantitative dimensions showed real strength — double-digit revenue growth, expanding margins, and a clear deleveraging trend backed by an improving debt service coverage ratio — sitting alongside a credit rating that was solid but not top-tier, and a valuation dimension that Kabita could not honestly score with the same confidence she managed for the previous three companies, because a clean current price-to-earnings figure for the company itself was simply not available from the sources she checked.

The sector-adjustment lesson did the most important conceptual work in the chapter: distinguishing a sector-wide construction slowdown, visible in a close peer's current losses, from a structural threat sitting in the same headlines — a competitor's joint venture doubling its own production capacity, an event that has nothing to do with where the construction cycle currently sits and everything to do with how much supply the whole market will have to absorb once that capacity comes fully online. Learning to tell those two forces apart, rather than treating every piece of bad sector news as the same kind of bad news, is the specific skill this case study was built to teach.

The worked score landed at 65 out of 100, a moderate result that implied a modest position rather than a high-conviction one — but the chapter's most important honesty was in explaining why the score landed where it did. Two of the seven dimensions were capped not because the underlying business looked weak, but because Kabita could not verify enough about them to score them any higher in good conscience. That is a distinct, less comfortable kind of caution than the ones she navigated in her first three case studies, and recognizing the difference between "the data says this is weak" and "I don't have enough data to know" was itself a skill worth the whole chapter.

Chapter 87 turns the lens in a genuinely different direction. Case Study 5 — A Rights Issue Decision moves away from scoring an entire company and toward evaluating a single corporate-action decision: whether to subscribe to a rights issue, the offer a listed company makes to its existing shareholders to buy additional shares, usually at a discount to the market price, in order to raise fresh capital. It is a narrower, sharper decision than anything Part XVI has asked of Kabita so far — not "is this a good company," a question she now has a practiced process for answering, but "given everything I already know about this company, is this specific offer, on these specific terms, worth saying yes to." Readers who have followed Kabita through a bank, a hydropower developer, a microfinance institution, and now a manufacturing company will find that same Canon Score discipline reapplied to a much more pointed question — proof that the framework built across this book is not just a way to rate a company once, but a way to keep making good decisions about the same company for as long as you hold it.
