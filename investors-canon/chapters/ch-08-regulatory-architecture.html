Markets do not police themselves. Left alone, they attract fraud, price-rigging and insiders who profit from what others do not know. Think of a busy haat bazaar with no weights-and-measures inspector: before long, some shopkeepers would start using light scales. Regulators are the inspectors. Their job is to set fair rules and enforce them, so the market can do its three real jobs: help companies raise money, find fair prices, and protect ordinary investors.

In Nepal, several institutions share this job, and their boundaries overlap. SEBON watches the share market. NRB controls the banks that lend into it. NEPSE runs the exchange. CDSC keeps the ownership records. Other bodies, such as the Nepal Insurance Authority and the Nepal Electricity Authority, shape whole sectors. For a NEPSE investor, knowing who controls what — and what pressures each one faces — is practical knowledge, not theory. A single regulatory decision has often moved the index several percent in a day, and 10–20% over a few weeks. Weak regulation has let investors lose money. And good reforms have sometimes created the best buying chances in the market's history.

This chapter maps the whole system of rules and rule-makers. It covers SEBON (the Securities Board of Nepal, the market's main regulator), NRB, CDSC, NEPSE itself as the exchange operator, merchant bankers, the rules on what companies must disclose, the ways investors are protected, and the key laws. Finally, it looks closely at political economy — how politics and money shape the way these institutions actually behave, as opposed to how their mandates say they should.

Lesson 8.1 — SEBON: Mandate, Powers, Structural Limitations, and Political Economy

What SEBON Is

The Securities Board of Nepal — Dhitopatra Board Nepal, in Nepali — is the apex regulatory authority for Nepal's capital market, established in 1993 under the Securities Exchange Act 2040 (1983) and now governed by the Securities Act 2063 (2007). It is an autonomous body under the Government of Nepal, headquartered in Kathmandu, with a mandate to regulate and develop the securities market, protect investor interests, and maintain market integrity. Its functional remit encompasses the registration and regulation of all market participants: NEPSE as the exchange, CDSC as the depository, stockbrokers, merchant bankers, mutual funds, portfolio managers, credit rating agencies, and listed companies.

SEBON is run by a Board. The Government of Nepal appoints the Chairman after a recommendation committee proposes names. The other members include government officials, a representative of NRB, and members from professional and business bodies. The Chairman's term is four years. In practice, terms have often been cut short or disputed, and top posts in the market's institutions have sometimes sat empty for months. (In 2025, a long vacancy in NEPSE's chairman post held up its planned platform for small companies.) The law calls SEBON autonomous. But because the appointment runs through the Ministry of Finance, its independence is limited in practice.

SEBON's Formal Powers

SEBON's statutory powers are extensive on paper. It can register and deregister market participants, approve and reject IPOs and rights issues, investigate market manipulation and insider trading, impose fines and suspend trading in specific securities, issue directives to listed companies, and refer criminal cases to the Attorney General's office for prosecution. It has the authority to inspect the books and operations of any registered entity, compel the production of documents and records, and — in theory — freeze assets in cases of suspected fraud.

On paper, this toolkit is similar to that of securities regulators in other developing markets. The real question is how often SEBON actually uses it — and that is where the problems start.

SEBON's Structural Limitations

Too few specialists. SEBON has a small staff for a market with about 8 million demat accounts. It has few experts in forensic accounting (digging through accounts to find fraud), data analysis or cyber-security. So most of its enforcement has been reactive: it acts after complaints or news reports, rather than spotting trouble first. Catching insider trading is hard. Someone has to match unusual trades against phone records, emails and company announcements. SEBON has built only part of that ability.

Weak punishments. SEBON has opened cases, fined people and suspended trading. But the fines have often been small next to the profit from the wrongdoing, and criminal prosecutions have been rare. Imagine a traffic fine of Rs 500 for a shortcut that earns you Rs 50,000: many people would pay the fine and keep doing it. Weak penalties work the same way in the share market.

Slow laws. SEBON's powers come from the Securities Act 2063, which only Parliament can change. New market tools — derivatives (contracts whose value depends on a share or an index), short selling (selling borrowed shares to profit from a fall) or computer-driven trading — need either a change in the Act or a SEBON directive. Parliament moves slowly, often taking years. So SEBON fills gaps with directives. These are quicker to issue but easier to challenge in court. One recent example: in August 2026, SEBON told NEPSE to suspend AI-based order entry until the practice had been studied.

The ownership conflict, looked at again. SEBON does not own any shares in NEPSE. Its link to the exchange is through governance — seats on the board — not ownership. The real ownership conflict sits elsewhere. As Chapter 7 showed, NEPSE is mostly owned by the state and state-linked bodies: the Government of Nepal, Rastriya Banijya Bank, the Employees Provident Fund and Nepal Rastra Bank. SEBON's own Board also includes government and NRB representatives. So SEBON is still not fully independent of the institutions it is meant to watch elsewhere in the financial system. The channel is shared political and institutional ties, not a shareholding. Strong action against NEPSE's weak operations, trading-system faults or broker misconduct can therefore be politically awkward. Think of a referee whose own committee includes officials linked to one of the teams. This conflict is not imaginary. It creates a built-in lean toward going easy, which is hard to overcome even for honest officials.

The Political Economy of SEBON

SEBON's Chairman is appointed by the Government of Nepal, and the appointment carries political weight that is disproportionate to the apparent technical nature of the role. The capital market touches the financial interests of hundreds of thousands of Nepali retail investors, of broker networks with significant political connections, of financial institutions whose board members often have political affiliations, and of the state itself as a major shareholder in listed entities.

Political cycles in Nepal — which are frequent, given the instability of coalition governments — create corresponding cycles in regulatory posture. A newly appointed SEBON Chairman seeking to establish credibility may pursue enforcement actions that a predecessor avoided. A Chairman nearing the end of tenure or facing political transition may prioritise stability over enforcement. Regulatory decisions on IPO approvals — particularly for companies with politically connected promoters — have at various times been subject to controversy, though formal documentation of improper influence is understandably difficult to establish.

For the investor, the practical implication is this: SEBON's behaviour is not fully predictable from its formal mandate. It must be analysed as an institution embedded in a political system, with its own bureaucratic interests, its governance conflicts, and its dependence on an appointment process that is not purely meritocratic. This does not mean SEBON is corrupt or incompetent — it has, in fact, made significant regulatory advances in recent years, including improvements in disclosure requirements, the introduction of automated surveillance systems, the tightening of broker capital requirements and, in August 2026, an order that brokers keep clients' money in bank accounts separate from their own. It means that SEBON's decisions must be interpreted in their political and institutional context, not taken at face value as purely technical regulatory outputs.

SEBON at a Glance
  • Founded in 1993 under the Securities Exchange Act 2040; now governed by the Securities Act 2063 (2007).
  • Powers: Registration, investigation, enforcement, directive-issuing authority.
  • Does not own equity in NEPSE (that structural conflict sits with the state and state-linked shareholders instead — see Chapter 7); SEBON's link to NEPSE is governance, not ownership.
  • Enforcement has historically been reactive, not proactive.
  • Chairman appointed by Government of Nepal — subject to political cycle.
  • Key regulatory products: IPO approvals, broker licensing, mutual fund registration, disclosure standards.
  • Surveillance system: SEBON has implemented market monitoring tools but forensic capacity remains limited.

Lesson 8.2 — NRB: How a Central Bank Becomes the Single Most Powerful Force in NEPSE

NRB's Formal Capital Market Role

Nepal Rastra Bank is Nepal's central bank, established under the Nepal Rastra Bank Act 2058 (2002). Its primary mandate is macroeconomic: maintaining price stability, managing foreign exchange reserves, regulating and supervising the banking system, and developing payment and settlement infrastructure. Its formal role in the capital market — as distinct from the banking sector — is secondary. NRB does not regulate NEPSE, SEBON, stockbrokers, or listed non-bank companies. That is SEBON's domain.

And yet, in practice, NRB is the single most powerful force in NEPSE's performance, valuation, and volatility. Understanding why requires understanding the transmission mechanisms through which NRB's banking sector decisions flow into equity market outcomes.

Transmission Mechanism 1: Credit and Liquidity

Banks are the main pipes that carry money through Nepal's economy. When NRB eases — cutting the policy rate, lowering the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR, the share of deposits banks must keep at NRB), or making it cheaper for banks to borrow from NRB overnight — banks have more money to lend and charge less for it. Some of that money is lent to investors against their shares. Some flows into shares from savers who now earn little on deposits. This extra money becomes buying pressure on NEPSE. Part 0 showed that the order matters: easier liquidity shows up first, and the jump in credit growth usually comes later. Credit growth confirms a trend; it rarely warns you early.

Tightening works in reverse. When NRB raises rates, raises the CRR or enforces its lending limits more strictly, banks slow new loans and push to recover old ones. Margin borrowers — people who borrowed against their shares — get hit twice. Their interest cost rises, and the value of their pledged shares may be falling as others sell. Some are forced to sell, which pushes prices down further. These falls can have nothing to do with how the companies themselves are doing.

Transmission Mechanism 2: The CD Ratio as a Market Switch

Few rules have moved NEPSE more directly than the cap on banks' Credit-to-Deposit (CD) ratio — total loans divided by total deposits, after some NRB adjustments. NRB caps it, currently at 90%, so banks do not lend out almost every rupee they hold. It is like a savings cooperative rule that says: lend out no more than 90 of every 100 rupees members have deposited. The rule is meant to keep banks safe. Its effect on NEPSE is a side effect — but a predictable one.

When banks get close to the cap, they all slow lending at the same time, including loans against shares. Investors with margin loans suffer first. Selling often starts in small, thinly traded stocks such as microfinance and small hydropower. It then spreads to large bank shares, as investors sell whatever they can to repay loans. This is what happened in 2021–22. Banks ran short of money to lend, the interbank rate jumped, and NEPSE fell from 3,198.60 (18 August 2021; 2 Bhadra 2078) to 1,815.14 (25 September 2022; 9 Ashwin 2079).

So the system-wide CD ratio, which NRB publishes every month, is a useful warning light. A rough rule of thumb: when most banks are above about 85–87%, the market is fragile. When they are below about 80%, banks have room to lend more. But room is not the same as appetite. In mid-July 2026 the commercial banks' CD ratio was only about 73% — plenty of room — yet loans grew only about 7% a year (mid-September 2026), because businesses were not borrowing much. A low CD ratio takes the brake off; it does not press the accelerator. Read it together with the interbank rate, which Part 0 found to be the best early warning.

Transmission Mechanism 3: Interest Rate Effect on Relative Valuation

Interest rates also change how shares look next to bank deposits. When NRB raises its policy rate, banks soon pay more on fixed deposits. Many Nepali investors see a fixed deposit as the main alternative to shares. If FD rates jump from, say, 7% to 10–12% — as they did in 2022–23 — a safe 11% starts to beat the uncertain return from shares. Today the opposite is true. In Ashwin 2083 (September–October 2026), commercial banks' average fixed-deposit rate for individuals was only about 4.1%. A low FD rate pushes savers toward shares.

This effect is stronger in Nepal because most investors are individuals, not large institutions. A pension fund can look years ahead. A household investor often needs a steady return to cover a loan EMI or family costs. When a fixed deposit pays 10% with almost no risk, that investor wants much more from shares before buying. A stock that looked fair at 20 times earnings can suddenly look expensive at the same price.

Transmission Mechanism 4: NRB's Margin Loan Regulations

NRB directly regulates how much banks may lend against shares. It sets the maximum loan-to-value ratio — the share of a holding's value that a bank may lend against it. Think of a gold loan: you pledge Rs 100 worth of gold and the bank lends you, say, Rs 70. For shares, the limit has usually been 65–70% of the lower of two prices: the average price over the last 180 days, or the latest market price. A July 2026 NRB directive lets banks go up to 80% for companies with strong dividend and compliance records, under each bank's board-approved policy (see Chapter 3). For some years there was also a ceiling on how much one person could borrow against shares — Rs 4 crore per bank from 2021, later raised step by step to Rs 25 crore — but NRB removed it in October 2025. NRB also caps how much of a bank's total lending can be secured by shares. These rules are not only about keeping banks safe. They are the main factor deciding how much borrowed money the market can carry.

When NRB tightens margin lending rules — reducing the LTV ratio, restricting which securities qualify as acceptable collateral, or limiting total exposure — it reduces the maximum leverage available to retail investors. In effect, it shrinks the pool of money the share market can use. When NRB loosens these rules — or floods banks with cheap money, as it did in 2020–21 during COVID-19 — borrowed money grows and price moves get bigger in both directions. The 2021 rise to 3,198.60 and the long fall that followed are the clearest example.

NRB's Institutional Position: Shareholder and Policymaker

As Chapter 7 explained, NRB held about 34.60% of NEPSE's shares when the exchange was founded. After a reported sale of part of that stake, it held 9.50% as of 2025. That is still a meaningful holding, and it gives NRB two roles that pull in opposite directions. As a shareholder, NRB gains when NEPSE earns well. NEPSE earns well when trading is busy, and trading is busiest in bull markets. As the central bank, though, NRB must stop borrowing in the financial system from getting out of hand. That job means tightening credit, which tends to cool bull markets. It is like a dam operator who also owns a rafting company downstream: the rafting business wants high water, but the dam job sometimes means holding the water back.

In practice, NRB's institutional culture prioritises its central banking mandate over its shareholder interests — monetary policy decisions in Nepal have not, based on available evidence, been distorted to support NEPSE valuations. But the duality creates an opacity in NRB's communications about capital markets. NRB's Monetary Policy statements occasionally comment on 'asset price speculation' in the stock market without always specifying the precise policy response, creating uncertainty that the market interprets in various ways. Investors who develop skill in reading NRB's Monetary Policy documents — particularly the language used around credit growth, the banking sector's CD ratio, and financial stability risks — gain a material analytical advantage.

Tracking NRB for NEPSE Intelligence
  • NRB Monetary Policy (once a year, at the start of the fiscal year): policy rate, CRR, SLR and credit targets — often market-moving.
  • NRB policy reviews (quarterly; the main mid-term review comes around February–March): changes to targets — watch for tightening signals.
  • Monthly Banking Statistics (nrb.org.np): CD ratio trends, credit growth by sector.
  • NRB Annual Report: Banking sector NPL ratios, provisioning coverage — signal for bank earnings risk.
  • NRB Press Releases: Occasional directives on margin lending, CD ratio adjustments — typically immediate market impact.
  • Financial Stability Report (yearly): NRB's own view of risks in the financial system, including from the share market.
  • Current Macroeconomic and Financial Situation (monthly): deposits, credit, CD ratio, interbank rate and remittances — the data behind Part 0's early-warning signals. Watch the interbank rate most closely.

Lesson 8.3 — CDSC: Clearing, Settlement, and Depository Functions

What CDSC Does and Why It Matters

CDS and Clearing Limited — usually called CDSC — is the central depository and clearing house for Nepal's share market. A depository is like a bank vault for shares, except that it is electronic: it holds shares as digital records instead of paper certificates. CDSC was set up in 2010 under the Securities Act 2063 and the Companies Act 2063, as a company owned by NEPSE. It does three linked jobs that sit behind every share trade on NEPSE. The first is depository services: holding shares in electronic form. The second is clearing: working out what each party owes after each trading day. The third is settlement: actually moving the shares and the cash between buyer and seller.

Before CDSC and the dematerialisation of securities, Nepal's capital market operated on physical share certificates. A buyer who purchased shares would receive a paper certificate that had to be physically transferred, registered with the company's share registrar, and stored safely. This process was slow (taking weeks), expensive, prone to fraud (forged certificates were not unknown), and practically impossible to scale into a market with thousands of daily transactions. CDSC's establishment and the subsequent mandatory dematerialisation of all listed securities was one of the most consequential infrastructure improvements in NEPSE's history.

The Depository Function: DEMAT Accounts

Every investor in NEPSE holds their securities in a dematerialised (DEMAT) account maintained within CDSC's central depository. The DEMAT account is a digital record that shows the investor's holdings across all listed securities — how many shares of which companies are held, their average acquisition price (where tracked), and any pledges or liens against the holding. Opening a DEMAT account, done through a registered Depository Participant (DP) — typically a licensed stockbroker or bank — is the first practical step for any investor entering NEPSE.

CDSC's central record — its ledger — is the final word on who owns what. When shares change hands on NEPSE, CDSC updates the ledger. When a company works out who gets a dividend or who may vote at an AGM, its share registrar checks CDSC's records as of the record date (the cut-off day for those benefits). This system has worked far better than the old paper-certificate era. Still, CDSC's computer systems have struggled at times to keep up with fast growth. Demat accounts — the electronic accounts that hold your shares — passed 5 million by 2022 and reached about 8.1 million by September 2026. At that point about 5.1 million people were active on MeroShare, and about 3.6 million had trading accounts with brokers. Many demat accounts are opened only to apply for IPOs, so the number of people who actually trade is much smaller.

The Clearing Function: Netting Obligations

At the end of each trading day, NEPSE produces a trade file. It records every trade made: buyer, seller, share, quantity and price. CDSC's clearing job uses this file to work out what each party owes overall — its net position. It does not settle every single trade one by one, which would mean a huge number of separate transfers. Instead, it nets them off. Say broker A's clients bought 10,000 NABIL shares on a given day and sold 6,000 NABIL shares. Broker A's net position is 4,000 shares to receive — not 10,000 coming in and 6,000 going out separately. Netting works like friends settling a shared trekking bill at the end of the trip: instead of everyone paying everyone, each person just pays or receives the difference.

This netting dramatically reduces the volume of actual securities and cash movements required to settle a trading day, reducing systemic risk and operational cost. The clearing function also calculates the net cash obligations — each broker's net amount payable or receivable based on the aggregate value of their clients' purchases and sales — which are then settled through the banking system.

The Settlement Function: T+2 and Its Implications

Nepal currently uses a T+2 settlement cycle. 'T' stands for the trade day. A trade made on day T is fully settled — shares delivered and cash paid — two business days later, on T+2. On T+2 the buyer's demat account is credited and the seller's is debited. Cash between the buyers' and sellers' brokers also moves on T+2, through designated settlement banks. Historically, several commercial banks have done this job at the same time rather than one single bank. This book has not independently confirmed which bank or banks do it today.

T+2 is slower than the T+1 cycles now used in India (NSE and BSE moved to T+1 in 2023) and the United States (which moved to T+1 in 2024). The longer gap matters to NEPSE investors in three ways. First, for two business days between the trade and settlement, there is counterparty risk — the risk that the other side fails to deliver the cash or the shares, leaving the trade unfinished. Second, your sale money arrives later, so you cannot put it to work as quickly. Third, shares you have just bought may not be free to sell until they reach your demat account, so in a fast-moving market the price can move against you while you wait. This matters little on calm days and much more in sharp sell-offs.

SEBON and CDSC have discussed moving to T+1, which would match regional peers and free up investors' money faster. The barriers are mostly technical. The banks' payment systems, CDSC's link to them, and brokers' back offices would all need upgrades. T+1 is not close: SEBON's August 2026 directives still told brokers to make sure investors get their money within T+2. Those directives did order one practical fix — linking brokers' systems with CDSC so that sellers no longer have to approve each share transfer by hand in MeroShare (the 'EDIS' step).

MeroShare: The Retail Interface

MeroShare is CDSC's web and mobile application that serves as the primary retail interface for NEPSE investors. Through MeroShare, investors can view their DEMAT holdings, apply for IPO and rights issue allotments, check allotment results, view dividend and bonus share credits, and access their transaction history. The launch and progressive improvement of MeroShare has been one of the most democratising developments in NEPSE's recent history — it moved IPO application from a cumbersome physical form process to a mobile-accessible digital one, dramatically reducing the friction of retail market participation.

MeroShare's IPO application functionality is particularly significant. Prior to its introduction, applying for an IPO required physically submitting an application form through a bank branch or broker, posting a cheque, and waiting weeks for results. MeroShare allows investors to apply through their phones, link their bank accounts for automatic debit on allotment, and receive results digitally. The number of IPO applications surged dramatically after MeroShare's adoption, and oversubscription ratios — already high — reached extraordinary levels (some issues drew applications for dozens of times more shares than were on offer) as applying became almost effortless. When an issue is oversubscribed, shares are given out by lottery — usually 10 shares each to the lucky applicants (more in some high-priced issues).

CDSC Operational Framework
  • CDS and Clearing Limited — established 2010; owned by NEPSE.
  • Functions: Depository (DEMAT records), Clearing (netting obligations), Settlement (T+2 transfer).
  • Settlement is handled through designated clearing banks (historically several at once, not a single named bank).
  • Demat accounts: passed 5 million by 2022 and about 8.1 million by September 2026 — but only about 3.6 million trading accounts.
  • MeroShare: CDSC's retail platform — IPO applications, holdings view, dividend tracking.
  • Settlement cycle: T+2 (under review for acceleration to T+1).
  • CDSC owns its own IT infrastructure — system outages have periodically disrupted settlement.

Lesson 8.4 — NEPSE as Exchange Operator: Listing Rules, Trading Rules, and Delisting Powers

NEPSE's Operational Role

SEBON sets the rules, and CDSC handles clearing and settlement. NEPSE itself, as the exchange operator, does three main jobs. First, it runs the listing framework: it decides which companies may trade on the exchange and on what terms. Second, it runs the trading system that brokers connect to: the NEPSE Online Trading System (NOTS), live since 2018, which investors reach through their broker's TMS (Trade Management System). Many people still call it NATS, after the NEPSE Automated Trading System it grew out of. Third, it enforces trading rules, including circuit breakers (automatic pauses when prices move too far), trading suspensions and limits on how far a price may move in a day.

NEPSE's governance is exercised through its Board of Directors, which includes representatives of the government, NRB, SEBON, and the licensed broker community. Day-to-day operations are managed by a General Manager (Chief Executive) appointed by the Board. The chairman's post is filled by the Ministry of Finance, and when it sits empty the board cannot take decisions. NEPSE's decisions on listing approvals, trading suspensions, and circuit breaker activations are among the most operationally immediate market-moving actions in the NEPSE ecosystem.

Listing Requirements

To list on NEPSE's main board, a company must meet a set of conditions. These come from SEBON's IPO rules and NEPSE's listing rules together. They include a minimum paid-up capital, which varies by sector — commercial banks need far more than, say, a manufacturing company. The company usually also needs about three years of financial history. Its accounts must be audited and prepared under Nepal Financial Reporting Standards (NFRS), Nepal's version of the international accounting rules. And it must have a proper governance structure, with independent directors, an audit committee and a risk management committee.

The IPO approval process runs through SEBON (which approves the prospectus and the issue), but listing itself is approved by NEPSE. This two-stage approval creates a sequential process where SEBON and NEPSE can, in principle, reach different conclusions — though in practice, coordination between the two institutions means that NEPSE listing refusals for SEBON-approved issues are rare.

A separate SME platform has been discussed for listing smaller companies that do not meet the main board criteria, which would widen the choice beyond the roughly 294 companies listed in mid-April 2026 (103 of them hydropower). NEPSE drafted rules for it in 2025, but the launch stalled. SEBON's ten-year blueprint, released in July 2026, now aims for more than 200 small and medium companies to be listed by 2036. SEBON has also considered licensing a second stock exchange; three groups applied, but the decision stalled in 2025.

Trading Rules: How Orders Are Matched

NEPSE's online trading system (NOTS, often still called NATS) is an electronic matching engine: it takes in buy and sell orders and pairs them. Trading hours are 11:00 AM to 3:00 PM. For NEPSE's first three decades this ran Sunday to Thursday, matching Nepal's traditional Sunday-to-Friday work week with Saturday as the weekly holiday; in 2026, after the government adopted a Saturday–Sunday weekend — part of its fuel-saving response to the oil shock from the Gulf war (see Chapter 6) — NEPSE moved to a Monday-to-Friday trading week (see Chapter 7). That 2026 change also removed the scheduling mismatch with Indian markets, which have long run Monday to Friday, simplifying the timing of any NEPSE-India capital flow responses to regional events.

NATS operates on a price-time priority algorithm: among all orders at the same price, the order that arrived earliest is executed first. Most orders are limit orders: buy or sell only at a stated price or better. Advanced order types common abroad — a stop-loss (an automatic sale if the price falls to a set level) or an iceberg order (a big order shown in small pieces) — are not generally available. That shows how basic NEPSE's trading machinery still is.

Circuit Breakers and Price Bands

NEPSE employs a two-level circuit breaker system to manage extreme price movements. At the individual stock level, daily price movements are limited to a maximum of 15% above or below the previous day's closing price (this band may be modified for specific categories of securities). When a stock reaches this limit — either the upper circuit or lower circuit — trading in that stock continues at the limit price for the remainder of the session, but no transactions can occur at prices beyond the limit.

At the market level, NEPSE can halt all trading if the overall index moves too far in a single day. Under the two-tier system in force since April 2026, a 5% move in the index within the first two hours triggers a 15-minute pause. An 8% move stops trading for the rest of the day. This is a market-wide circuit breaker. It works like the main fuse in a house, which trips when too much current flows at once. It is meant for broad panic or extraordinary events. Such halts are rare, but they have been triggered during sharp political uncertainty and market crises. The current rules took effect on 20 April 2026 (7 Baisakh 2083), weeks into the oil shock from the Gulf war (Chapter 6).

For the investor, circuit breakers create both protection and entrapment. Protection: in a panicking market, the daily price limit prevents a stock from falling to zero in a single session, giving rational buyers time to assess and intervene. Entrapment: in a declining market, a stock that hits the 15% lower limit on five trading days in a row has fallen about 56% (0.85 × 0.85 × 0.85 × 0.85 × 0.85 ≈ 0.44), even though each day it was only 'at its limit'. Investors who hold a lower-circuit stock face an exit problem — they cannot sell below the circuit limit, and if buyers are absent at the circuit price, they may be trapped in the position for days until sentiment shifts.

Delisting Powers

NEPSE has the authority to delist a company from trading under specified conditions: failure to meet continuing listing obligations (filing of audited financials, conduct of AGMs, maintenance of minimum paid-up capital), regulatory violations, court orders, or voluntary delisting initiated by the company's promoters. In practice, NEPSE's delisting mechanism has been applied more often through suspension — temporarily halting trading in a company's shares — than through permanent delisting.

Several listed companies have been in prolonged suspension — some for years — creating a class of NEPSE-listed securities that are technically on the exchange but practically untradeable. For investors who hold shares in suspended companies, the experience is financially and legally complex: they cannot sell, they may not receive dividends, and the regulatory resolution process (whether through merger, court-supervised restructuring, or eventual delisting) can be slow and uncertain.

Lesson 8.5 — Merchant Bankers and Issue Managers: Their Role in IPOs and Rights Issues

The Issue Manager's Role

When a company wishes to raise capital through a public offering — whether an Initial Public Offering (IPO), a Further Public Offering (FPO), or a Rights Issue to existing shareholders — it cannot simply declare an intention to sell shares and wait for buyers. A regulated, documented process must be followed, and a licensed intermediary — the Issue Manager, also called a Merchant Banker — must manage that process on behalf of the issuing company.

In Nepal, merchant bankers are licensed by SEBON. They handle almost every step of raising money from the public. They check the issuing company's finances, governance and legal standing — a process called due diligence. They prepare the prospectus, the detailed information document SEBON requires. They work with SEBON to get approval. They run the public application process through the banking system — the ASBA route that most investors use through MeroShare. They oversee the allotment of shares to successful applicants through CDSC's systems. And they advise the company on what price to set for the issue.

The Prospectus: The Investor's Critical Document

The prospectus is the most important document to read before applying for any IPO or rights issue. It contains the company's audited accounts for the past three years. It explains exactly why the company is raising money and how it will spend it. It describes the business and the risks specific to its work and its industry. It also gives background on the promoters, including any criminal or regulatory history.

In Nepal, prospectuses are posted on the SEBON website and the company's website from the day the issue is announced. Even so, surveys and everyday experience show that many retail investors applying for IPOs never read the prospectus at all. They apply because of the sector's reputation, word of mouth, or simple faith that every IPO will jump on listing. Given how often Nepali IPOs have listed at a big premium, this is understandable. But it becomes risky when the company is weak and the hoped-for listing gain never comes.

For a disciplined investor, the prospectus is required reading. Focus on four sections. First, the auditor's report: look for qualifications or 'emphasis of matter' paragraphs, where the auditor flags doubts or concerns about the accounts. Second, the MD&A (Management Discussion and Analysis), which shows how management itself sees the business. Third, the related party transactions section. It can reveal how promoters move value out of the company — for example, by renting their own building to it at a high rent. Fourth, the risk factors section. If it is written honestly rather than copied from a template, it names the business's real weak points.

IPO Pricing in Nepal: The Regulatory Framework and Its Consequences

SEBON's regulations govern how IPO prices are set in Nepal. The framework has evolved over time. For some categories of issuer — particularly financial institutions — SEBON's guidelines specify a maximum issue price relative to net worth per share, limiting the premium that promoters can charge public investors. For other categories, a book-building process has been piloted, allowing institutional investors to bid in a price discovery range before the public offer price is fixed.

The historical consequence of regulated pricing in Nepal has been systematic underpricing of IPOs relative to their subsequent market price — creating the listing gain phenomenon that has driven retail IPO fever. When an IPO is priced at, say, Rs 100 per share and lists on NEPSE at Rs 180 on the first trading day, the 80% gain creates powerful incentives for retail investors to apply for every IPO regardless of fundamental merit. Over time, this dynamic has supported the flow of capital into the market but has also conditioned retail investors to treat IPOs as lottery tickets rather than as investments in specific businesses with specific risk-return profiles.

Not all IPOs list at premiums. Companies with weak fundamentals, unfavourable market timing, or excessive issue sizes relative to market depth have listed below their issue price, and investors who applied for the full allotment have lost capital. The discipline of reading the prospectus and applying a fundamental filter to IPO decisions — rather than applying for every issue — is one of the most undervalued skills in Nepal's retail investment community.

Rights Issues: The Often-Misunderstood Capital Event

A Rights Issue is a capital-raising event in which an existing listed company offers new shares to its current shareholders, in proportion to their existing holdings, at a price that is typically below the current market price. For example, a company might offer a 1:1 rights issue at Rs 500 per share to shareholders of record, when the market price is Rs 700. Each existing shareholder who holds 100 shares has the right — but not the obligation — to purchase an additional 100 shares at Rs 500.

Rights issues are frequently misunderstood by retail investors in Nepal. A common misconception is that receiving rights is equivalent to receiving a dividend or bonus — a free gift from the company. This is incorrect. A rights issue dilutes existing shareholders unless they exercise their rights. If you own 100 shares pre-rights and the company issues 100% rights (1 new share per existing share), and you do not exercise, you now hold 100 shares in a company that has twice as many shares outstanding. Your ownership percentage has halved. The market price per share will adjust (theoretically) downward to reflect the dilution and the lower rights price.

Rights issues create an analytical decision for investors: subscribe (pay the rights price to maintain your percentage ownership) or do nothing (and accept the dilution). Rights entitlements cannot be sold on NEPSE; shares left unsubscribed are auctioned, and the auction premium goes to the company, not to the shareholder who let them lapse. The correct decision depends on the company's purpose for raising capital, the attractiveness of the rights price relative to fundamental value, and the investor's assessment of whether the capital raise will be value-accretive or merely dilutive.

Common IPO and Rights Issue Mistakes
  • Applying for every IPO without reading the prospectus — treating IPOs as guaranteed gains.
  • Confusing rights issue entitlement with a bonus share — they are fundamentally different events.
  • Ignoring the purpose of capital raised in a rights issue — a bank raising capital to cover NPLs is very different from one raising capital for growth.
  • Failing to account for the dilution effect when evaluating rights issue attractiveness.
  • Overweighting IPO allotment luck in portfolio planning — in oversubscribed issues, allotment is decided by lottery.
  • Not checking the Issue Manager's track record — some have brought low-quality issuers to market repeatedly.

Lesson 8.6 — SEBON's Disclosure Requirements: What Listed Companies Must Publish and When

The Information Architecture of a Listed Company

In any capital market, the quality of investment decisions is constrained by the quality of available information. A market where companies can conceal losses, delay material disclosures, or present misleading financial narratives is a market where retail investors are systematically disadvantaged relative to those with inside access. SEBON's disclosure requirements are designed to create a level informational playing field — ensuring that all investors, large and small, have access to material company information at the same time.

Nepal's disclosure framework, established under the Securities Registration and Issuance Regulation 2073 and SEBON's various directives, specifies four categories of mandatory disclosure for listed companies: periodic financial disclosures, event-driven material disclosures, governance disclosures, and annual report requirements.

Periodic Financial Disclosures

Listed companies in Nepal must publish quarterly financial statements within 30 days of each quarter's end. Commercial banks follow formats set by Nepal Rastra Bank as well as NFRS. Their quarterly results include the income statement, the balance sheet and key ratios. These ratios — net interest margin, cost-to-income ratio, NPL (non-performing loan) ratio and capital adequacy ratio — are the main tools for analysing banks. Non-bank companies' quarterly reports are less standard and often less detailed.

Annual audited accounts must be ready for the Annual General Meeting, which the Company Act requires within six months of the fiscal year's end. (Nepal's fiscal year runs from about mid-July to mid-July — Shrawan to Asar.) Banks also need NRB's approval of their accounts before the AGM, which is why bank AGMs and dividend news bunch up in the months after Asar. The audit must be done by an auditor licensed by the Institute of Chartered Accountants of Nepal (ICAN); larger companies such as banks must use senior, A-class auditors.

In practice, timeliness compliance varies significantly across listed companies. Large commercial banks — with professional finance departments, strong regulatory supervision from NRB in addition to SEBON, and sophisticated audit relationships — generally meet disclosure deadlines. Smaller listed companies — some manufacturing entities, smaller hydropower developers — have at times submitted quarterly results significantly after deadlines, without consequential enforcement action from SEBON.

Event-Driven Material Disclosures

SEBON's rules require listed companies to disclose 'price-sensitive' or 'material' information as soon as it happens, not at the next regular report. Material information means news that could move the share price. It includes Board decisions on dividends, rights issues or bonus shares. It includes big changes in management or ownership, such as promoter share transfers above set limits. It includes major contracts, such as Power Purchase Agreements or large supply deals, and action by regulators such as NRB or the Nepal Insurance Authority (formerly Beema Samiti). It also includes court orders that affect the company, and anything else a reasonable investor would see as important to their decision.

In practice, 'immediate' disclosure has often been read loosely on NEPSE. There have been cases where important news — a bank's bad loans rising, a hydropower project falling behind schedule, a promoter pledging shares below market value — reached some people before the formal announcement. That creates information asymmetry: some people know more than others, and insiders gain at retail investors' expense. It is like a few traders at Kalimati market hearing about a tomato shortage a day early and buying up the stock. SEBON's surveillance system tries to spot trading patterns that suggest insider trading before big announcements, but enforcement has been limited, as Lesson 8.1 discusses.

Governance Disclosures

Listed companies must keep and publish key governance information. This includes who sits on the Board and which directors are independent. It includes whether there is an Audit Committee and who is on it. It includes related party transactions, which must be disclosed in detail and approved by the Audit Committee. And it includes who owns the shares, including promoter holdings. The framework is based on international best practice, but how well it works varies a lot from company to company.

Related party transaction disclosure is particularly important for NEPSE investors. In many Nepali companies — particularly smaller manufacturing and trading companies — the boundary between the listed entity's commercial operations and the promoters' other business interests is porous. Promoters may sell goods or services to their listed company at above-market prices, lease property to the company at inflated rates, or route contracts to related businesses in ways that transfer value from public shareholders to insiders. The mandatory disclosure of related party transactions provides the raw material for detecting this — but only if investors read the disclosures and apply analytical scrutiny.

Annual Reports: The Underutilised Research Tool

The annual report is the fullest information document a listed company publishes. It is also the one Nepali retail investors most often ignore. Beyond the financial statements, a good annual report has several useful parts. The Chairman's letter can show strategic priorities and how frank management is. The Managing Director's report gives detail on how the business performed. The corporate governance report shows board meeting attendance and committee membership. The auditor's report includes any emphasis-of-matter paragraphs. And the notes to the financial statements hold the detail the main statements hide: related party transactions, contingent liabilities (possible future costs, such as a pending lawsuit), accounting policy choices and results by business segment.

Nepal's NFRS standards, aligned with IFRS, require extensive note disclosures that, if read carefully, can reveal management's accounting choices and the degree of conservatism or aggression in financial reporting. A bank that applies aggressive loan classification standards (delaying the recognition of non-performing loans) will show a lower NPL ratio in its headline disclosure but may reveal the underlying stress in the notes to its impairment schedule. A hydropower company that capitalises significant costs that might more conservatively be expensed will show a larger asset base and better-looking returns — until the day it is forced to write down those capitalised costs.

Lesson 8.7 — Investor Protection Fund and Grievance Redressal in Nepal

The Investor Protection Fund

The Investor Protection Fund (IPF) was established under the Securities Act 2063 to provide a limited safety net for investors in the event of broker default — the failure of a stockbroker to deliver shares or cash owed to clients. The fund is maintained by SEBON and is built from contributions by licensed stockbrokers (a percentage of their annual commission income) and penalties collected from regulatory violations.

The IPF is designed to compensate investors for losses arising specifically from broker insolvency or fraud — not from market losses on investment decisions. If a stockbroker becomes insolvent and cannot return client securities or cash held in their accounts, affected clients can file claims with SEBON for compensation from the IPF, up to a specified limit per investor.

For NEPSE investors, the Investor Protection Fund (IPF) matters in a limited but real way. It is a safety net against broker failure. That risk is low in normal times. It becomes real in cases of broker fraud, or in extreme market stress, when the margin loans a broker holds against clients' shares are not enough to cover what it owes. Be clear about its limits. The IPF does not protect you against investment losses, market crashes or bad advice. It only covers a broker going bust or committing fraud — just as deposit insurance covers a bank collapsing, not a bad investment you chose yourself.

SEBON's Grievance Redressal Mechanism

SEBON maintains a formal investor grievance mechanism through which investors can file complaints against brokers, listed companies, issue managers, and other regulated entities. Complaints can be filed online through the SEBON portal or in writing at SEBON's Kathmandu office. SEBON's supervision staff are responsible for reviewing complaints, investigating their merits, and taking regulatory action where violations are established.

In practice, the grievance redressal process in Nepal's capital market is slow and outcomes are uncertain. Investigation timelines can run into months. The remedies available to SEBON — fines, suspension, license cancellation — may not provide the specific financial relief an individual investor is seeking for a specific loss. Civil litigation through the court system is a parallel option but faces its own delays in Nepal's judicial system.

For common complaints — a broker not carrying out an order you placed properly, a company crediting bonus shares late, delays in demat transfers — follow three steps in order. First, take it up directly with the broker or the company. Second, if that fails, complain to SEBON through its grievance portal. Third, if a lot of money is at stake, talk to a lawyer about a civil case. Since August 2026, every broker must have a dedicated complaints unit and report its complaints, and how they were settled, to SEBON each month — so ask for that unit first.

Investor Associations

Beyond SEBON's formal mechanism, Nepal has several retail-investor associations — citizen groups that speak for small investors. They have pushed for fairer IPO allotment, better disclosure, faster handling of complaints and more investor education. They have no legal power, but they have sometimes influenced SEBON's decisions.

Retail investor associations in developing markets generally operate with limited formal power but can be effective at creating public pressure for reform. Nepal's investor community has been increasingly active on social media and through investor associations in articulating grievances and demanding regulatory accountability — a development that, over time, may accelerate improvements in Nepal's market governance.

Lesson 8.8 — Key Acts Governing the Market: Securities Act 2063, Company Act 2063, BAFIA

The Legislative Framework: An Integrated System

Nepal's capital market does not operate under a single omnibus statute. It is governed by an interconnected set of legislative instruments, each addressing a distinct dimension of market activity: the Securities Act for market regulation, the Company Act for corporate governance and shareholder rights, and BAFIA for the banking sector that dominates NEPSE's listed universe. Understanding these acts — not their full technical detail, but their practical significance — gives the investor the context to interpret regulatory developments, understand investor rights, and assess the risk that a specific regulatory change poses to a portfolio holding.

Securities Act 2063 (2007 AD): The Foundation Statute

The Securities Act 2063 is the foundational legislation for Nepal's capital market. It establishes SEBON, defines the categories of securities subject to its jurisdiction (shares, debentures, bonds, units of mutual funds), specifies the registration and licensing requirements for market participants, sets out the framework for IPOs and secondary market regulation, and provides SEBON's enforcement powers.

The key parts for investors are: the ban on insider trading (trading on important information the public does not yet have); the ban on market manipulation, including spreading false news to move prices; the rule that public issues must come with a prospectus; the rule that listed companies must report their results regularly; and the provisions that set up the Investor Protection Fund.

The Act has been amended several times since its 2063 BS enactment, reflecting the evolution of the market. However, significant gaps remain. The Act's treatment of derivatives, algorithmic trading, short selling, and electronic market platforms remains underdeveloped relative to the pace of market evolution. SEBON has bridged some of these gaps through directives and regulations — secondary legislation that carries less statutory weight but can be issued more quickly than parliamentary amendments.

Company Act 2063 (2006 AD): Shareholder Rights and Corporate Governance

The Company Act 2063 governs all companies incorporated in Nepal — listed and unlisted. For NEPSE investors, its most directly relevant provisions are those establishing shareholder rights, corporate governance requirements, and the rules around dividends, rights issues, bonus shares, and mergers.

Under the Companies Act, shareholders have several rights. They may attend and vote at Annual General Meetings (AGMs). They receive any dividends the company declares. They get a rights issue offer in proportion to what they already hold. If the company is wound up (closed down), they get their share of whatever is left after debts are paid. And they may see the company's register of shareholders. These rights are strong on paper. In practice, they depend on the company's governance culture and on whether regulators are willing to enforce them.

The Companies Act also sets rules for mergers and acquisitions of listed companies. This matters more and more on NEPSE, because NRB has actively pushed banks and microfinance firms to merge. A merger between two listed companies needs approval at each company's general meeting, consent from regulators (NRB for banks and financial institutions, SEBON for the share-market side), and an agreed share swap ratio. The swap ratio is the number of shares in the merged company that each existing shareholder receives for their old shares. For example, a ratio of 1:0.9 means every 100 old shares become 90 new ones. Swap ratios often cause disputes, especially when small shareholders in the smaller or weaker company feel their holding has been undervalued.

BAFIA 2073 (Banks and Financial Institutions Act 2017 AD): The Banking Sector's Constitutional Document

Banks, development banks, finance companies and microfinance institutions together make up close to 40% of NEPSE's total market value (about 38.8% in mid-March 2026; see Chapter 7). So the Banks and Financial Institutions Act 2073 (BAFIA) is in practice one of the most important laws for NEPSE investors, even though it is not a securities law.

BAFIA — the Bank and Financial Institutions Act — sets the licence categories for banks and financial institutions: commercial banks, development banks, finance companies and microfinance institutions. It sets their minimum paid-up capital. It limits how much they can lend to one borrower or to connected parties. It sets the conditions for paying dividends — for example, a bank may not pay a dividend if its capital adequacy ratio is below the legal minimum. And it gives NRB its powers to supervise and discipline the sector.

For NEPSE investors in banking stocks, BAFIA's provisions on capital adequacy, dividend restrictions, and merger requirements are directly relevant to investment analysis. A bank's ability to pay dividends — a primary return mechanism for many NEPSE retail investors who hold bank shares for their cash return — is directly gated by BAFIA's capital adequacy conditions and NRB's approval. BAFIA also establishes the framework within which NRB's consolidation directives operate: the Act empowers NRB to require weaker institutions to merge with stronger ones, creating involuntary merger dynamics that can significantly affect NEPSE-listed bank shareholders.

Lesson 8.9 — The Political Economy of Regulation: How Incentives Shape NRB, SEBON, and NEA Decisions

Why Political Economy Matters for the NEPSE Investor

The formal analysis of regulatory frameworks — what the law says, what the institution's mandate is, what powers it holds — provides the skeleton of understanding. But the living reality of regulation in any country is shaped by something the law does not capture: the incentives, pressures, constraints, and interests that determine how institutions actually exercise their formal powers. Political economy is the study of this living reality — of how political systems and economic interests interact to shape policy outcomes.

For the NEPSE investor, political economy is not an abstract academic exercise. It is practical intelligence. Some investors understand why SEBON has historically gone easy on insider trading. They understand why NRB words its monetary policy statements about the share market carefully, with an eye on politics. They see why NEA's Power Purchase Agreement talks move slowly even when hydropower developers urgently need money. And they see why broker consolidation has been proposed but not carried out, despite clear evidence that it would improve the market. Those investors are better placed to foresee regulatory changes, understand how they will hit prices, and act before the crowd.

The Political Economy of SEBON

SEBON works in a political environment where the share market touches the money of a large and vocal group: millions of retail investors who vote, broker networks with political connections, bank promoters with long ties to government, and the government itself, which owns shares in listed companies.

This creates pressures that push SEBON's behaviour in a steady direction. The first is pressure to approve IPOs generously. Every IPO SEBON approves creates a new group of investors with a stake in the market, who will support it politically. Every IPO SEBON rejects for poor quality creates opponents — the company's promoters, their bankers and their political friends. But it creates almost no supporters in return. The public investors who would have lost money on a bad IPO rarely get together to thank SEBON for protecting them. Because approvals win friends and rejections only win enemies, the system leans toward approval.

Second, the pressure against aggressive enforcement. SEBON's enforcement actions — particularly against prominent broker networks or politically connected listed company promoters — create immediate, organised opposition. The beneficiaries of enforcement (retail investors protected from insider trading or market manipulation) are diffuse and largely unorganised. The targets of enforcement are concentrated and motivated. This asymmetry is a universal feature of regulatory politics, and Nepal's SEBON is not uniquely susceptible to it — but it is more acutely exposed than regulators in systems with stronger judicial independence and civil society oversight.

Third, the revolving door dynamic. In markets globally, senior regulatory officials sometimes move to the private sector entities they previously regulated — broker firms, merchant banking houses, investment management companies. This prospect — whether consciously acknowledged or not — can influence regulatory decisions in ways that favour industry at the expense of investor protection. Nepal's financial sector is small enough that personal relationships between regulators and industry participants are dense and long-standing, amplifying this dynamic beyond what prevails in larger, more anonymous markets.

The Political Economy of NRB

NRB's position in Nepal's political economy is more complex than SEBON's, because NRB's mandate is broader (macroeconomic stability, not just capital market health) and its institutional capacity is stronger. NRB has historically maintained a degree of operational independence from political interference that exceeds what SEBON has achieved — but it is not immune.

The key political economy tension for NRB, from the NEPSE investor's perspective, is the conflict between its macroprudential mandate (preventing systemic financial risk, including stock market speculation funded by bank credit) and the political costs of tightening. When NRB raises rates or tightens credit, it damages the returns of hundreds of thousands of retail investors and makes bank loan EMIs more expensive for borrowers. Both groups express their dissatisfaction through the political system — through parliamentary debates, media pressure, and direct political lobbying.

The consequence is that NRB's tightening cycles have often been delayed relative to when macroprudential indicators would suggest action was warranted. The 2021 boom is the clearest case. NEPSE reached 3,198.60 in August 2021, and margin lending had grown very fast. A regulator acting early might have cooled things months before. When NRB did act — the Rs 4 crore per-bank margin cap in its 2021/22 monetary policy, followed by a severe shortage of money to lend — the brake came hard, and the index fell to 1,815.14 by September 2022. The pattern — delayed action followed by sharp correction — is characteristic of regulators operating in environments where the political cost of preemptive action exceeds the political cost of reacting to a crisis.

The Political Economy of NEA: Hydropower's Hidden Regulator

Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA) is not a capital market regulator. But for investors in hydropower — the sector with the most listed companies on NEPSE (103 of about 294 in mid-April 2026) — NEA is arguably the most consequential institution after NRB. NEA's decisions on Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) rates, grid connection timelines, and export power tariffs directly determine the revenue of every listed hydropower company.

NEA (the Nepal Electricity Authority) is a state-owned monopoly buyer of electricity from private hydropower producers — that is, it is their only customer at home. Its PPA talks are a two-way negotiation between a government body and private developers, but NEA holds most of the power. It is the only buyer for Nepal's domestic electricity. As a state body, it is not driven mainly by profit. And it carries complex political duties: keeping consumer electricity prices low, managing the cost of subsidised tariffs to the government, and balancing private developers' interests against the goal of bringing power to rural areas. It is like a dairy cooperative that is the only milk buyer in a village: farmers can argue about the price, but they have nowhere else to sell.

The consequence of NEA's institutional character is that PPA negotiations are slow, PPA rates have not always kept pace with developers' financing costs, and the timeline from project completion to revenue recognition has frequently exceeded projections. For NEPSE investors in hydropower stocks, NEA's behaviour is the primary non-financial risk — the risk that even a well-constructed, well-financed hydropower project will face revenue uncertainty because NEA delays grid connection, disputes metering, or renegotiates PPA terms.

NEA's political economy is shaped by the fact that cheap electricity is a populist political goal — no politician wins votes by raising electricity tariffs — while adequate PPA rates for private developers are a technical financial necessity that lacks popular constituency. This asymmetry means that NEA consistently faces political pressure to cap PPA rates and subsidise domestic consumers, even when the financial arithmetic requires higher developer compensation to attract private capital into the sector.

Exports are the main hope for easing this tension. Nepal already sells power to India — in one month reported in October 2026, NEA earned close to Rs 6 billion (Rs 6 arba) from exports there — and sends a smaller amount (about 40–60 MW) to Bangladesh through India's grid. A long-term agreement signed in January 2024 aims for Nepal to export 10,000 MW to India within ten years. More export income, earned in foreign currency, would strengthen NEA's finances and could leave room for fairer prices for private developers. If you own hydropower shares, watch export earnings and new deals. Watch the weather too: monsoon floods in 2026 damaged several projects and briefly cut exports to Bangladesh.

Implications: How to Use Political Economy Analysis

Political economy analysis translates into practical investment intelligence through several channels. First, regulatory announcements from SEBON or NRB should be evaluated not only for their stated rationale but for their political context. A SEBON directive that appears to impose a burden on a specific market segment may be more explicable as a response to political pressure from a competing interest group than as a purely technical regulatory decision.

Second, the timing of regulatory action tells you something. Tightening that comes right after a change at the top — a new government, a new NRB Governor, a new SEBON Chairman — often reflects the new leaders' priorities. It may signal a new stance that lasts as long as they stay in charge.

Third, the gaps in regulation — the areas where formal powers exist but enforcement is absent — are often more informative about political economy than the areas where regulation is active. A SEBON that has formal insider trading enforcement powers but consistently under-prosecutes tells an investor that insider trading information advantages persist and should be factored into how market pricing dynamics are interpreted.

Fourth, regulatory reform that reduces inefficiency — that aligns the formal mandate of an institution more closely with its actual incentive structure — creates investment opportunity. When SEBON tightens IPO standards after a period of loose approvals, the quality of the listed universe improves, which eventually supports better fundamental-based pricing. When NRB's consolidation policy reduces the number of fragile microfinance institutions and creates stronger surviving entities, it improves the investment quality of the remaining listed MFIs. The investor who anticipates these reform cycles — rather than reacting to them after they have already moved prices — captures the most attractive returns.

Political Economy Checklist
  • Has there been a recent change in SEBON Chairman or NRB Governor? Expect potential regulatory posture shift.
  • What is the government's fiscal position? Fiscal stress increases pressure on NRB to maintain accommodative policy.
  • Are there upcoming elections? Pre-election periods often see political pressure against monetary tightening.
  • Is SEBON's enforcement activity increasing or declining? A declining trend suggests political accommodation pressure.
  • What is NEA's grid expansion timeline? Delays in evacuation infrastructure bottleneck hydropower revenue.
  • Are India-Nepal power export negotiations progressing? Resolution materially changes NEA's financial position.
  • Is NRB's language in its Monetary Policy becoming more concerned about 'asset price speculation'? Tightening often follows.
  • Is the Gulf war oil shock pushing inflation up (6.34% in mid-September 2026)? Rising inflation makes NRB slower to ease, whatever the political pressure (see Chapter 6).
