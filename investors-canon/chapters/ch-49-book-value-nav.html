WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Book value (net worth) — total assets minus total liabilities. Tangible book value — book value minus goodwill and other intangible assets. Goodwill — the extra paid in a merger above the net assets acquired. Revaluation reserve — an increase in book value from revaluing assets such as land. NAV — net asset value per unit of a mutual fund: holdings plus cash minus liabilities, divided by units. Replacement cost — what it would cost to build the same asset today, often far above its historical book cost.

Lesson 49.1 — What Book Value Actually Measures

The Kalanki house

Imagine a house in Kalanki, Kathmandu, bought twenty years ago for NPR 40 lakh: 15 lakh for the land and 25 lakh for building it. Today the land alone might fetch NPR 3 crore, because the area has grown so much. The house is older but still standing and usable.

If you asked "what is this house worth?" and someone said "NPR 40 lakh, because that is what the owner paid", you would object at once. The original cost tells you almost nothing about today's worth.

That is the central tension of this chapter. Book value is what a company paid for its assets, adjusted for depreciation and profits built up over time. It is not necessarily what those assets are worth today, or what someone would pay to own the whole business.

Book value and book value per share

Formally, book value is the company's value as recorded on its balance sheet: total assets minus total liabilities, also called shareholders' equity or net worth.

If Nabil Bank has total assets of NPR 500 arba and total liabilities (deposits, borrowings and other obligations) of NPR 460 arba, its book value is NPR 40 arba. In theory, that is what would be left for shareholders if the company sold every asset and paid every liability at their recorded values.

Book Value Per Share (BVPS) divides net worth by the number of shares. With NPR 40 arba of net worth and 32 crore shares, BVPS is NPR 40,00,00,00,000 ÷ 32,00,00,000 = NPR 125 per share. Every NEPSE investor sees this number constantly — on broker terminals, in annual reports, and in the quarterly reports listed companies file with the Securities Board of Nepal (SEBON) and NEPSE.

Why it matters for some businesses and not others

Despite the Kalanki problem, book value matters — because for some businesses it is unusually close to true value.

  • Banks and financial institutions: their assets and liabilities are mostly financial (loans, deposits, investments), not physical property whose worth drifts away from its cost. So book value stays close to reality.
  • Hydropower companies with land bought decades ago, hotels on prime land, manufacturers with old plants: book value can be wildly different from what the company would fetch in a sale or cost to rebuild today.

Chapter 39 introduced the Price-to-Book (P/B) ratio for banks. This chapter goes deeper: why book value works so well for some Nepali sectors and so poorly for others, and how a careful analyst adjusts for the difference.

KEY CONCEPT Book value (net worth) = Total Assets − Total Liabilities. Book Value Per Share = Net Worth ÷ Number of Shares Outstanding. It is accounting net worth, not necessarily market or liquidation value.

The P/B ratio

P/B = Market Price Per Share ÷ Book Value Per Share

If our bank trades at NPR 340 against a book value of NPR 125, its P/B is 2.72x. The market pays NPR 2.72 for every NPR 1 of recorded net worth. That premium must be earned by the bank's ability to make returns on its net worth — its Return on Equity (ROE) — above what a similar, safer investment would give.

A P/B below 1x — trading below book — means the market thinks the recorded net worth overstates true value, or that earning power is so weak investors will not pay even the accounting value of the assets.

Both readings are common on NEPSE. Both mean you must ask why, rather than mechanically buying "cheap" P/B shares or avoiding "expensive" ones.

Lesson 49.2 — Why Book Value Matters Most for Banks and Financial Institutions

A bank's stock-in-trade is money

To see why P/B is the single most important valuation measure for Nepali banks, development banks, finance companies and insurers — far more than for a trader or a hotel — look at what these businesses are on the balance sheet.

A bank manufactures nothing. Its "stock" is money. It takes in deposits (a liability) and lends them out as loans (an asset), earning the gap between what it pays depositors and charges borrowers — the net interest margin from Chapter 40. Both sides of its balance sheet are financial items in rupees, not physical goods whose value depends on wear, location or rebuilding cost.

Why that makes book value reliable

When Global IME or Nabil Bank records a loan of NPR 10 lakh, that loan is worth roughly NPR 10 lakh (after allowing for expected credit losses, which Nepal Rastra Bank requires banks to provide for). It is not worth NPR 3 lakh or NPR 40 lakh depending on the property cycle. A rupee of deposit is worth a rupee.

That is fundamentally different from a hydropower company's 40-year-old plot of land, whose recorded cost has almost nothing to do with its current worth. Because a bank's assets and liabilities are already close to their real value, its book value is a fairly reliable floor estimate of its worth, and P/B becomes a meaningful yardstick across the sector.

Book value is also the regulator's measuring stick

There is a second reason. Nepali banks, development banks and finance companies are regulated by Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB) capital adequacy rules. They must hold minimum ratios of core capital and total capital to risk-weighted assets.

That capital is, in effect, book value. Shareholders' equity stands in front of depositors and absorbs losses before depositors are ever at risk. A bank's ability to grow its loans — and so its future profits — is capped by how much capital it holds, because NRB will not let it lend beyond its capital limit. Think of it as the size of a water tank: the bigger the tank, the more fields you are allowed to irrigate.

That is why Nepali banks issue rights shares and bonus shares so often:

  • Rights shares bring in new money and grow book value.
  • Bonus shares add not a rupee of book value, but move reserves into paid-up capital to meet NRB's minimum capital rules.

Both support further lending. So an investor in a bank is asking not only "is the share cheap against its assets?" but "is this capital base big enough, and used well enough (shown by ROE), to keep growing the business?"

REGULATORY DETAIL Nepal Rastra Bank's capital adequacy framework (based on Basel standards) requires commercial banks ("A" class) to keep minimum capital ratios against risk-weighted assets — around 11% total capital, with sub-limits on core (Tier 1) capital. Since shareholders' equity is the top of this ratio, book value is not just an accounting figure for Nepali banks — it is a binding regulatory limit on how much a bank can lend and grow.

Insurers: similar, with one extra layer

For insurers a similar logic applies, with one added layer. An insurer's balance sheet carries large actuarial liabilities — estimated future claims and policy obligations — which are projections, not fixed amounts like a bank deposit. So an insurer's book value is somewhat less precise than a bank's.

The principle still holds. Insurers are financial businesses whose "product" is a pool of contractual and statistical obligations, not physical stock. So P/B is a far more relevant lens for Nepal Life Insurance or Prabhu Insurance than for a hotel or a trading house.

P/B as the first tool, not a side check

That is why Chapter 39 and this chapter agree: for financial institutions, P/B is not a side check on a DCF or DDM — it is often the first tool practitioners reach for. The alternative, projecting decades of loan growth and margins in a DCF, is more fragile and more prone to modelling error than asking: "what return is this management earning on this book of equity, and what multiple of book value is fair for that return?"

The underlying reason is simple. Banks, development banks, finance companies and insurers hold financial assets and liabilities already recorded close to their real value, unlike land or machinery, whose book value can stray far from true worth.

Lesson 49.3 — Adjusted and Tangible Book Value: Cleaning Up the Number

Reported book value is not always the clean figure Lesson 49.2 describes, even for financial institutions. Take it at face value and you can be badly misled. Two adjustments matter most in Nepal: removing intangible assets such as goodwill, and checking revaluation reserves.

Adjustment 1: remove goodwill

Goodwill appears when one company buys another for more than the fair value of its identifiable net assets. The extra price is recorded as an intangible asset called goodwill.

Nepal has had a wave of bank mergers and acquisitions over the past decade, driven partly by NRB's push to consolidate banking into fewer, stronger institutions (from the mid-2010s onwards). When a stronger bank absorbs a weaker development bank, any premium paid above the target's net assets shows up as goodwill on the combined balance sheet.

Goodwill is a real accounting asset, but it has no liquidation value. In a wind-down it is worth zero, because it is not something you can sell; it is the accounting entry for having paid extra for growth, market share or a banking licence. It is like paying extra for a shop's "goodwill" and regular customers — real while the shop runs, worth nothing if you close it.

Tangible Book Value strips it out:

Tangible Book Value = Total Shareholders' Equity − Goodwill − Other Intangible Assets, divided by shares outstanding.

For a bank that grew a lot through acquisitions, tangible book value can be noticeably lower than headline book value. P/TBV (price to tangible book value) gives a more cautious, more liquidation-realistic picture of what really stands behind shareholders.

CASE IN POINT When a merger creates a combined bank, the acquirer's balance sheet may show goodwill for the premium paid over the target's net assets — often to gain its branches, deposit base or banking licence. When comparing P/B across merged and non-merged banks, also compare P/TBV. Two banks with the same headline P/B of, say, 1.8x can carry very different quality of book value if one balance sheet includes several arba of goodwill and the other does not.

Adjustment 2: check revaluation reserves

Under Nepal Financial Reporting Standards (NFRS), which Nepali companies — especially banks and larger corporates — have gradually adopted, property, plant and equipment can in some cases be carried at a revalued amount rather than original cost. The increase goes to a "revaluation reserve" within equity, not through profit and loss.

This is common for land, and especially relevant in Nepal. Land prices in the Kathmandu Valley and other cities have risen enormously over decades, while the recorded cost of older holdings may still reflect prices from twenty or thirty years ago.

A revaluation reserve is not automatically a worry — often it moves book value closer to reality, fixing the Kalanki problem this chapter opened with. But apply two tests before trusting one:

  1. When, and by whom? Was it done by a professional, independent valuer, or by an internal estimate that may be hopeful?
  2. Could the value actually be realised? Could the company sell that land at the revalued price without disrupting its own business? (A hydropower company cannot sell the land under its powerhouse and keep operating.) Would a forced or rushed sale fetch anything near the revalued figure?

A revaluation reserve not updated for over a decade, using an old valuation in a market that has moved sharply since, deserves real doubt. It may overstate true adjusted book value just as easily as original cost understates it.

WARNING A revaluation reserve raises recorded book value, but it is only as reliable as the valuation behind it. An old, stale or internally produced revaluation — especially for land the company cannot sell without ceasing to operate — can make book value look stronger than true liquidation value supports. Always check the date and independence of the valuation before treating a revaluation reserve as solid, investable equity.

Sort every asset into three piles

Reported book value is a starting point, not a finishing point. To build an adjusted or tangible book value, ask asset by asset which pile it belongs in:

  • Close to cash value — bank loans net of provisions, cash, government securities.
  • Intangible with no liquidation value — goodwill, capitalised software, deferred tax assets that depend on future profits.
  • Physical asset whose recorded cost may be stale either way — land, buildings, hydropower civil works.

Only after this sorting does comparing price with adjusted book value across companies mean something, instead of comparing accounting figures that just happen to share a label.

Lesson 49.4 — Net Asset Value (NAV) for Mutual Funds

Chapter 16 introduced Nepali mutual funds: pooled investment schemes run by asset management companies (such as NIBL Ace Capital, Nabil Investment Banking, Sunrise Capital or Global IME Capital), listed and traded on NEPSE — mostly as closed-end funds with a fixed number of units, plus a smaller number of open-end schemes. This lesson adds the valuation tool specific to funds: Net Asset Value (NAV).

NAV: book value for a basket of shares

NAV is book value's close cousin, but for a portfolio of securities rather than an operating company:

NAV per unit = (market value of the portfolio + cash and receivables − liabilities such as fees payable) ÷ units outstanding

Example: a fund such as NIBL Sahabhagita Fund holds NEPSE shares and government securities now worth NPR 90 crore, has NPR 2 crore in cash and NPR 1 crore of payables. Its net assets are NPR 91 crore. With 9 crore units (a typical face value in Nepal is NPR 10), NAV per unit is NPR 91,00,00,000 ÷ 9,00,00,000 = NPR 10.11.

NAV is published regularly — SEBON's Mutual Fund Regulations, 2067 require fund managers to publish each scheme's NAV at least once a week, and to report monthly NAV figures to SEBON within fifteen days of the month's end — so investors get a clear, up-to-date benchmark against the fund's trading price. That is the key difference from a company's book value, which is only as current as the last quarterly report and uses old cost for many assets. A fund's NAV is close to a live market value, because what it holds are listed securities with visible prices.

KEY CONCEPT NAV per Unit = (Market value of portfolio holdings + cash − liabilities) ÷ Units outstanding. Unlike operating-company book value, mutual fund NAV is marked to the current market prices of the underlying securities, making it a far more current and reliable benchmark of intrinsic value.

Why closed-end funds trade away from NAV

The famous feature of closed-end funds — the main type on NEPSE — is that their trading price often differs from NAV. They trade at a discount (price below NAV) or, less often on NEPSE, a premium (price above NAV).

A closed-end fund has a fixed number of units that trade between investors on the exchange, like a company's shares. Unlike an open-end fund, you cannot hand units back to the fund at NAV whenever you like. With no such automatic way to pull price back to NAV, a gap can open and last.

Why Nepali funds usually trade at a discount

Nepali closed-end funds usually trade below NAV, with the gap widening and narrowing with market mood. When the Canon checked all 42 schemes in August 2026, every one traded below NAV — by a median of about 6.7 percent and at most 13 percent (Chapter 16). Wider gaps are possible in weaker markets, but do not assume them. Four reasons:

  1. Thin trading. Units are hard to sell later, so buyers demand a discount.
  2. Fees. Management fees and expenses are a continuing drag that a buyer in the market pays for without having chosen the manager.
  3. Mood. Nepali investors have at times been lukewarm on professionally managed funds compared with picking shares themselves, especially in bull markets, when direct stock-picking feels (rightly or wrongly) more exciting.
  4. Maturity. Most Nepali closed-end funds have a fixed term, often 10 years, after which they wind up and pay out. As that date nears, the payout becomes more certain and the discount tends to narrow.

Take a closed-end fund with a published NAV of NPR 12 a unit trading at NPR 11.20 — about a 6.7% discount, the August 2026 median. A buyer is, in effect, getting NPR 12 of listed securities and cash for NPR 11.20 — provided the published NAV is accurate and the buyer can wait until the discount narrows or the fund matures and pays out its net assets. It is like buying a bag of rice worth Rs 1,000 for Rs 933, but having to wait to cook it.

The opportunity — and the risk

This creates a real, if patient, value opportunity: buy units of a well-run closed-end fund at a wide discount, expecting the gap to close over time — because mood improves, because the fund nears maturity (when holders usually receive close to full NAV in cash or in kind), or because the fund itself buys back units (a step some funds have taken to narrow a stubborn discount).

The risk is that the discount can widen further before it narrows, and NAV itself can fall if the underlying portfolio — heavily weighted to NEPSE banking and hydropower shares for most Nepali funds — drops in a downturn. A discount-to-NAV strategy is a bet on the gap closing, not protection against a falling portfolio.

How to check a fund

Compare its latest published NAV per unit (from the fund manager's website or SEBON disclosures, usually weekly) with its NEPSE price:

Discount (or premium) = (NAV − Market Price) ÷ NAV

A discount widening over several months, with no change in portfolio quality, can signal an undervalued entry point. But always check the fund's remaining life, expense ratio and how concentrated it is (especially in a handful of bank or hydropower counters) before deciding the discount is unjustified.

Lesson 49.5 — Asset-Heavy Companies: Hydropower, Hotels, and the Replacement Cost Problem

Back to the Kalanki house. It applies with full force to one group of Nepali companies where book value, taken at face value, can be one of the most misleading numbers on the balance sheet: asset-heavy businesses such as hydropower companies, hotels and manufacturers sitting on large landholdings.

Hydropower: book value depends on when it was built

A run-of-river project — like those of Chilime Hydropower, Butwal Power Company or the many smaller listed producers on NEPSE — needs a huge up-front outlay:

  • land — sometimes bought decades ago at prices far below today's, sometimes recently at much higher prices;
  • civil works — dam, headrace tunnel, powerhouse;
  • electro-mechanical equipment — turbines, generators, transformers.

Under historical-cost accounting — the default under NFRS unless a company chooses to revalue — all of this is recorded at what it cost, then depreciated over its useful life.

So two plants with the same capacity, output and NEA power purchase agreement can show very different book values, just because one was built ten years ago at lower cost and the other recently at costs pushed up by years of rupee depreciation, dearer steel and cement, and higher wages. Comparing P/B across hydropower companies without adjusting compares apples with oranges: the book value is shaped by when the plant was built, not by how valuable or productive it is.

Replacement cost

The more important distortion runs the other way: replacement cost — what it would cost to recreate the asset's earning capacity today, as opposed to what it originally cost. Building an equivalent plant today (same capacity, head and site conditions) might cost a good deal more than an older plant's book value, because construction, land and equipment costs have all risen.

A hydropower plant's true value is arguably closest to the present value of its future cash (exactly what Chapter 46's DCF estimates), not its book value or even its replacement cost. But replacement cost is a useful sanity check, especially as a floor. If a plant's listed shares value it below what an equivalent new plant would cost to build, and its PPA and remaining licence term are sound, that is a meaningful sign the market may be undervaluing it, all else equal.

WARNING Historical-cost book value for Nepali hydropower, hotel and manufacturing companies often understates true economic and replacement value, because land and construction costs recorded years or decades ago do not reflect today's prices. Relying on book value or P/B alone for these sectors — without adjusting for revaluation, replacement cost, or a DCF of the underlying licence — will usually make truly valuable asset-heavy businesses look artificially expensive on P/B, or hide real differences in asset quality between companies.

Hotels: the land beneath

Hotels have a related but different version. A hotel such as Soaltee or Yak & Yeti sits on prime Kathmandu land that may have been bought generations ago for a tiny fraction of today's value.

The hotel building depreciates under normal accounting, pulling book value down over time. Meanwhile the land beneath — often most of an urban hotel's real value — has soared in value and is not depreciated at all. (Land is not depreciated under NFRS, but it stays at old cost unless revalued.)

So a hotel's book value can badly understate its true net asset value, because the most valuable thing it owns — central land — is recorded at a decades-old cost. Here, adjusted or revalued book value, using a current independent land valuation, is far more informative than the raw figure. It is also why hotel and property-linked companies sometimes attract corporate interest (privatisation proposals, buyouts, redevelopment plans): a buyer who sees the gap between book value and land value can spot an opportunity a purely accounting reading misses.

Manufacturers: a milder version

Manufacturers with old factories and land — cement, cigarette and consumer goods makers and similar NEPSE listings — face a milder version. Machinery really does wear out roughly in line with depreciation, so the distortion is smaller for equipment.

But where a manufacturer owns large industrial land now surrounded by towns — a factory built decades ago on what was farmland at the edge of the Kathmandu Valley or Biratnagar, now inside dense urban sprawl — the same gap applies. Its land may be on the books at a fraction of one percent of current market value. Its P/B is simply not comparable with a bank's: the bank's book value is close to reality, while the manufacturer's may hugely understate its true asset backing once land is properly revalued.

A three-step discipline

When valuing an asset-heavy company:

  1. Check for revaluation. Has the company revalued its land and buildings? If so, how recently, and by whom?
  2. If not, estimate the land yourself. Make an independent, even rough, estimate of what the land alone is worth at today's prices — using comparable land sales in the area, often available from local land revenue (malpot) offices, property brokers or news of nearby sales. Add the gap between that estimate and recorded cost to book value, as a rough adjusted book value.
  3. Cross-check with cash flows. Always compare any asset-based estimate with a DCF of the operating business (Chapter 46). A hydropower plant's or hotel's true worth ultimately depends on the cash its operations can generate, not just the resale value of the soil and concrete beneath it.

In short: for an asset-heavy Nepali company, do not rely on reported book value alone. Check the date and independence of any revaluation reserve; where none exists, build a rough adjusted book value from current local land values; then compare price-to-adjusted-book-value, not price-to-reported-book-value, across peers.

Lesson 49.6 — The Danger of Book Value for Growth and Intangible-Heavy Businesses

So far, the worry has been that book value understates the worth of certain asset-heavy businesses. Book value has a different danger too. For growth-oriented, intangible-heavy or asset-light businesses, it can hugely understate true value — or become irrelevant to it. Unwary investors then wrongly dismiss such a company as "expensive" on P/B when it is not expensive at all compared with its real earning power and growth.

Value that lives off the balance sheet

Think of a company whose main value is not physical assets but intangible advantages: a strong brand, an exclusive distribution network, its own technology, regulatory licences, trained people, or simply a proven ability to grow earnings at high returns with very little extra capital.

A well-run consumer brand, a telecom or tech-enabled service business, or an asset-light trading and distribution company can earn huge profits and cash compared with the modest equity on its balance sheet — exactly because its value does not depend on owning lots of land, plant or equipment. Such a company naturally shows a very high ROE, and a correspondingly very high P/B can be fully justified, not a sign of overvaluation.

It is like a famous momo shop in a small rented room. Its "book value" is a few tables and steamers, but its name and loyal customers earn far more than those assets would suggest.

The justified P/B link

The maths here settles what might look like a contradiction between P/B analysis and the rest of this Part. A justified P/B is tied to ROE, the cost of equity and expected growth — the same drivers as the Dividend Discount Model (Chapter 48):

Justified P/B ≈ (ROE − g) ÷ (r − g)

where ROE is the sustainable return on equity, g the expected long-run growth rate, and r the cost of equity (Chapter 46).

A company with ROE of 25%, a cost of equity of 14% and growth of 8% has a justified P/B of (0.25 − 0.08) ÷ (0.14 − 0.08) = 0.17 ÷ 0.06 ≈ 2.83x. And many high-ROE, asset-light businesses can sustain P/B of 4x, 5x or more when growth is strong and lasting.

An investor who screens NEPSE for "cheap" shares on P/B alone, without checking whether a high P/B is backed by an equally high, lasting ROE, will consistently avoid the best compounding businesses and drift towards statistically cheap but low-return ones — a value trap in reverse.

KEY CONCEPT Justified P/B is driven by the relationship between ROE, growth and the cost of equity: Justified P/B ≈ (ROE − g) ÷ (r − g). A high P/B is not automatically "expensive" if a durable, high ROE backs it — and a low P/B is not automatically "cheap" if it reflects a truly low and unsustainable ROE.

When accounting hides value being built

The danger grows for businesses that are investing in intangible growth that accounting forces them to expense at once rather than record as an asset.

Under NFRS and most accounting systems, spending on building a brand (advertising and marketing), training a distribution network or developing organisational skills is charged to profit and loss in the year it is spent. It is not recorded as an asset and depreciated over its useful life — even though it may be creating real, lasting value, just as a factory or hydropower plant does.

So such a company's book value can actually fall (through the expense) in the very years it is building its most valuable long-term assets. This is the mirror image of the hydropower land story:

  • there, book value under-captures value that clearly exists (physical land);
  • here, book value never captures value being actively created (brand, network, skills), because the rules do not allow it to be recorded at all.

A young or fast-growing company with low, or even falling, book value can still be creating enormous shareholder value. Book value is simply the wrong lens for it, and screening such companies on P/B will consistently misprice them.

A NEPSE-specific caution

Book value and P/B are deeply embedded in how Nepali investors, brokers and financial media talk about bank and finance shares — arguably more than in more developed markets, given how much banking dominates NEPSE's float and trading. So there is a real temptation to apply the same P/B lens to every other sector: manufacturing, hotels, trading, insurance, and any new tech-enabled or consumer businesses that list.

The message of this chapter is not that book value is unimportant. Lessons 49.1 to 49.4 show how essential it is for financial institutions and mutual funds. But its usefulness depends on the sector. A disciplined investor consciously chooses the right lens for each business, rather than defaulting to whichever multiple Nepali media quote most often for that share. For asset-light, intangible-driven or early-growth businesses, P/B and even adjusted book value can be close to meaningless; DCF (Chapter 46) or earnings-based relative valuation (Chapter 47) will tell you far more.

Chapter recap

  • Book value = total assets − total liabilities (net worth); BVPS = net worth ÷ shares. It is what was paid, adjusted — not necessarily what assets are worth today, like a Kalanki house recorded at its 20-year-old price. P/B = price ÷ BVPS (e.g. NPR 340 ÷ 125 = 2.72x).
  • For banks and financial institutions, book value is close to real value (loans and deposits are financial) and is the base of NRB's capital rules — so P/B is often the first tool. Insurers too, with care for actuarial estimates.
  • Clean up the number: subtract goodwill from mergers to get tangible book value (P/TBV), and test any revaluation reserve for its date, independence and realisability.
  • Mutual fund NAV is marked to market. Nepali closed-end funds usually trade below NAV (a median of about 6.7% and at most 13% in August 2026); a discount can be an opportunity, but check tenure, fees, concentration and the risk of NAV falling.
  • For hydropower, hotels and land-rich manufacturers, historical cost can badly understate value. Check revaluation, estimate land at today's prices, consider replacement cost, and cross-check with a DCF.
  • For asset-light, brand-driven and growth businesses, book value misses most of the value. Justified P/B ≈ (ROE − g) ÷ (r − g): a high P/B can be justified, and a P/B below 1x is not automatically cheap.
