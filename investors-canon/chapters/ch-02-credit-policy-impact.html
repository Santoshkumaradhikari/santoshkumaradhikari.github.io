Credit Policy and Its Direct Impact on NEPSE

In Nepal, credit — money that banks lend — does more than finance the share market. It has been the single best guide to where the NEPSE index goes next. Every big turning point in NEPSE since the mid-1990s can be traced back to a change in bank lending six to eighteen months earlier. When Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB), the central bank, fills the banking system with money and private-sector lending speeds up, share prices rise long before company profits justify it. Part of that borrowed money flows into shares directly, through margin loans (loans taken against shares you already own). Part flows in indirectly, because easy money makes people feel richer and braver. When NRB tightens — by raising its policy rate (the benchmark interest rate it sets), capping lending to certain sectors, or letting banks hit their lending limit — the reverse happens just as strongly. Think of credit as the water in an irrigation canal: when the gate opens, every field turns green; when it closes, even the best field dries out. Most NEPSE investors watch price charts and quarterly earnings and never look at the canal gate. This chapter shows you how. It traces Nepal's three big credit booms and busts, explains how margin lending works in Nepali banks, lists NRB's sector lending caps and how they have been loosened, and uses real numbers from 2020 to 2026 to show how monthly credit data can warn you early.

Lesson 2.1 — The Credit-GDP Relationship: Why Bank Lending Moves NEPSE

Banks dominate Nepal's financial system more than in almost any other South Asian country. Commercial banks, development banks and finance companies handle most of the formal lending in the economy. NEPSE is also heavily weighted toward these same institutions: banking and financial shares usually make up more than half of the market's total value and a similar share of daily trading. This has a direct consequence. The companies that dominate the index are the same companies that create credit, and credit is the main way NRB's policy reaches the economy. So the health of bank balance sheets, and the speed at which banks are growing their loans, works as a stand-in for the health of the whole index. A bank-heavy market cannot escape the credit cycle. In a real sense, it is the credit cycle, traded on a screen.

Credit reaches share prices through four channels at once. First, the direct channel: margin lending (covered in Lesson 2.3) lets bank money flow straight into share purchases, pushing prices up as more loans are paid out. Second, the wealth channel: cheap, plentiful credit lifts land and house prices — Nepali banks have long lent heavily to property and construction — and people who own that land feel richer and move savings into shares. Third, the earnings channel: a bank's profit depends on how fast its loan book grows and on its net interest margin (the gap between what it earns on loans and pays on deposits). When lending grows, the profits of the nineteen listed commercial banks rise almost automatically, and because they are the biggest weights in the index, NEPSE's total earnings rise with them, whatever the real economy is doing. Fourth, the liquidity channel: deposits that banks cannot lend to good borrowers sit idle, and some of that money drifts into the share market, through banks' own investments or their customers, making it cheaper and easier to hold shares.

Nepal's history gives an unusually clear test of this link, because credit has swung very widely in a short time. One useful measure is credit-to-GDP: total bank lending as a share of the country's annual economic output (GDP). NRB research covering 1990 to 2025 puts the long-run average at about 45 percent, with a low near 11 percent in the early 1990s and a high just above 95 percent in 2022. The World Bank's latest figure, for 2024, puts private-sector credit at 92.11 percent of GDP — among the higher ratios in South Asia, and very high for a country at Nepal's income level. A ratio of 90 to 95 percent is not alarming in a rich economy with deep markets. But Nepal has shallow capital markets, few exports and a heavy dependence on remittance-funded spending. In that setting, such a high ratio means banks have grown faster than the real economy can use their money productively. That gap — more lending than real business can absorb — is exactly where price bubbles, including share-market bubbles, tend to form.

KEY CONCEPT Credit-to-GDP measures total outstanding bank and financial institution credit to the private sector divided by nominal GDP. A rising ratio signals the financial system is expanding faster than the real economy, a classic precondition for asset price inflation. In Nepal this ratio moved from roughly 75 percent in 2019 to just above 95 percent in 2022, the fastest three-year expansion the modern financial system has recorded, and NEPSE's all-time high arrived squarely inside that window.

For an investor, the lesson is simple but often ignored. However carefully you value one company, that work sits on top of a foundation of credit and liquidity, and the foundation can overpower company facts in either direction. A well-run development bank with clean loans will still see its share price fall when credit contracts, because every bank's funding costs rise and loan growth stalls at the same time. A mediocre finance company can see its price triple in a credit boom simply because money is plentiful and speculators are looking for shares that move fast. Picking stocks while ignoring the credit cycle is like a fisherman on the Koshi watching individual waves and ignoring the flood that is rising underneath them.

Lesson 2.2 — The Three Credit Cycles: Lessons from 1994, 2009, and 2021

Nepali monetary history since financial liberalization in the early 1990s divides cleanly into three credit boom-and-bust episodes, and each one left a visible fingerprint on NEPSE. Understanding these three episodes in sequence gives the investor a template for recognising the fourth one, whenever it arrives.

The first boom ran from 1994 to 1996. It followed the opening of the banking sector and the licensing of new joint-venture commercial banks in the late 1980s and early 1990s. NRB research shows credit-to-GDP growing by 20.9 percent in 1994, 29.2 percent in 1995 and 16.4 percent in 1996 — an extraordinary pace for an economy where, only a few years earlier, the state had decided almost all lending. NEPSE itself only began trading in 1994, so it is hard to see this boom in index data. But the same over-stretch showed up later. New, thinly capitalised banks had competed hard for loans, and by the end of the decade the sector had a serious problem with non-performing loans (NPLs — loans that borrowers have stopped repaying).

The second boom, from about 2007 to 2010, is the one most Nepali investors over forty remember. It centred on land and housing loans, which tied bank balance sheets, land prices and NEPSE tightly together. Credit-to-GDP rose from about 43.5 percent in 2007 to about 56 percent by 2009. Banks were flush with deposits from remittances and the post-conflict recovery, and they competed to finance land purchases and houses in the Kathmandu Valley and other cities. Land prices in parts of Kathmandu and Lalitpur rose several times over in two to three years. NEPSE followed the same logic. Bank and finance company shares — the easiest way for ordinary investors to ride the boom with borrowed money — rose sharply into a peak in August 2008. Then NRB stepped in. It capped how much banks could lend to real estate and tightened the rules for classifying property loans, because land prices were clearly distorted and bad loans were rising at several finance companies. The reversal was severe. Credit-to-GDP fell back to about 45.8 percent by 2012. Several finance companies and a few development banks failed or were forced to merge. NEPSE entered a long bear market and did not find a lasting bottom until 2011, in the low 300s — a fall of well over half.

The third boom was the biggest, and it matters most today, because its after-effects still shape NRB policy in 2025 and 2026. Credit-to-GDP jumped from 75.3 percent in 2019 to a record 95.03 percent in 2022 — the sharpest three-year rise on record. The cause was NRB's response to COVID-19. It cut policy rates, relaxed the rules for classifying and provisioning loans so pandemic-hit borrowers were not pushed into default, and pumped in refinancing money. At the same time, remittances could not be spent abroad because travel was frozen, so they piled up as bank deposits. Banks had surplus money and almost no normal borrowers: construction had stopped, tourism was dead and imports were disrupted. So they lent heavily for margin loans, real estate and consumption — the categories that reach NEPSE fastest. The index had traded near 1,184 in mid-2018 and touched about 1,189 in June 2020. It then climbed almost without a pause to an all-time closing high of 3,198.60 on 18 August 2021 — about 2.7 times its starting level, in barely fourteen months.

CASE IN POINT Between June 2020 and August 2021, NEPSE rose from roughly 1,189 to an all-time high of 3,198.60, a 2.7-fold rise (an increase of roughly 170 percent) in fourteen months, coinciding almost exactly with the period in which Nepal's credit-to-GDP ratio expanded from the high 70s to the low 90s (percent of GDP). Margin lending outstanding, real estate exposure, and consumption credit all grew far faster than the underlying loan book average during this same window. By mid-2022 the credit-to-deposit ratio had breached NRB's 90 percent regulatory ceiling, banks stopped disbursing fresh credit, the average base rate of commercial banks rose from roughly 6.84 percent to 8.98 percent within a year (pushing the weighted average lending rate above 10.5 percent), and the interbank rate spiked to 6.56 percent, consistently above its intended corridor. NEPSE fell by more than a third from its peak within months, and margin-loan borrowers who had pledged shares near the top were forced to liquidate into a falling market, an example of the forced-selling cascade discussed in Lesson 2.3.

The fall after the 2021 peak was a smaller, faster replay of 2009 to 2012. In 2022 the credit-to-deposit (CD) ratio — how many rupees a bank has lent for every 100 rupees of deposits — reached and then broke NRB's 90 percent ceiling. Banks then simply stopped making new loans of any kind, including margin loans, however good the borrower. The problem was a shortage of money in the whole system, not the quality of any one borrower. Deposits grew only about 4.1 percent while loans grew 10.5 percent, leaving banks short of funds. Interest rates on both deposits and loans rose sharply within one fiscal year. Share valuations fell across the board, and banks forced margin borrowers to sell, which made the fall worse. The lesson repeats in all three cycles: NEPSE booms are credit booms dressed up as share-market booms. And NEPSE busts are credit squeezes, enforced by the same lending ceilings — above all the CD ratio — that let the boom run so far in the first place.

Lesson 2.3 — Margin Lending Mechanics: How Nepalis Borrow to Buy Shares

In Nepal, margin lending means loans from "A", "B" and "C" class banks and financial institutions against listed shares you pledge as security, usually through your demat account. You can use the loan to buy more shares or for other cash needs. This is different from the margin trading that brokers offer directly in bigger markets. In recent years Nepal has also started letting licensed stockbrokers, with SEBON approval, offer small margin facilities of their own. But the bank margin-loan market is far larger, and it is the focus of this chapter.

NRB controls bank margin lending through four settings, which it has changed many times over the past decade. Each change alters how much borrowed buying power the whole system can create. The first is the loan-to-value ratio (LTV): the most a bank may lend as a share of the pledged shares' value, usually measured at the lower of the 180-day average price or the latest price. The second is the aggregate limit: how much of a bank's core capital (its own money — paid-up capital plus reserves) it may commit to margin loans in total. The third, until it was removed in late 2025, was the single-customer limit: a fixed rupee ceiling on how much any one borrower could take across all banks. The fourth is the risk weight: how much capital a bank must set aside against each rupee of margin loans. A higher risk weight makes margin lending less profitable for the bank, so banks offer less of it.

Regulatory Evolution: Margin Lending Limits

REGULATORY DETAIL The Loan-to-Value ceiling, the percentage of a pledged share's price a bank may lend against, has been raised three times since 2018: from 50 percent to 65 percent in December 2018, from 65 percent to 70 percent in the 2020-21 easing cycle, and most recently, under NRB's Unified Directives issued in mid-July 2026, to as high as 80 percent for shares of companies that meet a formal internal scoring standard covering paid-up capital size, listing duration, profitability, dividend history, credit ratings, and regulatory compliance. Each increase mechanically expands system-wide margin buying power without any change in the underlying quality of collateral.

Apart from the per-borrower caps of 2021–22, almost every revision has given the system more room to borrow against shares. That pattern tells you something. NRB has used margin lending rules as a lever to support the share market — and, through it, demand for new share issues — whenever it judged that credit needed a push. This was most visible in 2024 and 2025, as the slump after 2021 dragged on and NEPSE struggled to stay above the 2,800 to 3,000 range.

The October 2025 removal of the single-customer limit deserves special attention, because it changed how concentrated margin borrowing can become. Before that, there was a fixed rupee ceiling on how much any one investor could borrow against shares across all banks combined. That ceiling had risen from Rs 4 crore per bank in 2021–22 to Rs 25 crore (Rs 250 million) by the FY 2025/26 monetary policy. It slowed down any single investor trying to build a very large borrowed position. With it gone, the only firm limit is each bank's overall cap of 40 percent of core capital. In mid-2025, the core capital of the twenty commercial banks was estimated at about Rs 600 billion. That allowed about Rs 240 billion of margin lending across the system, of which about Rs 120 billion had already been lent and about Rs 120 billion was still available. In principle, a few large, well-connected borrowers can now use up a big slice of a bank's remaining room. As of mid-2025, Nabil Bank, Global IME Bank and Kumari Bank were among the biggest margin lenders in rupee terms; the newly merged Nepal Investment Mega Bank had the most unused room, and Siddhartha Bank the least.

WATCH FOR Total margin lending outstanding across the banking system stood at roughly Rs 76.5 billion in mid-July 2023 and had grown to approximately Rs 162.9 billion by mid-June 2026, an increase of over 110 percent in three years and a rise of about 16 percent in the final eleven months alone. Margin lending nonetheless still represents only about 2.7 percent of total bank credit outstanding, which sounds modest, but concentration matters more than the aggregate share: because margin lending is disbursed almost entirely against a narrow set of large-cap, liquid NEPSE counters, its marginal impact on those specific share prices, and on index-level sentiment, is far larger than its 2.7 percent share of the loan book would suggest.

Every investor who uses margin should know exactly how a margin call works in Nepal. The bank values your pledged shares every day, or nearly every day, at the market price. As the price falls, the loan becomes a bigger share of the shares' value. If it crosses the bank's maintenance threshold — set a little below the maximum LTV to give a buffer — the bank issues a margin call. You must then add cash, add more shares as security, or let the bank sell part of your shares to bring the ratio back. Margin loans in Nepal are concentrated in a small group of popular large shares. So when the whole market falls, many borrowers get margin calls at once, and forced selling hits the same few shares at the same time. Their prices fall further, which triggers more margin calls on other borrowers holding the same shares. It is like a crowded footbridge where one person slips and the whole crowd stumbles: the fall feeds itself, with nothing changing inside the companies. This is exactly what deepened NEPSE's fall after August 2021. Nepali commentators — including a widely discussed Kathmandu Post opinion column in mid-2026 — have warned it could happen again, and harder, given how much margin rules were loosened between 2024 and 2026.

Lesson 2.4 — Sector-wise Credit Caps and Real Estate Exposure

Besides margin rules, NRB also limits how much bank credit can go into sectors prone to bubbles — real estate and housing above all. The logic is the same. Land and shares are Nepal's two main speculative assets. Both are financed from the same pool of bank loans, and a boom in one often spills into the other, as happened in 2009–2010 and again in 2020–2021.

NRB has long capped the share of a bank's loans that can go to real estate and housing. It has raised and lowered that cap through different cycles. It loosened it when the sector needed money — for example after the 2015 earthquake, when rebuilding needed loans. It tightened it when the sector overheated — in 2009–2010, and to a lesser degree in 2021, when land prices and construction lending were rising fast. Within the real estate cap, home loans to families buying a house to live in have usually been treated more gently than commercial property or land-purchase loans. The reasoning is that a family home carries different risks, and has more social value, than land bought to sell later at a profit.

The latest easing cycle shows the pattern clearly. Under the FY 2025/26 monetary policy, NRB raised the largest housing loan that gets favourable treatment from Rs 20 million to Rs 30 million. It also set LTVs at 80 percent for first-time home buyers and 70 percent for others. This was a deliberate loosening to revive a construction and property sector that had slumped for years after the 2021–22 credit squeeze. The same October 2025 circular (Asoj 22, 2082) that removed the margin single-customer cap described it as removing the Single Obligor Limit on share-backed loans: the Rs 25 crore (Rs 250 million) ceiling on total borrowing against shares by one borrower, or a group of related borrowers, across all banks. Be precise about what changed. The removal applied only to loans backed by shares. The general limit on lending to one borrower in other loan categories, set as a percentage of a bank's core capital, stays in place. Even so, in the share-loan market the change removed the last fixed rupee brake on how big one borrower's position can grow. Only each bank's own risk checks and the overall 40-percent-of-core-capital ceiling are left.

REGULATORY DETAIL The Single Obligor Limit on share-backed loans — which had capped any one borrower's or related group's total share-collateral borrowing across all banks combined at Rs 25 crore (Rs 250 million) — was abolished under an amendment to NRB's Unified Directives issued on Asoj 22, 2082 (8 October 2025), acting on Capital Market Reform Taskforce recommendations. The same circular cut the minimum holding period for banks' own share investments from one year to six months and scrapped the 20-percent-of-core-capital annual cap on portfolio sales — collectively the most significant loosening of share-market-related banking regulation in Nepal's post-2010 history.

You can see the risk in NRB's own data on what secures bank loans. In the latest breakdown, for the first half of FY 2025/26, loans backed by land and buildings made up 63.9 percent of all outstanding bank credit. That includes loans officially booked for other purposes, such as working capital or margin loans, but secured by property. Loans secured by current assets — stock and receivables of farms and businesses — made up another 15 percent. So the banking system, and with it NEPSE, stays very sensitive to land prices, whatever the loans are officially for. Most of the loan book rests on one kind of asset, and land prices tend to rise and fall together.

Lesson 2.5 — Reading Credit Growth Data as a Leading Indicator for NEPSE

NRB publishes detailed monthly and half-yearly statistics, including the "Current Macroeconomic and Financial Situation" report and regular monetary policy reviews. They break down credit growth by type of borrower, by sector and by type of institution. They are free on NRB's website. In this book's view, they are the most under-used research source among Nepali retail investors, most of whom never look beyond the daily NEPSE prices and company quarterly reports.

The latest half-year data available as this chapter is written covers the first six months of FY 2025/26 (mid-July 2025 to mid-January 2026). It shows both the value and the limits of this data as an early warning. Private-sector credit grew 3.6 percent in those six months — about Rs 197.47 billion of new lending, on top of Rs 5,695.17 billion already outstanding. Compared with a year earlier ("point-to-point" growth), credit was up 6.7 percent, well below NRB's 12.0 percent target for the year. The Rs 197.47 billion of new credit was also well below the roughly Rs 265.56 billion lent in the same six months a year before. Commercial banks grew their loans by 3.7 percent, development banks by 2.9 percent, and finance companies by only 1.2 percent. That order tells a story. Smaller lenders, which pay more for their deposits, feel a credit slowdown first. So finance-company loan growth is a useful early-warning signal: it tends to turn before commercial-bank loan growth does.

PRACTICAL TOOL Before trading on any macro thesis, check NRB's "Current Macroeconomic and Financial Situation" report, published on the NRB website typically within four to six weeks of each fiscal-year quarter's close, and the annual Monetary Policy document, published each mid-July (Ashadh/Shrawan). Both are free, in English, and contain the sector-wise and institution-wise credit growth tables referenced throughout this chapter. Cross-reference the credit-to-deposit ratio reported there against the regulatory ceiling (approximately 90 percent) each time it is published; a ratio pressing against that ceiling in 2021–22 preceded a NEPSE correction of significant magnitude within two to four quarters.

The data on who is borrowing carries another signal that is easy to miss. In the same release, businesses (non-financial institutions such as property developers, traders and factories) held 62.7 percent of outstanding credit, and individuals and households held 37.3 percent. Watch where new lending is growing fastest. In this period it was consumer loans (9.1 percent, the fastest of any category) and margin loans (8.3 percent), not loans to build factories or businesses. When credit grows mainly to fund spending and speculation rather than new productive capacity, the growth has historically been less durable and more likely to reverse sharply than growth in industrial or export lending.

In practice, check three numbers each time NRB publishes new figures, at least every quarter. First, private-sector credit growth compared with NRB's own target. Growth running well below target — like the 6.7 percent against a 12.0 percent target in early FY 2025/26 — means banks are still cautious, and NEPSE rallies are less likely to be fuelled by credit and more likely to fade. Second, the CD ratio compared with the roughly 90 percent ceiling. A ratio climbing toward the ceiling, as in 2022, is the most reliable early warning this chapter can offer of a coming liquidity squeeze and NEPSE correction. Third, margin lending growth compared with total credit growth. When margin loans grow much faster than lending overall — as in most periods covered in this chapter — an outsized share of new credit is flowing straight into shares. That has usually pushed prices up in the short run. But it has also been followed by a sharper-than-usual fall once the credit cycle turns.

Lesson 2.6 — Regulatory Risk: Why NRB's Next Circular Matters More Than Any Chart

Every rule described in this chapter — higher LTV ceilings, the removal of borrower limits, lower risk weights on margin loans, bigger housing loans — can be reversed by NRB with a single directive. Nepal's history shows NRB has done exactly that, sharply and with little warning, more than once. The 2009–2010 real estate tightening and the 2022 lending freeze driven by the CD ratio both arrived with little lead time compared with how hard they hit the market. Both times, the investors who suffered most were those who had borrowed to buy shares, assuming the rules would stay the same.

As of mid-2026, the trend is clearly toward looser rules. The margin LTV ceiling has been raised to as high as 80 percent for scored companies. The single-borrower limit on share-backed loans has been abolished. Risk weights on margin loans have been cut, and housing loan limits widened. Margin loans outstanding were about Rs 162.9 billion in mid-2026. That is still only 2.7 percent of all bank loans, but it is up more than 110 percent in three years. NEPSE fell to about 2,490 in October 2025, rallied to about 2,960 by late March 2026, then slipped back to about 2,600 by mid-July 2026 — still well below the August 2021 high, but again reacting to the same credit forces described in this chapter. In July 2026, total bank loans of about Rs 5,915 billion against deposits of about Rs 8,268 billion put the CD ratio comfortably below the 90 percent ceiling. That cuts both ways. The current easing has room to run before it hits the limit that ended the 2021 boom. But when the ratio does approach the ceiling again, the reversal is likely to be at least as sudden as in 2022, because NRB treats that ceiling as a hard wall, not a gentle slope.

WARNING Every regulatory relaxation catalogued in this chapter increases the system's capacity to generate a credit-fuelled NEPSE rally, and every one of them is reversible without advance notice through a single NRB unified directive. Nepal's banking regulator has changed margin lending and sector-wise exposure rules more than half a dozen times since 2018 alone. An investor who has built a leveraged position on the assumption of continued regulatory support for margin lending is making an implicit bet on NRB policy continuity that history does not support; the 2022 credit-to-deposit-ratio-driven lending freeze arrived with only a few months of visible warning in the underlying data and considerably less warning in public communication.

The right response is a discipline, not a forecast. First, never treat today's maximum LTV as a safe level to borrow up to. Keep well below it. The bank's margin-call level sits below the maximum to protect the bank, not you, and a falling market can wipe out that buffer in days. Second, track the CD ratio and private-sector credit growth each time NRB publishes them. Both have usually moved against NEPSE two to four quarters ahead, which gives you real time to reduce risk before a broad fall. Third, treat any sudden jump in margin lending growth — like 2020–2021 and again 2024–2026 — as a sign that the popular large shares used as security are becoming more fragile, not safer because so many people hold them. Fourth, accept that a bank-dominated, land-secured financial system like Nepal's will keep producing credit cycles as big as 1994–96, 2008–10 and 2020–22. Until the make-up of the market and the way it is financed change, NEPSE will keep trading as a geared bet on that cycle rather than as a mirror of company profits.

CAUTION Do not confuse a rising NEPSE index with rising corporate value creation during a credit boom. In each of Nepal's three identified credit cycles, share price appreciation substantially outpaced any measurable improvement in the productive capacity or export competitiveness of the underlying economy. The gains were real for those who exited before the reversal and were erased, often with borrowed capital attached, for those who did not.

Chapter recap

Nepal's credit-to-GDP ratio has gone through three booms and busts since banking was opened up: the 1994–96 expansion; the 2008–10 real estate boom, which pushed the ratio from about 43.5 percent to 56 percent before years of contraction; and the 2020–22 pandemic surge, which took it from 75.3 percent to a record 95.03 percent and carried NEPSE from about 1,189 to an all-time high of 3,198.60 in fourteen months. Each cycle ended when bank lending hit NRB's ceiling — first the older credit-to-core-capital-and-deposit limit of about 80 percent, and from 2021–22 the 90 percent CD ceiling — and forced a sudden lending freeze with little warning.

Margin lending is the most direct path from bank credit to NEPSE buying. NRB controls it with four levers: the loan-to-value ceiling, the overall limit as a share of core capital, the single-customer limit (now abolished), and the risk weight for capital purposes. Every one of these has been loosened repeatedly since 2018 — most of all through the October 2025 removal of the single-customer cap and the July 2026 rise in the LTV ceiling to 80 percent for scored companies.

Real estate loans and margin loans come from the same pool of bank credit and have usually moved together. The concentration is built into the system: land and buildings secured 63.9 percent of all outstanding bank credit in the latest breakdown. So the banking system, and therefore NEPSE, stays highly sensitive to land prices, whatever category new loans are booked under.

NRB's monthly and half-yearly reports — the CD ratio, credit growth by sector and loan growth by type of institution — work as real early-warning signals for NEPSE, usually two to four quarters ahead. If you track margin lending growth against total credit growth, and the CD ratio against its ceiling, you get advance warning of both rallies and falls that chart reading or company analysis alone cannot give.

As of mid-2026, the rules are firmly in a loosening phase. Margin loans outstanding have passed Rs 162.9 billion, more than double their mid-2023 level, though they are still only about 2.7 percent of all bank credit. Fast growth from a low base, combined with steadily looser limits, is the same pattern that came before both the 2009–10 and the 2021–22 reversals.

If you borrow to buy NEPSE shares on today's margin terms, size the position assuming NRB can tighten those terms suddenly — because it has, more than once, with little public warning. Stay well below the maximum LTV, check the CD ratio every time NRB reports it, and treat credit-driven rallies as chances to buy with a clear exit plan, not as proof that prices have moved to a permanently higher level.
