WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Income statement — the profit and loss account: income earned and costs paid over a period. Net interest income (NII) — a bank's interest earned on loans minus interest paid to depositors, like a grain trader's buying-and-selling margin. Impairment charge — the cost a bank books for loans that may not be repaid. Royalty — the fee a hydropower company pays the government for using the river. Underwriting result — an insurer's premiums minus claims and costs, before investment income. EPS — earnings per share: profit divided by the number of shares.

Lesson 26.1 — The Architecture of Income: Why Banks, Hydropower, and Insurers Don't Look Alike

Three businesses, three shapes

An income statement — the profit and loss account — is not a neutral list of income minus costs. Its shape comes from the business behind it. NEPSE's three biggest sectors earn money in completely different ways:

  • A bank earns by borrowing from depositors and lending at a higher rate — like a grain trader buying from farmers and selling in town.
  • A hydropower company earns by turning a fixed, weather-dependent plant into electricity sold under a contract.
  • An insurer earns by collecting premiums today against promises to pay uncertain claims later.

Read all three the same way, and you will misjudge at least two of them.

Who regulates what

  • About forty percent of NEPSE's tradable value is in banks, development banks and finance companies, supervised by Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB) under its Unified Directives and its Guidance Note on Interest Income Recognition.
  • A large slice is in hydropower companies, most of which sell all their power under long-term Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) to the Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA), the state-owned single buyer.
  • Life and non-life insurers form a smaller but distinct third group, regulated by the Nepal Insurance Authority (formerly the Insurance Board).

This chapter builds a separate mental template for each sector, then covers two topics that apply to every company — how bonus shares change earnings per share, and how dividend announcements can mislead — and ends with a checklist of the most common ways results are dressed up.

KEY CONCEPT Always read a Nepali income statement in its regulator's format. Banks follow NRB's format; insurers follow the Insurance Authority's; hydropower companies follow the general NFRS format with no sector template. That last point is itself informative: hydropower income statements vary more from company to company than bank or insurer statements do.

Lesson 26.2 — Reading a Bank's Income Statement: Net Interest Income and the Impairment Line

Start with net interest income

A bank's main line is net interest income (NII): interest earned on loans, investments and lending to other banks, minus interest paid on deposits, borrowings and debentures. It usually makes up about 70 to 80 percent of a Nepali commercial bank's operating income (Chapter 0.2), the rest coming from fees, trading and other income. Its path over four to eight quarters tells you more about the bank's core health than any other single number.

Interest income and the suspense account

Reported interest income is not simply the loan rate times the loan book. NRB's rules on this began with a 2019 guideline. Since July 2025 they sit in a Guidance Note on Interest Income Recognition, which sorts every loan by its NFRS 9 credit-loss "stage" at the end of the previous quarter. Banks may count interest as it falls due on Stage 1 and Stage 2 loans — broadly "Pass" loans, "Watchlist" loans and restructured loans that are still being repaid.

For Substandard, Doubtful and Loss loans, interest due goes into an interest suspense account instead of profit, and is counted only when actually paid in cash. Think of a shopkeeper who writes a doubtful customer's dues in a separate notebook and counts them only when the money arrives.

So as a bank's bad loans rise, its interest income falls automatically — even before any provision is booked. If NII slows in a quarter while the loan book is still growing, check the NPL ratio and the suspense movement in the notes. A growing book with slowing NII is a classic early sign of loan trouble.

REGULATORY DETAIL NRB sorts loans into five classes — Pass (up to 1 percent provision), Watchlist (5 percent), Substandard (25 percent), Doubtful (50 percent) and Loss (100 percent). The pass-loan rate has moved several times: NRB had raised it to 1.25 percent, then cut it in steps — to 1.20 percent, to 1.10 percent in the 2024/25 monetary policy, and back to 1.0 percent by March 2025 (NRB Monetary Policy 2025/26). Check the current Unified Directive before relying on any one figure. A loan overdue by more than a year is usually classed Loss whatever its collateral. How fast a bank's loans slide down these classes, shown in the provision note, is a better forward signal than the NPL ratio itself: the NPL ratio is a snapshot, while the slide is the movement.

Fees and other income

Below NII come:

  • fee and commission income — service charges, loan processing fees, guarantee commissions, remittance fees and card income;
  • net trading gain — mostly foreign-exchange dealing;
  • other operating income — often dividends from subsidiaries, gains on selling property taken over from defaulters ("non-banking assets") and one-off items.

Slow down here. A bank's core strength is NII plus fees. If "other operating income" is unusually large in a quarter — especially gains on non-banking assets or one-time recoveries of written-off loans — part of the profit will not repeat. Banks break this line down in the notes. Checking it before accepting a quarter's EPS takes five minutes and is one of the most valuable habits you can build.

The impairment line

"Impairment charge/(reversal) for loans and other losses" is where the credit cycle is booked. Under NFRS 9, banks estimate expected credit losses (ECL) with a forward-looking model. But NRB's minimum provisions also apply, and where NRB's figure is higher, the bank must keep the higher one through the regulatory reserve.

So this line is a mix of accounting judgement and regulatory floor. Sharp swings — especially big reversals of past provisions — deserve scrutiny. A bank under earnings pressure has a reason to release provisions in a weak quarter to protect headline profit.

WATCH FOR A bank reporting flat or rising profit while its NII falls and its impairment charge falls or reverses in the same quarter is very often managing the bottom line through provisions, not better business. Check the impairment line against the NPL ratio and the provision balance in the same statements. They should move the same way; when they do not, that difference is your finding.

Down to net profit

After impairment come operating expenses (staff, premises and other costs), operating profit, non-operating items (such as gains or losses on selling fixed assets), profit before tax, income tax, and net profit.

Below net profit, in the statement of changes in equity, banks must also transfer amounts to the regulatory reserve — for example, the gap between NFRS 9 and NRB provisions, deferred tax assets, and actuarial gains on staff pensions. This is not part of the income statement, but it reduces the free reserves available for cash or bonus dividends (Lesson 26.5).

Lesson 26.3 — Reading a Hydropower Company's Income Statement: Revenue, Royalty, and Depreciation

A hydropower income statement looks simple next to a bank's: one revenue line, a few costs, depreciation, finance cost and profit. The simplicity is misleading. Most of the analysis sits in two things that never appear as separate lines: the terms of the PPA, and the policy for depreciating the plant.

Revenue under the PPA

Most NEPSE hydropower companies sell all their power to the NEA under a PPA lasting typically 20 to 35 years from the commercial operation date. Revenue is counted as power is delivered, at the PPA rate — and that rate is not flat:

  • Wet season (Baisakh to Mangsir, roughly mid-April to mid-December): a lower rate.
  • Dry season (Poush to Chaitra, roughly mid-December to mid-April): a much higher rate, because dry-season power is scarce.

A run-of-river plant has no storage. It produces most of its power in the monsoon at the lower rate and little in winter at the higher rate. So the same company's quarterly revenue can differ two or three times between a monsoon quarter and a winter quarter — with no change in the plant at all.

Compare its first quarter (mid-July to mid-October, peak monsoon) with its third quarter (mid-January to mid-April, the lean season) without adjusting for this, and you will reach exactly the wrong conclusion. It is like judging a mango seller's business by comparing Asar sales with Magh sales.

CASE IN POINT Two hydropower companies of similar size can report very different yearly revenue because one has real dry-season output (from a plant with a height or storage advantage, or a peaking design) and the other is pure run-of-river, with output collapsing in winter. Comparing their P/E ratios without first checking each one's wet-to-dry output mix — given in the directors' report or management discussion — compares two different cash-flow shapes as if they were the same.

A second, quieter issue is hydrology risk. A weak monsoon, damage from a glacial lake outburst flood, or turbine wear from sediment can each push a year's output well below the PPA's expected average. This shows up only as lower revenue, with no special line. Read the directors' report to learn whether a soft year was due to water (probably temporary) or to the plant (possibly lasting — repeated outages, grid curtailment, or transformer and turbine failures).

WATCH FOR Curtailment by the NEA — when the grid cannot take all the power a plant could produce, especially in monsoons crowded by new run-of-river plants — is an increasingly common reason revenue falls short of what the plant can generate. It is a sector-wide risk, outside the company's control, and it can cap the earnings of even a well-run plant. Look in the directors' report for "deemed generation" or claims against the NEA for curtailment compensation.

Royalty

Below revenue sits royalty paid to the Government of Nepal under the Electricity Act framework, in two parts:

  • a capacity royalty — a fixed charge per kilowatt installed, paid whatever the output;
  • an energy royalty — a percentage of the value of power produced, which rises after the plant's early years of concession.

Find the royalty step-up date in the company's licence terms. Profit usually drops in the year the energy royalty rises — an entirely predictable event that the market still sometimes treats as a surprise.

Depreciation and the IFRIC 12 question

The most important accounting choice in a hydropower income statement is how the plant and civil works are written off:

  • as ordinary property, plant and equipment, depreciated over the plant's physical life (perhaps 35 or 40 years); or
  • under the rules for service concessions (NFRS / IFRIC 12), as a financial asset or an intangible right, written off over the PPA or licence term (perhaps 30 years).

This is unsettled in Nepal: auditors and companies differ on whether the NEA's single-buyer role makes it a "grantor" under IFRIC 12. The result is that depreciation charges are not always comparable between hydropower companies, even of the same size and cost. A shorter write-off period means a bigger yearly charge and lower reported profit in the early years, though the cash earned may be identical. Before deciding that one hydropower share is "cheaper" on P/E, read the depreciation policy note.

Finance cost

Hydropower projects carry heavy debt — 60:40, 70:30 or more is routine — so interest on project loans (usually shared among several Nepali banks) is a big cost. During construction, interest is added to the plant's cost (capitalised) instead of being expensed.

That creates a timing trap. Just before the plant starts commercial operation, finance cost looks low and profit looks flattering. On the day capitalisation stops, the same interest moves onto the income statement, and finance cost can jump sharply in one quarter with no change in the loan.

WARNING Some hydropower companies have kept capitalising costs — including some running costs during a long "trial run" after the plant was physically finished — beyond what NFRS strictly allows. This delays expenses and shows a better pre-operation profit to people being asked to buy rights or bonus shares. Treat an unusually long gap between announced physical completion and declared commercial operation as worth investigating in the notes on capitalised interest and work in progress.

Lesson 26.4 — Reading an Insurer's Income Statement: Premium, Claims, and the Actuarial Reserve

Insurance accounts rest on an idea foreign to banks and hydropower: the company collects cash now (premium) against a promise to pay an uncertain amount later (claims). The whole art is estimating that future cost today.

From gross premium to net premium

The top line is gross written premium — all premium written in the period. From it the insurer deducts reinsurance premium ceded — the share of risk (and premium) passed to a reinsurer, often Nepal Reinsurance Company or a foreign reinsurer, to limit large or catastrophic losses. What remains is net premium income, the risk the insurer actually keeps.

Watch the share ceded over time. A rising share means the insurer keeps less risk — cautious, but it caps profit. A falling share at the same time as fast premium growth can mean the insurer keeps more risk than its capital can comfortably carry. Its solvency filings with the Insurance Authority, not the income statement, will confirm which.

Claims and the reserving judgement

Below net premium sits net claims incurred (for life insurers, claims and benefits plus the change in actuarial reserves). This is where the biggest judgement lies, because IBNR claims — "incurred but not reported", losses that have happened but not yet been claimed — must be estimated by an actuary. A change in assumptions can move profit widely with no change in actual claims.

Nepal Reinsurance Company's widely reported multi-arba-rupee loss, driven by a sharp rise in claims from Nepali insurers including weather and catastrophe losses, shows how quickly claims can swamp premium when reserving proves too optimistic in one bad year.

For life insurers, the actuarial reserve — today's value of future policy benefits minus future premiums, worked out by the appointed actuary using mortality, lapse and discount-rate assumptions — is both a liability and, through its yearly change, a cost. A life insurer can show strong premium growth while profit stays flat or falls, because claims and reserve increases absorb the gain.

Commission and management expenses

Insurers pay agents and brokers commission — a large cost in Nepal's agent-driven market, especially for life insurance. Their management expenses are capped by the Insurance Authority as a share of premium. An insurer persistently above the cap is flagged by the regulator and may later face limits on new business or raising capital. That risk shows first in a note, long before it becomes headline news.

Investment income

Insurers hold large investment portfolios funded by premiums — especially life insurers, whose promises run for decades. Their investment income (interest on government securities and fixed deposits, dividends, and gains on shares and mutual funds) is often bigger and steadier than the profit from insurance itself.

KEY CONCEPT For any insurer, work out the underwriting result (net premium minus net claims, commission and management expenses) separately from the investment result. A company earning all its profit from investments while its insurance business loses money is not being paid for the insurance risk it takes. It is really an investment fund with an insurance licence attached — a weaker kind of earnings than profitable underwriting.

Lesson 26.5 — Bonus Shares, Rights Issues, and the Arithmetic of EPS

No topic confuses NEPSE investors more than what bonus shares do to earnings per share (EPS) — profit divided by the number of shares. It deserves a slow, step-by-step look.

What a bonus issue does

A bonus issue turns part of a company's free reserves (for banks, with NRB approval and enough capital) into new shares given to existing owners in proportion — "10 percent bonus" means 10 new shares for every 100 held.

A bonus creates no new value. Total equity is unchanged; it is simply cut into more pieces, like cutting the same roti into eleven slices instead of ten. That is why NEPSE adjusts the price down on the ex-bonus date. A share at Rs 500 before a 10 percent bonus opens at about Rs 455 (500 ÷ 1.10). You own 10 percent more shares at a lower price; your wealth is the same until the market re-rates the share.

Restated EPS: the rule to know

Under NFRS, following IAS 33, when bonus shares are issued, the share count used for EPS must be restated for all periods shown — as if the bonus shares had always existed. So when a bank shows "restated EPS" for last year, last year's profit has not changed at all. Only the number of shares dividing it has changed, so that the two years can be compared fairly.

PRACTICAL TOOL To check any company's post-bonus EPS: divide this year's profit by the new (post-bonus) number of shares, and compare it with the "restated EPS" given for last year. If EPS fell from Rs 25 to Rs 20 only because of a 25 percent bonus while profit stayed flat, profitability has not worsened: Rs 25 per 100 old shares has become Rs 20 per 125 new shares — the same wealth, sliced differently. Always ask: did net profit itself rise, fall or stay flat? That is the real performance question.

Mistaking a bonus-driven EPS fall for a profit fall has caused mispriced sell-offs in Nepali bank and hydropower shares around AGM season, as investors read "EPS fell" in a headline without checking why. Before reacting to any EPS change, split it into the part due to profit and the part due to the share count.

Rights issues are different

A rights issue sells new shares to existing owners at a set price, often below the market price, and brings new cash into the company. It is treated differently. Because a rights issue below market price contains a hidden bonus element (owners who do not take it up are diluted at less than market value), the rules require an adjustment using a theoretical ex-rights price (TERP). The bonus part is restated backwards; the new-money part counts from the issue date.

Companies and auditors calculate this and show the restated EPS in the annual report, so you rarely need to work out TERP yourself. Just remember: a rights issue really does dilute EPS in a way a bonus does not, because it enlarges the capital the company must now earn a return on.

How dividends are announced

Nepali companies, especially banks, usually announce one combined "dividend" figure that bundles cash and bonus shares. "20 percent dividend" might be 15 percent bonus plus 5 percent cash, or another split — and the split matters greatly for the cash you actually receive.

Under NRB's dividend rules, any bank dividend (cash or bonus) may be paid only from distributable profit after regulatory adjustments, including the regulatory reserve transfers in Lesson 26.2. And NRB must approve a bank's dividend before it is final, so the board's proposal and the approved dividend can differ. Treat proposed figures in the media as provisional until NRB approval is confirmed in the book-closure notice.

CAUTION When a company — especially a bank short of capital — announces a headline "dividend" percentage, check the cash-versus-bonus split before expecting cash. A "12 percent dividend" that is all bonus shares returns no cash at all. It only raises the share count, and the price falls to match on the ex-date. Your real return depends on what the market does with the adjusted price, not on the announced percentage.

Lesson 26.6 — The Analyst's Red Flags: How Nepali Companies Dress Up the Income Statement

With the three templates built, you need a checklist of the earnings-management techniques most often seen in NEPSE results — sometimes outright misstatement, more often aggressive but defensible accounting choices.

1. Delaying loan downgrades

Because NRB's suspense rules depend on loan class, a bank under pressure may keep a weakening loan in Watchlist instead of Substandard for as long as the paperwork allows, so its interest still flows to profit. Evergreening — rolling over a struggling loan, sometimes with a small fresh loan used to pay the old loan's interest — keeps a loan "performing" on paper after its cash flow has failed. NRB inspection findings, when reported in the press, are the best outside check on a bank's self-reported loan quality.

2. Smoothing with provisions

ECL models depend heavily on management's assumptions (probability of default, loss given default, economic outlook). A bank can soften a weak quarter by using a cheerier outlook or releasing provisions built up earlier. Your defence is the trend: follow provisions as a share of the loan book over eight to twelve quarters, and treat any sharp single-quarter reversal with suspicion until you have checked the NPL and loan-growth figures.

3. One-off income propping up profit

Banks and hydropower companies have sometimes covered a weak quarter with one-off gains — a bank selling non-banking assets, a hydropower company booking an insurance settlement or one-time NEA curtailment compensation — inside "other income" without highlighting it. Read the breakdown of other income, line by line, before accepting net profit as typical.

4. Capitalising costs too long

As Lesson 26.3 showed, stretching the capitalisation period for hydropower costs inflates the asset and delays expenses, flattering profit before listing or a rights issue. The tell is a gap between physical completion and declared commercial operation longer than commissioning should need.

5. Insurers changing reserving assumptions

A change in mortality or lapse assumptions, or in the IBNR method, can move an insurer's profit a lot in one year. These are defensible actuarial judgements, rarely outright misstatement. But when underwriting results jump in the same year as a disclosed change in assumptions, examine the assumption change itself, not just the profit.

6. Timing bonus and rights announcements

Management controls when and how big a bonus is, relative to the results announcement. Some companies have highlighted strong absolute profit while giving the bonus-driven EPS dilution less prominence in the same release. This is about emphasis, not numbers — but it is exactly what you see through by going straight to the statements and notes instead of headlines.

7. Related-party deals

Loans to firms linked to a bank's promoters, insurance written for related parties on soft terms, or hydropower construction contracts given to promoter-linked contractors can shift profit or loss around a business group. Related-party notes are required. A widening related-party balance with no clear business reason deserves direct questions.

WARNING None of these techniques is, on its own, proof of fraud. Most sit within the judgement NFRS allows, and auditors sign them off as fair. Your job is not to assume wrongdoing, but to work out a normal, sustainable profit by removing one-off, discretionary and timing items — and to value the company on that figure, not on headline profit or EPS.

That is why comparing hydropower shares needs more digging in the notes than comparing banks.

Chapter recap

  • Banks, hydropower companies and insurers earn money in different ways, so read each income statement with its own template and in its regulator's format.
  • Banks: start with net interest income. Interest on bad loans goes to suspense, so rising NPLs cut income early. Check one-off "other income", and treat provision reversals alongside falling NII as a warning.
  • Hydropower: revenue swings with the season (higher dry-season rate, lower monsoon output). Watch hydrology, NEA curtailment, the royalty step-up, the depreciation policy (physical life vs IFRIC 12 concession term) and the jump in finance cost after construction ends.
  • Insurers: separate the underwriting result from investment income; watch reinsurance, IBNR and actuarial assumptions, and the management-expense cap.
  • Bonus shares create no value; EPS is restated for the new share count. Ask whether profit itself grew. Rights issues add capital and really dilute EPS.
  • Check the cash-versus-bonus split of any "dividend", and treat bank dividends as provisional until NRB approves.
  • Before valuing, strip out delayed downgrades, provision smoothing, one-off gains, over-long capitalisation, assumption changes and related-party effects to find a sustainable profit.
