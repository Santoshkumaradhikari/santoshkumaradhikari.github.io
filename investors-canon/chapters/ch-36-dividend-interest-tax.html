WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Cash dividend — part of profit paid to owners in money. TDS — tax deducted at source. Final withholding payment — under Section 92, a deduction that completes your tax on that income. Deemed dividend — a bonus share treated by law as if a dividend of its face value had been paid. Debenture — a long-term loan to a company that pays fixed interest (the coupon) and trades on NEPSE. PAN — Permanent Account Number, your tax identity; link it to your demat and bank accounts.

Lakshmi had held 200 shares of a hydropower company for three years. When her first dividend arrived, it was about 5% smaller than she had worked out from the company's notice. Her bank statement showed a line she had never noticed: "TDS on dividend". She rang her broker, sure it was a mistake.

It was not a mistake. Lakshmi had met Nepal's withholding system: the government takes its tax on investment income before the investor ever sees the money. This chapter explains how that works for dividends and interest, and what — if anything — you need to do afterwards.

Lesson 36.1 — Cash Dividends and the Five Percent Withholding

The rule

A cash dividend is a share of the company's profit paid to owners in money. Every NEPSE-listed company is a resident company, and every cash dividend it pays carries tax.

Under Section 88 of the Income Tax Act, 2058 (2002) and its Schedule 1, the company must keep back 5% of the gross dividend and pay only the rest to the shareholder. This is called TDS — "tax deducted at source". It is a legal duty, not a courtesy. A company that fails to deduct correctly must pay the missing tax to the Inland Revenue Department (IRD) itself.

How the money moves

  1. The board proposes a dividend as a percentage of paid-up capital. "20 percent" means Rs 20 for each Rs 100 share.
  2. The Annual General Meeting (AGM) approves it.
  3. The company, usually through its share registrar and the bank account linked to your demat account, works out each shareholder's gross dividend.
  4. It deducts 5% and sends the other 95% to your bank account.
  5. It deposits the 5% with the IRD, tagged to your PAN (Permanent Account Number) where it has one, within 25 days after the end of that month — the normal deadline for all TDS in Nepal.

For Lakshmi: a 10% dividend on 200 shares of Rs 100 is Rs 2,000. She receives Rs 1,900. The other Rs 100 is already with the IRD in her name.

REGULATORY DETAIL The 5% dividend rate is set in Schedule 1 of the Income Tax Act, 2058. It has stayed the same through every Finance Act from FY 2077/78 to FY 2083/84. Bank interest is different: its rate went up from 5% to 6% in 2023. Dividend TDS has been the most stable rate in Nepal's whole withholding list.

The rate is the same whatever the size of the dividend and whatever the sector. A family with 200 shares and an institution with 2 lakh shares of the same company both lose 5% at payment. What differs is what that 5% means afterwards.

KEY CONCEPT Withholding describes how tax is collected: at source, before you are paid. A final tax describes whether that deduction settles your tax completely. Think of a ticket checker at a bus park. Withholding is the checker taking the fare at the gate. "Final" means the fare covers the whole journey — no extra charge at the end. For individuals receiving dividends, the 5% is almost always both. For institutions, it usually is not. Mixing up the two is the most common mistake investors make when reading their dividend statements.

Lesson 36.2 — Final Withholding: Why Dividend Income Never Touches Your Tax Return

What "final" saves you

Section 92 of the Income Tax Act calls some payments a "final withholding payment". For these, the tax taken at source completes your tax on that income. You do not add it to your salary. You do not run it through the income tax slabs. It leaves the tax system the moment the 5% is cut.

Dividends received by a person as an investor fall into this group. That is good news for a salaried worker, a retired teacher, a shopkeeper or a homemaker who has put family savings into NEPSE. Income tax slabs for individuals rise with income, and the top slabs are far above 5%. Dividends skip them entirely.

Example: an investor receives Rs 5 lakh in cash dividends in a year. The tax is exactly Rs 25,000 — even if their salary already puts them in the top slab.

CASE IN POINT Two people in Kathmandu each earn Rs 18 lakh a year. The first earns it all as salary. Much of that salary is taxed in the higher slabs, at 20% and above. The second earns Rs 12 lakh in salary and Rs 6 lakh in dividends from a NEPSE portfolio built over ten years. Only the Rs 12 lakh goes through the slabs. The Rs 6 lakh is taxed at a flat 5%. Same income, clearly less tax. This is not a loophole. Section 92 was designed this way to encourage more Nepalis to own shares.

The narrow exception

The final treatment applies to a person receiving dividends as an investor. It does not apply when the dividend is earned inside a share-dealing business — for example, a broker firm run as a sole proprietorship, or a person whose registered business is trading shares.

In that case the dividend is business income. It is added to the business's profit, and the 5% becomes an advance credit, not a final settlement. For an ordinary investor who buys shares for personal savings, however actively, this exception almost never applies. It explains why brokers and merchant banks show dividends differently in their own accounts.

WARNING "Final" does not mean you must hide or ignore dividend income. If you already file a tax return for other reasons — a registered business, rent, or other income that is not final — most Nepali tax practitioners advise listing dividends and interest in the return as final-taxed income, even though it does not change the tax you pay. This matches the TDS records the IRD already holds against your PAN, and avoids questions later if your savings look larger than your taxable income alone would explain.

If your only incomes are salary (already taxed at source) and investment income that is all final-withheld, Nepal's system generally does not require you to file a return at all. But the filing rules depend on your total income mix and change from time to time, so check the current rules rather than treating this as a blanket rule.

Lesson 36.3 — Bonus Shares: The Dividend You Pay Tax On Before You Own It

A dividend paid in shares

Bonus shares are new shares given free to existing owners. The company turns saved profits (its reserves) into share capital. No cash moves. Yet the law taxes them as if a dividend had been paid at the shares' face value.

Under Section 53(1) of the Income Tax Act, a bonus issue is treated as a dividend equal to the face value of the new shares — on NEPSE almost always Rs 100 a share. That "deemed" dividend carries the same 5% tax as a cash dividend.

The problem is obvious: with no cash payment, there is nothing to cut the tax from. It is like a farmer paid in sacks of rice who still owes a cash fee to the cooperative.

How the tax is usually paid: the small cash dividend

The tax must be settled before CDSC credits the bonus shares to shareholders' demat accounts. That is one reason bonus shares can arrive weeks after the AGM.

In practice, most NEPSE companies solve the cash problem by declaring a small cash dividend alongside the bonus, sized to cover the 5% tax on the whole dividend. The cash is then withheld as tax, so the shareholder receives the shares but little or no cash. Two FY 2082/83 examples (Insurance Khabar, 21 November 2026):

  • Citizens Bank International: 3.80% bonus + 0.20% cash = 4% total. 5% of 4 is 0.20 — the cash part exactly covers the tax.
  • Laxmi Sunrise Bank: 5% bonus + 0.26% cash = 5.26% total. 5% of 5.26 is about 0.26 — again, the cash part covers the tax.

When you see a dividend like "5% bonus and 0.26% cash", read the cash part as "tax money", not as income for you. If a company declares bonus shares with no cash part, the tax still has to be paid; the arrangement differs from company to company, so read the notice.

WARNING Many investors think bonus shares are "free" with no tax. To the tax office, a bonus share is a dividend paid in kind. Not seeing a deduction on your statement does not mean no tax was paid — it was usually paid from the small cash dividend declared with the bonus. This matters most when you sell, because of the cost question below.

Why this tax matters years later

The 5% paid at the bonus sets your cost basis — what the law says you paid — for those shares. Because tax was paid on their Rs 100 face value, your cost for each bonus share is Rs 100, not zero.

When you later sell them on NEPSE, capital gains tax is charged on the sale price minus Rs 100 (Chapter 35). An investor who treats bonus shares as costing zero overstates the gain on every bonus share sold.

PRACTICAL TOOL Log into Meroshare and open your portfolio and corporate-action history for each company. Check the bonus ratio declared at each AGM against the shares actually credited. If a 10% bonus was declared on 500 shares, you should see 50 new shares, not 47 or 48. Keep a simple notebook or spreadsheet: AGM date, bonus ratio, shares received, and a cost of Rs 100 per share. You will need exactly this when you sell part of a holding that has been through several bonus years.

A worked example. An investor holds 1,000 shares of a made-up company, Himalayan Hydropower Ltd (HHL). The AGM approves a 15% bonus.

Lesson 36.4 — Interest Income: Deposits, Debentures, and the Parallel Six Percent Regime

Three sources of interest

Interest runs on a track very like dividends. A NEPSE investor usually earns it from three places:

  • Bank deposits — savings accounts and fixed deposits (FDs), often used to park cash between share purchases;
  • Listed debentures — long-term loans to a bank or company, paying a fixed interest (the coupon) and traded on NEPSE;
  • Government and development bonds — usually held indirectly through mutual funds.

Bank deposits: 6%, final

Interest paid by a bank or financial institution licensed by Nepal Rastra Bank to an individual carries 6% withholding (raised from 5% by the Finance Act for FY 2080/81). For an individual who is not in the money-lending business, this is also a final withholding payment under Section 92. The bank deducts 6%, sends it to the IRD, and your tax on that interest is finished.

Debentures: the same 6%

Listed debentures follow the same pattern. The issuer or its trustee deducts 6% from each half-yearly coupon. For an individual, this is final.

Example: a bank debenture pays 11% a year. After 6% tax, you keep 11% × 0.94 = 10.34%. The coupon in the offer document is always the before-tax figure, so use 10.34% when comparing it with dividends or FDs.

REGULATORY DETAIL For years, the rate on individual interest was 5%. The Finance Act for FY 2080/81 (May 2023) raised it to 6%, over objections from banks and depositor groups that it would discourage savings. It has been 6% since, including FY 2083/84. These rates are lines in each year's Finance Act, not permanent law. Check them every year.

No small-amount exemption

Some people think only "big" deposits are taxed. There is no such exemption. The 6% applies to any interest a bank pays an individual — from a child's savings account to a crore-rupee FD.

For an investor with a high salary who parks large cash in FDs between share purchases, the 6% final tax is quite generous. Like dividends, the interest never climbs into the higher income slabs.

CAUTION Interest from informal lending — money lent privately to relatives, friends or an unregistered savings group — gets no automatic 6% treatment, because no licensed body deducts tax at source. You must report it yourself, and it is taxed at your slab rate. So a private loan at 14% may not beat a bank FD at 9% once the real tax is counted — and it carries far more risk of not being repaid.

Lesson 36.5 — Individual vs Institutional Investors: Similar Rates, Different Weight

Because 5% and 6% appear everywhere, it is tempting to think individuals and institutions are taxed the same. They are not. The same deduction can mean three very different things depending on who receives the money.

Individuals: final

For an individual, 5% on dividends and 6% on interest is the end of the matter, whatever else they earn.

Companies earning interest: only a down payment

For a resident company, the 15% withheld on interest is only an advance — like a deposit on a motorbike, with the rest paid later. The company adds the interest to its profit and pays the full company tax rate: 25% for an ordinary company, and 30% for banks, financial institutions, insurers and some capital-market firms such as merchant banks and brokers. The 15% already withheld is subtracted from the final bill.

Companies receiving dividends: excluded

Dividends paid by one resident company to another are treated more kindly. Section 53(3) of the Income Tax Act excludes them from the receiving company's taxable income.

The reason: the profit behind the dividend has already been taxed once at the paying company. Taxing it again in full at the receiving company would be like charging a toll twice on the same road. The 5% withheld is not refunded, but nothing more is added, so for the receiving company too the 5% ends up being the last tax — for a different legal reason than an individual's.

KEY CONCEPT Similar deductions, three outcomes. For an individual, the tax withheld is final (Section 92). For a company receiving dividends from another resident company, it is in effect the last tax because the dividend is excluded from income (Section 53(3)). For a company receiving interest, the 15% is only a down payment on a 25–30% tax bill. A TDS certificate tells you little until you know which of the three applies.

What this means for a Nepali household

A household that holds NEPSE shares and bank FDs in family members' own names gets final treatment on both. If the same assets sat inside a family-owned private company, dividends would face no extra tax (thanks to Section 53(3)), but interest would be taxed at 25% (or 30% for a financial institution) instead of a final 6%.

That is one clear, number-based reason Nepali investors are usually advised to hold FDs and debentures in personal names, even if a company structure is attractive for other reasons.

That is one reason such funds can offer returns that compare well with investing directly: the fund does not pay the layered tax a private company holding the same assets would face.

Lesson 36.6 — Reading Your Own Tax Trail: Records, Reconciliation, and Common Mistakes

Check what should have happened

Knowing the rates is half the job. The other half is checking your own papers. Companies paying cash dividends must issue TDS records, and the information reaches the IRD against your PAN — if your PAN is correctly linked to your demat account. Banks issue interest certificates on request, and most now show full-year interest and TDS in mobile and internet banking.

The most common problem: the wrong PAN

The usual failure is not a wrong calculation by the company or bank. It is a PAN mismatch. Perhaps you opened your demat account years ago with an old PAN, by mistake with your spouse's PAN, or with no PAN at all. Then the TDS the IRD has recorded will not appear under the PAN you use today.

Because dividend and interest tax is final, this rarely costs extra tax. But your official tax record will not show income you really earned. That can cause trouble years later — for example, when buying land and declaring where the money came from. Fix it now through your depository participant and bank.

PRACTICAL TOOL Once a year — soon after the fiscal year closes in mid-July, when most AGMs and dividends are done — put three things side by side: your Meroshare portfolio statement (dividends and bonus shares by company), your bank statement (net dividend credits), and your FD and debenture interest certificates. Check that every cash dividend equals 95% of the gross rate declared at the AGM, and every interest credit is net of 6%. If a figure does not match, ask the company's registrar or your bank. Usually the error is in your expectation of the gross rate; occasionally a small or newly listed company has made a mistake.

Do not mix this up with capital gains tax

A second mistake: assuming "dividends are taxed at 5%, final" also covers selling the shares. It does not. Tax on dividends and interest is a tax on income received while you hold an asset. Capital gains tax is a separate tax on the profit when you sell it, with its own rates, holding periods and rules (Chapter 35). They are separate events and need separate records.

CAUTION Do not treat the headline dividend rate as your cash yield. A "25 percent dividend" means Rs 25 per Rs 100 share before tax; you receive Rs 23.75. FD rates quoted in the market are also before tax, and you keep 94% of them. So when comparing a dividend yield with an FD rate, cut 5% off the dividend and 6% off the interest first.

Chapter recap

  • Companies deduct 5% from cash dividends and banks deduct 6% from interest before paying you (Income Tax Act, 2058, Section 88). Both rates are unchanged for FY 2083/84.
  • For an individual investor, both are final withholding payments under Section 92: they never enter the income tax slabs, however much else you earn.
  • The exception is dividends earned inside a share-dealing business, where the 5% is only an advance credit.
  • Bonus shares are taxed as a dividend at 5% of their Rs 100 face value (Section 53(1)). The tax is settled before the shares are credited, usually from a small cash dividend declared with the bonus — "3.80% bonus + 0.20% cash" means the cash covers the tax.
  • The tax paid on bonus shares gives them a Rs 100 cost for capital gains tax later. Keep a record for each bonus year.
  • Companies are taxed differently: interest is fully taxable at 25–30%, with the 15% withheld only a credit; dividends from another resident company are excluded from income (Section 53(3)). Approved retirement funds are generally exempt.
  • Your job is to check, not calculate: dividends should arrive at 95% of the declared rate, interest at 94%, and bonus share counts should match the declared ratio. Make sure the PAN on your demat and bank accounts is correct.
  • Dividend tax and capital gains tax are separate taxes on separate events. Keep records for both.
