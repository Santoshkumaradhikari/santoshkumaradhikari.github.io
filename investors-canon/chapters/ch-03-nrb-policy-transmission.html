NRB Policy Transmission — From Announcement to Share Price

This chapter starts with Nepal Rastra Bank's mid-July policy announcement — the most awaited event in the Nepali investment year — because so much retail trading revolves around an event that, in truth, changes almost nothing on the day itself. Every Shrawan, as the new fiscal year begins, brokers' offices in Kathmandu, Pokhara, Biratnagar and Butwal fill with investors swapping rumours about what the Governor will say on the policy rate, the cash reserve ratio and margin lending limits. Prices move on the rumour, move again on the announcement — and then, the part retail investors keep getting wrong, keep moving in fits and starts for months afterwards, as the policy actually works its way through the banks and into share prices. This chapter is about that gap: between the press conference and the price, between what NRB says and what NEPSE eventually does about it. It is like the monsoon forecast versus the actual rain: the forecast arrives in a day, but the rivers rise over weeks. Understanding the gap is the difference between an investor who panic-sells on policy day and buys back three months later at a worse price, and one who reads the process correctly and gets ahead of a move the market has not yet priced in.

In Nepal, policy reaches share prices more slowly, more unevenly and less completely than in the developed markets that Nepali finance textbooks describe. When the US Federal Reserve changes rates, US government bond prices adjust within seconds, and share index futures within the same minute, because the market is full of computer programmes constantly trading the links between policy rates, bond yields and share values. NEPSE has none of that. It has a few thousand active daily traders out of more than five million demat account holders. Its credit system runs almost entirely through banks, with only a shallow corporate bond market. And NRB governs banks mainly through written directives, not through market operations that instantly reprice tradable instruments. So the chain from policy to price has more links, more friction at each link, and more room for a policy signal to be absorbed, delayed, bent or even reversed before it reaches a company's share price. This chapter builds that chain link by link, tests it against five real Nepali episodes with dates and index levels, and gives you a framework for reading NRB policy the way a professional analyst would, rather than the way the trading-floor rumour mill does.

Lesson 3.1 — The Transmission Chain: How a Policy Announcement Becomes a Price

To understand NEPSE's reaction to NRB policy, separate two things that market chatter constantly mixes up: the announcement effect and the transmission effect. The announcement effect is the repricing on the day or in the week of the news, as people update their expectations the moment the information is public. The transmission effect is the slower, real process by which the policy changes the cost and supply of credit, which in turn changes company profits, household saving and the money available to buy shares. It is the second, slower effect that decides whether a policy change produces a lasting move in the index, or just a short mood-driven wobble that reverses within days.

In markets with deep bond markets, active derivatives and many kinds of professional investors, the announcement effect does most of the work. Sophisticated players price in the future effects immediately. On NEPSE, the announcement effect is real but shallow: moves on policy day are often mood-driven overreactions, partly reversed within one to three sessions. The transmission effect dominates over one to twelve months, as the real credit and liquidity effects work through the banks. An investor who treats the policy-day move as the whole story is trading noise; one who follows the transmission is trading the signal.

KEY CONCEPT Announcement effect is the market's immediate, sentiment-driven reaction to the headline of a policy statement. Transmission effect is the slower, structural repricing that occurs as the policy actually changes bank liquidity, credit availability, and lending rates over subsequent weeks and months. In NEPSE, the two are frequently disconnected — the initial move can go one way and the eventual, fundamentals-driven move can go the other.

In simple form, the chain runs like this. First, NRB sets or changes its tools: the policy repo rate (the rate at which it lends short-term money to banks); the cash reserve ratio, or CRR (the share of deposits banks must keep idle at NRB); the standing facility rates that form the corridor around the policy rate; and — most important for share investors — directives on how much banks may lend against shares, known in Nepal as margin lending. Second, banks and financial institutions (BFIs) build the directive into their own lending rules. That usually takes weeks, because each must revise its credit manual, get board approval and often ask NRB's regulation department for written clarification before branches can apply it. Third, the change in the cost or supply of credit flows through two channels that matter for NEPSE: the direct channel, where margin loans become easier or harder to get; and the indirect channel, where the general cost of credit affects companies' borrowing costs, spending and therefore future profits. Fourth, only once credit has actually changed on bank balance sheets does the change show up in numbers you can track: the average interbank rate, the credit-to-deposit (CD) ratio and the growth of margin loans outstanding. Fifth, and only then, does the index move in a way that reflects the real transmission rather than just a reaction to a headline.

Why the chain matters for the retail investor

Every extra link is a place where the signal can be delayed or weakened. A rate rise announced in July may not show up as tighter margin lending until October, because banks keep honouring existing credit lines until they expire and only tighten new loans. A margin relaxation announced in a quarterly review may take six to eight weeks to actually add buying power, because brokers and banks must update systems, recalculate collateral values and tell clients their new limits. This is the delay that experienced professionals use, and that retail investors trading on headlines keep misjudging — buying excitedly on the day of a good-sounding announcement, then being surprised weeks later that the index has not moved, because the actual credit has not yet reached the market.

Lesson 3.2 — The Nepali Policy Calendar and Its Rhythms

Many central banks — the US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank — meet every six to eight weeks on a fixed calendar. NRB's main tool is instead its yearly Monetary Policy, published around the start of the fiscal year in July (recent statements have come in early-to-mid July). It sets the rate corridor, the CRR and the year's priorities for banks. It is followed by quarterly reviews, usually around Mangsir, Falgun and Baisakh, in which NRB adjusts the yearly policy in the light of new data on inflation, the balance of payments and financial stability. On top of this, NRB issues standalone directives (niti-nirdeshan) through the year on specific topics — margin lending terms, loan-to-value limits for real estate, risk weights for certain assets. These are not tied to the calendar and can arrive with little warning.

This calendar shapes how NEPSE behaves. The market has learned to treat mid-July as a big event and positions itself weeks ahead, so trading and price swings rise through late Asar into early Shrawan, whatever the policy actually contains. The quarterly reviews and standalone directives get much less attention from retail investors, even though they have caused some of NEPSE's sharpest moves — especially on margin lending. They are often more important for share prices than the yearly policy itself, because they act directly on the credit that pays for share purchases.

WATCH FOR The headline July Monetary Policy sets the year's broad rate and CRR stance, but it is the quarterly reviews and standalone margin-lending directives — often issued with little fanfare in Ashwin, Magh, or mid-year — that have historically moved NEPSE more sharply, because they act directly on the credit channel that funds share purchases rather than on the general cost of money.

A second feature of the Nepali system is that NRB controls finance mainly through written directives to banks, not through market trading that instantly reprices something. When the Federal Reserve changes its target rate, the effect spreads within minutes through trading in Treasury bonds and interest rate futures, which everyone can see and act on at once. When NRB changes the margin loan-to-value ceiling, the effect travels through a circular to bank compliance departments, which update their internal manuals over days or weeks; only then does the change reach the investor at the branch or broker. There is no tradable instrument that instantly reflects the new margin rules. The information is in the circular, but the economic effect exists only once thousands of loan officers and branch managers have applied it. This paper-based rather than market-based route is the biggest single reason NEPSE reacts to NRB more slowly and in stages than markets built around rate-sensitive instruments that trade continuously.

Lesson 3.3 — Five Historical Episodes and Their Lags

Theory is only useful if it fits NEPSE's real history. This lesson works through five real episodes, each showing a different pattern: a multi-year boom and bust driven by a capital rule; a pandemic-era flood of liquidity; a margin-lending tightening before a crash; a rate-rising cycle that overpowered a simultaneous relaxation; and the 2025–26 easing cycle, showing the same mechanism in reverse.

The paid-up capital rule and the 2016 peak. In the mid-2010s, NRB told commercial banks to raise their minimum paid-up capital about fourfold, to around eight billion rupees, by mid-2017. Banks did it mostly through rights issues, bonus shares and mergers rather than fresh cash. Existing shareholders were diluted, but in the short run they felt richer as bonus shares multiplied in their demat accounts. Together with the post-earthquake rebuilding story and generally easy money, this rule fed a long rally that carried NEPSE to a then-record of about 1,881 on 27 July 2016 — roughly twelve months after the rule was first announced. The reversal was just as slow. As bonus issues and mergers added new shares faster than real demand could absorb them, the index gave back all its gains and more, falling to about 1,103 by March 2019 — a drop of about forty percent over nearly three years. It is NEPSE's clearest example of a purely administrative rule, with no direct link to interest rates or liquidity, driving a multi-year boom and bust through its effect on the supply of shares.

The pandemic liquidity flood and the 2021 peak. The FY 2020/21 Monetary Policy, issued during the COVID-19 lockdowns, was strongly supportive. Refinance facilities were expanded, interest rates were pushed to record lows, and the margin loan-to-value ratio was raised from 65 to 70 percent of a share's collateral value, directly increasing what investors could borrow against shares they already held. Add a captive retail investor base — many with less work during lockdowns and easy online trading as the TMS system spread — and the result was one of the sharpest bull runs in NEPSE's history: from around 1,200 in mid-2020 to an all-time intraday high near 3,227 on 19 August 2021 (closing peak 3,198.60 the day before). The delay from the first easing (the July 2020 policy) to the final peak was about twelve to thirteen months, and the index kept climbing well after the easiest parts of the policy had been absorbed — a sign of the momentum and herd behaviour covered in Lesson 3.6.

The margin-lending caps and the 2021–22 correction. Even while the FY 2020/21 easing was still working through, NRB's FY 2021/22 Monetary Policy moved to cap the credit channel that had fuelled the boom. It limited individual margin borrowing to Rs 4 crore (40 million) per bank or financial institution and Rs 12 crore (120 million) across all institutions combined — the "4/12" rule, as brokers called it. Together with a wider liquidity crunch, as deposit growth slowed and banks' CD ratios pressed against the ceiling, this is widely seen as the trigger for NEPSE's fall from its 2021 peak. The index fell from about 3,200 to about 1,815 over the following thirteen months. The decline unfolded gradually rather than in one sharp break, as you would expect when credit lines from before the directive expired and were not renewed on the old terms.

The 2022 tightening that overpowered a relaxation. The FY 2022/23 Monetary Policy, announced on 22 July 2022 during a balance-of-payments crisis and pressure on foreign exchange reserves, raised the policy repo rate from 5.5 to 7 percent, the CRR from 3 to 4 percent and the bank rate from 7 to 8.5 percent — one of NRB's sharpest one-year tightenings. Yet the same statement relaxed the margin ceiling, letting an individual borrow up to Rs 12 crore from one or more institutions combined instead of under the stricter earlier formula. NRB and commentators openly described this as support for the share market. In practice, NEPSE kept falling for two more months, to about 1,815 on 25 September 2022 — about 43 percent below the intraday peak. A targeted relaxation of margin lending could not offset the wider tightening from higher rates and a higher CRR raising the cost of credit across the economy. It is the clearest recent proof that investors who watch only margin announcements, and ignore the broader rate and reserve stance, are looking at one link of a longer chain.

The October 2023 margin directive. On 19 October 2023, NRB issued a detailed directive on margin lending. It capped loans at 70 percent of the lower of the 180-day average price or the last traded price; limited loans to one year; restricted re-valuing collateral to lend more; required banks to apply basic screens such as P/E ratios and dividend history before lending; and capped any one bank's margin book at 40 percent of its core capital. It was a risk-control tightening issued into an already weak market, and it shows yet another pattern. There was no sharp reaction. The directive was absorbed gradually, as banks already near their old margin limits adjusted over the following quarters, and no single big index move can be pinned to the announcement date. Not every policy change produces a visible, datable market reaction — especially when it tightens standards in a market where demand for credit is already weak.

The 2025–26 easing cycle: the mechanism in reverse. The latest cycle shows the same mechanism running backwards, and gives the clearest recent example of a measurable delay. Through 2025, NRB steadily eased the rules on share-backed lending. In May 2025 it cut the risk weight on share-backed loans from 125 to 100 percent, freeing bank capital for more margin lending. In October 2025 it scrapped the Rs 25 crore (250 million) ceiling on combined margin borrowing altogether. Its December 2025 first-quarter review cut the policy repo rate from 4.50 to 4.25 percent and the standing liquidity facility rate from 6.00 to 5.75 percent, narrowing the corridor. NEPSE, which had bottomed near 2,469 in October 2025, rallied over the following months — faster around the March 2026 change of government — to a local peak near 2,970 on 25 March 2026, then fell back. Then, on 15 July 2026, NRB published a unified directive allowing banks, at their discretion, to lend up to 80 percent of value on margin loans for qualifying companies with strong dividend and compliance records. The index had hit an eight-month low before the directive, closing at 2,576.29 on 14 July 2026. By 28 July 2026 it had recovered to about 2,697 — a rise of close to five percent within two weeks. That is one of the quickest and most clearly traceable reactions in the recent record, probably because a market that had lived through two years of on-off margin tightening was primed to react fast to signs of further easing.

CASE IN POINT Between the NRB unified directive of 15 July 2026 permitting up to 80 percent loan-to-value margin lending for qualifying companies and NEPSE's recovery from 2,576.29 on 14 July to roughly 2,697 by 28 July 2026, the observable lag was on the order of two weeks — one of the fastest documented transmission episodes in recent NEPSE history, and a useful benchmark against which to judge how quickly a given policy signal is being absorbed.

Lesson 3.4 — Why Transmission Is Slow and Partial in Nepal

Five structural features of the Nepali financial system explain the lag pattern documented above, and an investor who internalises them will read every future NRB announcement more accurately than one who does not.

Banks hold a near-monopoly on credit. In markets with deep corporate bond and commercial paper markets, a change in the policy rate reprices a wide range of tradable debt almost at once, and that flows into share valuations within the same day. Nepal has no comparably deep corporate bond market, and almost no commercial paper. Nearly all credit, including margin loans, passes through banks and financial institutions. So the main route from policy to share prices is the slow one — banks changing their own lending rules — not the fast one of a bond market repricing every minute.

REGULATORY DETAIL NRB's principal tools are the policy repo rate and the interest rate corridor formed by the Standing Deposit Facility (the rate at which banks park excess liquidity with NRB) and the Standing Liquidity Facility (the rate at which banks borrow overnight from NRB), the Cash Reserve Ratio, and administrative directives (niti-nirdeshan) issued to BFIs on specific lending categories including margin lending. Only the first set — the rate corridor and CRR — operates anything like an open market operation; margin lending rules are transmitted entirely through directive and bank-level compliance, which is why they take materially longer to show up in observable market behaviour than a corridor rate change does in the interbank market.

Rules are applied on paper, not through the market. As Lesson 3.1 explained, a circular from NRB's regulation department must be read, interpreted and put into practice by the compliance and credit teams of dozens of separate banks before it has any real economic effect. Each moves at its own pace — some update their loan books within days, others take two or three months. So one announcement turns into a scattered series of small credit changes over a long period. That is exactly why episodes like the October 2023 directive show no clean, datable index reaction, even though the policy itself was clear and specific.

Thin free float and a retail-dominated investor base. Active trading on NEPSE is concentrated in a fairly small number of shares. Promoter holdings, government holdings and long-term institutional holdings make up a large part of all listed shares and rarely trade. Trading is dominated by retail investors — widely thought to hold the great majority of active demat accounts — who trade on mood, rumour and momentum rather than on valuation models that react to the policy rate. This has two effects. First, real information about changed credit takes longer to reach prices, because the typical trader is not re-running valuations after every NRB circular. Second, when the mood does change, it changes much more sharply than the fundamentals justify, because many similarly informed traders move together. That produces the familiar pattern of an overreaction on policy day, followed by a partial reversal.

Circuit breakers and settlement rules slow full repricing. NEPSE limits how far each share's price can move in a day, and halts the whole market after big index moves. These rules are meant to stop one-day panics, but they also stop big news from being fully priced in a single session. A change in credit conditions that might justify an immediate 10 or 15 percent price change elsewhere instead plays out over several sessions, as the daily limit resets each morning. Add T+2 settlement, which slows how fast money can be recycled from one trade to the next, and the market's own plumbing adds days of delay on top of the information and behaviour delays already described.

Politics and the budget compete for attention. NRB policy rarely moves alone. It reacts to and interacts with government fiscal policy, remittance trends and balance-of-payments pressure — and, as 2025–26 showed, with changes of government that can dominate attention at the same time as a policy announcement. When a new government or a budget lands in the same window as a monetary change, retail investors and even professionals struggle to tell which one is driving a price move. That blurs the link between any single NRB action and its "true" effect on the index.

WARNING Do not assume that a monetary easing automatically means a NEPSE rally, or that a tightening automatically means a fall. The 2022 episode shows a targeted margin lending relaxation issued in the same policy statement as a sharp rate and CRR hike, and the rate hike dominated: the index kept falling for two more months and stayed weak through 2023. Always weigh the full policy package — rate corridor, CRR, and sector-specific directives together — rather than reacting to whichever headline is most flattering to your existing position.

Lesson 3.5 — Reading the Signals: A Practical Framework for Investors

Given these delays, a disciplined NEPSE investor needs a few trackable indicators that show whether an NRB action is really changing credit conditions — rather than relying on the index's first, often misleading, reaction on announcement day.

Tell the announcement date from the effective date. NRB circulars often name an effective date later than the date they are published. For directives that each bank's board must adopt, the date the change actually reaches branches can lag even that by weeks. Before deciding how "the market" reacted to a policy, check which date it is actually reacting to.

Track the weighted average interbank rate and the CD ratio, both published regularly by NRB. They show whether a rate or CRR change has really tightened or loosened liquidity across the system. Do not rely on the policy rate itself, which is a reference rate rather than the rate that actually governs banks' day-to-day behaviour.

Track margin loans outstanding, where available from NRB's financial statistics and brokers' disclosures. It is the most direct sign of whether a margin directive has actually changed the amount of borrowed buying power in the market, whatever the index has already done.

Within any single policy statement, separate the rate package from the sector directives, and score each on its own — tightening, neutral or loosening — instead of reading the policy as one signal. As the 2022 episode shows, the two can point in opposite directions in the same announcement.

Compare the delay you observe with the historical range in Table 3.1: from about two weeks at the fast end (the July 2026 margin directive) to more than a year at the slow end (the 2015–16 capital rule and its unwinding). Do not assume one fixed delay fits every kind of policy. Margin directives acting on a market that is already primed and borrowed-up tend to work faster than broad rate and reserve changes acting through the whole economy.

PRACTICAL TOOL Before trading on any NRB announcement, run this five-point check: (1) What is the effective date, not the publication date? (2) Does the statement contain both a rate-corridor component and a sector-directive component, and do they point the same direction? (3) Has the interbank rate or CD ratio actually moved yet, or only the policy rate on paper? (4) What was the comparable historical lag for this type of directive in Table 3.1? (5) Is a political or fiscal event occurring in the same window that could be confounding the read? An announcement that fails several of these checks is one where the announcement-day price move is more likely noise than signal.

Lesson 3.6 — Behavioural Traps Around Policy Events

The final and perhaps most consequential lesson of this chapter is behavioural rather than mechanical. Retail investors in NEPSE consistently make three related errors around policy events, and each is a direct consequence of misunderstanding the transmission lag documented above rather than of any failure to read the policy correctly on its own terms.

The first mistake is treating the policy-day price move as the final verdict. Because the transmission effect usually dominates over months, not days, buying or selling purely on the announcement reaction means trading the least informative part of the process — a burst of mood among retail traders who, by definition, have not yet seen any real change in credit. The better trade often comes weeks later, once the interbank rate, the CD ratio or margin loan data start to move. By then the market has usually adjusted only partly, and real mispricing can still be found.

The second mistake is borrowing heavily in anticipation of a margin relaxation that has been rumoured or announced but not yet put into practice by your bank. The gap between an NRB directive and its availability at the broker's counter can be several weeks. Some investors take on margin debt expecting a relaxation that has not yet arrived. Others assume a discretionary rule — such as the July 2026 directive's 80 percent loan-to-value ceiling — applies to everyone, immediately. They often find that their bank has not yet updated its policy, or that their shares do not meet the "strong dividend and compliance history" test the directive actually sets. They are left over-committed against a policy that has not yet delivered.

CAUTION A discretionary or conditional NRB provision — such as the 2026 unified directive's 80 percent loan-to-value ceiling for "qualifying" companies — is not a blanket entitlement. Eligibility criteria, individual bank implementation timelines, and each institution's own risk appetite all sit between the circular and your actual available margin. Confirm your specific eligibility and your specific bank's implementation status before increasing leverage on the assumption that a policy applies to you.

The third mistake is panic-selling on a tightening announcement without checking whether it is offset elsewhere in the same package, or whether it can realistically take effect quickly given the frictions described in Lesson 3.4. The 2022 episode is useful here too, from the other side. Investors who panic-sold just on the CRR and repo rate rise, without noticing that the same policy eased margin limits, happened to be right: the tightening, not the easing, decided the market's direction over the next year. But getting it right required weighing both parts, not reacting to either headline alone. What this chapter asks for is neither blind optimism about every easing nor automatic panic at every tightening. It is a habit: split every NRB announcement into its rate part and its sector-directive part; judge which is likely to dominate given the economic and political backdrop; and size your positions for the multi-month delay that NEPSE's history actually shows, not for the often misleading first-day move.

Chapter recap

NRB policy reaches NEPSE through a longer chain with more friction than in markets built on continuously traded, rate-sensitive instruments. That is because Nepal's credit runs almost entirely through banks, margin rules are applied through written directives rather than market operations, and trading is retail-heavy and mood-driven rather than kept in line by professional investors.

The episodes in this chapter show delays from about two weeks, for the July 2026 margin liberalisation, to well over a year, for the 2015–16 capital rule and the 2021–22 boom and bust. No single fixed delay can be assumed; compare each type of policy with its own history.

A single NRB statement often contains both a rate part (the repo rate, CRR and standing facilities) and a sector part governing margin lending, and the two can point in opposite directions. The July 2022 policy combined a sharp rate tightening with an explicit easing of margin limits — and the tightening decided the market's direction for the next year.

Track effective dates, not announcement dates. Watch the weighted average interbank rate, the CD ratio and margin loans outstanding as early signs of real transmission. Treat policy-day index moves as noisy and often reversible, not as a verdict.

Discretionary or conditional rules in NRB directives, such as higher loan-to-value limits for eligible companies, are not automatic rights. Before borrowing more, check that your shares qualify and when your own bank will actually apply the change.

The disciplined response to any NRB announcement is to break it into its parts, judge which part will dominate given the economic and political backdrop, and size positions for the months over which Nepali monetary policy has historically played out — not to trade the first-day headline in either direction.
