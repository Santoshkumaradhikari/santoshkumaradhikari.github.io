Lesson 44.1 — The NEPSE Hydropower Universe: One Sector, Several Different Businesses

From builder to buyer

The last two chapters showed how a hydropower project is financed and how its cash is modelled from scratch: sizing the loan, the tariffs, the licence period, and how much everything depends on the water in one river in one year.

This chapter turns that knowledge outward. You are no longer a lender or promoter building a model. You are an investor in Kathmandu, Biratnagar or anywhere with a NEPSE trading account. Your broker's screen shows nearly a hundred hydropower tickers. You need a repeatable way to decide which, if any, deserve your money.

Four businesses under one label

A fact that surprises many new investors: "hydropower stock" is not one kind of investment. The label covers at least four different business models. Mixing them up is the most common analysis mistake in this part of NEPSE.

  1. The single-asset operator. The company owns exactly one power plant, on one river, with one Power Purchase Agreement (PPA — the long-term contract under which it sells its electricity, almost always to the Nepal Electricity Authority, NEA). Upper Tamakoshi Hydropower is the textbook case: one 456-megawatt (MW) peaking run-of-river plant on the Tamakoshi River in Dolakha, and nothing else. Buying it is a concentrated bet on one river, one tunnel, one powerhouse and one buyer. A bad year on that river has nowhere to hide.
  2. The diversified holding company. Chilime Hydropower began in 2003 running its own 22.1 MW plant on the Chilime River. Over two decades it has taken stakes in, and consolidated, other plants — including Rasuwagadhi (111 MW) and the Sanjen cascade projects. Buying Chilime today is like buying a small basket of rivers. Butwal Power Company (BPC) is similar: it runs older plants such as Andhikhola (9.4 MW after its 2014 upgrade) and Jhimruk (12 MW), and holds stakes in other hydropower SPVs (special purpose companies set up for one project). A drought or landslide on one stream does not wipe out the whole company's output.
  3. The small single-project IPP. An independent power producer (IPP) like Ridi Hydropower or Sanima Mai Hydropower, usually under 25 MW, built and listed by a promoter group mainly to raise public money for that one project. There are dozens of these on NEPSE. Most of the sector's retail trading happens here, because they are small, jumpy and easy to speculate on.
  4. The pipeline or growth company. It is still building its plant, or has just switched it on and has not yet had a stable full year. You are not really analysing a running business yet. You are analysing a construction project with a ticker attached. The risks are closer to the project-finance material in Chapter 42 than to a normal listed share.
KEY CONCEPT A Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) is the contract, almost always with the NEA, that fixes how much a hydropower company is paid per unit of electricity (per kWh) and for how many years. Everything else in this chapter — royalty step-ups, dividend capacity, valuation — is one question in different clothes: what does the cash under this particular PPA look like, year by year, until it runs out?

First question: which model is this?

Before you open a single annual report, ask which of the four models you are looking at. Each is judged differently:

  • Single-asset operator: judge it almost entirely on one river and one PPA.
  • Diversified holding company: judge it like a small group of businesses — add up the parts, then add a discount or premium for how well the parent shares money between its subsidiaries.
  • Small IPP: judge it on the same basics as the single-asset operator, but with much less room for error. It has no other asset to fall back on and often carries higher fixed costs per megawatt.
  • Pipeline company: judge it first on construction risk, and only second as a running business.

Lesson 44.2 — Run-of-River versus Storage/Peaking: Reading the Risk Profile Correctly

Chapter 43 explained the engineering difference between run-of-river (ROR) and storage or peaking designs. This lesson turns that into an investor's view of risk, because it is the biggest single reason a hydropower company's quarterly revenue looks "lumpy".

Pure run-of-river: full in monsoon, thin in winter

A pure run-of-river plant has no real reservoir. It generates roughly in step with whatever water is in the river at that moment. In Nepal, the monsoon decides that: about 80% of the year's rain falls between June and September.

A pure ROR plant on a river without much glacier or spring flow runs at or near full capacity in the monsoon. In the dry months, December to April, it can fall to a fraction of that — sometimes 20–30% of installed capacity. That is not a fault; it is the plant working as designed.

But it means a pure ROR company's quarterly revenue swings hard between wet and dry quarters. Compare one quarter's earnings per share (EPS) with another without adjusting for season, and you will draw the wrong conclusion every time.

Peaking run-of-river: smoother days, same seasons

A peaking run-of-river (PROR) plant — Upper Tamakoshi again — adds a small pondage: a short-term storage basin holding a few hours of water. This lets it bunch its generation into the hours when electricity is worth most, usually the morning and evening peaks, when NEA demand (and, under time-of-day tariffs, the price it pays) is highest.

Think of a tea shop that keeps a big flask of tea ready for the morning rush instead of brewing cup by cup. That smooths daily revenue and can lift the average price above the plain energy rate. But it does nothing for the wet-versus-dry season swing. The total water in the year is still the limit.

True storage: the all-weather asset

A true storage project has a dam and reservoir big enough to hold weeks or months of inflow. It is the rarest type among NEPSE companies. Storage is far more expensive to build per megawatt, and Nepal's private hydropower boom has been almost entirely run-of-river.

If a company does own one, it is the closest thing in the sector to an all-weather asset. It can release water in the dry season, when tariffs (and the NEA's need for steady power) are highest, turning a weakness into a strength. Treat it as clearly better quality than a ROR plant of the same size, and expect the market to price it higher — but check that the premium is not bigger than the real cash advantage.

WARNING Never compare two hydropower companies' quarterly EPS directly without first checking that both quarters fall in the same season. A Poush (mid-winter) quarter set against a Shrawan–Ashwin (peak-monsoon) quarter will make almost any ROR company look like it is collapsing or booming, when nothing has changed except the calendar.

What to check

Ask: how much of this company's installed capacity is pure ROR, how much peaking ROR, how much storage? And how does its river's dry-season flow compare with its wet-season flow?

Companies publish monthly or quarterly generation in their annual reports (Lesson 44.4). Plotting a few years of these numbers is the fastest way to see a plant's real seasonal pattern, rather than trusting a one-word "run-of-river" label on a broker's factsheet.

Lesson 44.3 — The PPA Clock and the Royalty Staircase

Every hydropower company's cash has two clocks ticking inside it. Both matter a lot to a long-term investor. Neither appears as one line on the income statement.

Clock 1: the PPA term

A PPA is not a permanent right to sell power. It is a fixed-term contract, usually 25 to 35 years from the commercial operation date (COD). After that, the company must agree new terms with the NEA or, in principle, sell power under whatever market arrangement exists then. It is like a shop on a long lease: what matters is not only the rent today but how many years are left before the landlord can change the deal.

For a young plant like Upper Tamakoshi (commissioned 2021), decades remain, so this is a minor point today. But several older private plants on NEPSE date from the mid-1990s and early 2000s, including some of BPC's and Chilime's founding plants. For these, check how many years are left. A plant near PPA expiry faces real uncertainty about its future tariff.

The annual report's project description should show this. Write it down for every hydropower holding: "PPA signed in [year], term [n] years, so it expires in [year]."

Clock 2: the royalty staircase

The second clock — far more often missed — is the royalty staircase. Under Nepal's Electricity Act and its rules, generators pay royalty to the Government of Nepal in two parts:

  • a capacity royalty — a fixed yearly charge per kilowatt of installed capacity; and
  • an energy royalty — a percentage of the value of energy actually generated and sold.

Both step up partway through a plant's life. Commonly, after about fifteen years of operation, the rates rise a lot — in some structures several times over on the capacity part and by several percentage points on the energy part.

The logic is simple. Young plants are still repaying heavy loans, so the state takes a small share early. Once the debt is mostly repaid and free cash is higher, it takes a larger share.

REGULATORY DETAIL Nepal's hydropower royalty is a capacity royalty (per kW of installed capacity) plus an energy royalty (a percentage of energy sold), under the Electricity Act and its rules, with both rates rising once a plant passes roughly fifteen years of commercial operation. The exact rupee and percentage figures are revised from time to time by the Ministry of Energy, Water Resources and Irrigation. Always check the current schedule and each company's own reported royalty expense; do not assume the rate is the same as when the PPA was signed.

Why this matters for picking stocks

A plant's margin after royalty is not constant. It shrinks as the plant ages, even if generation and tariff revenue stay flat.

An investor who values an older plant by simply carrying last year's net margin forward is quietly assuming royalty never rises. For a plant near or past year fifteen, that is plainly wrong. And a newer plant still inside the low-royalty years has a margin cushion that will wear away on a known timetable. That is a predictable headwind. Build it into any multi-year projection instead of treating it as a surprise.

CASE IN POINT Some of Nepal's oldest listed private and semi-public hydropower plants — commissioned in the 1990s and early 2000s — have already moved into the higher royalty band. Their royalty expense as a share of revenue is visibly higher than that of plants commissioned in the last five to ten years. Comparing two companies' royalty-to-revenue ratios side by side is a quick way, using only annual reports, to estimate where each plant stands on its own staircase, before you read any PPA document.

So the checklist item is twofold: (1) how many years are left on this PPA, and (2) has this plant already crossed the royalty step-up, or when will it? Write both numbers in your notes before looking at a single valuation multiple.

Lesson 44.4 — Reading the Annual Report: Generation Data, Realised Tariff, and the Monsoon Story

A Nepali hydropower annual report holds more useful information outside the financial statements than inside them. Most retail investors never turn to those pages. This lesson shows which ones matter.

The generation and sales table

Start here. A well-run company shows, usually near the directors' report, the units (kWh or GWh) generated and sold in the year, often by month or at least by quarter. It is the most important table in the report, because it answers three questions no ratio can answer alone.

Question 1: was a fall the plant's fault or the river's?

Compare this year's generation with earlier years. If it fell, was the problem the plant — a landslide-damaged intake, a transformer failure, a planned overhaul — or the whole river, such as a weak monsoon?

Parts of Nepal had a clearly weaker monsoon in 2023, and several ROR-heavy companies reported noticeably lower generation and revenue that year purely from lower river flow. Hydrology risk is not just a line in a textbook; it shows up in real reported numbers every few years.

Telling the two apart matters:

  • a bad monsoon is a recurring, cyclical risk — average it over several years;
  • equipment or landslide damage is a one-off — unless it keeps happening, in which case it says something worrying about maintenance or the site's geology.

Question 2: what is the plant load factor?

The plant load factor is actual generation divided by the most the plant could produce running at full capacity every hour of the year. For a pure ROR plant with weak dry-season flow, 40–55% is often normal and not a warning sign by itself. What you want to watch is whether it is stable, rising or falling year after year for reasons other than rainfall.

Question 3: what tariff did it really earn?

This is the step most investors skip. Work out the realised average tariff: total energy revenue ÷ total units sold. Then compare it with the PPA's base tariffs — usually set separately for wet and dry seasons, with the dry rate clearly higher to compensate ROR plants for their weakest months.

If the realised tariff has been rising faster than the PPA's agreed escalation would explain, find out why. Is the company genuinely producing more of its energy in the higher-priced dry season (a lasting change, perhaps from a pondage upgrade)? Or was it a one-off adjustment or an NEA arrears settlement that will not happen again?

PRACTICAL TOOL Keep a simple five-column tracking sheet for every hydropower holding: (1) units sold this year vs. last year, (2) realised average tariff vs. PPA base tariff, (3) royalty expense as % of revenue, (4) dividend per share vs. free cash flow per share, (5) years left on the PPA. Update it once a year straight from the annual report, and most of the judgments in this chapter become visible at a glance.

Where royalty and tax are hiding

Finally, look at how royalty and tax are shown. Royalty is sometimes buried inside "other operating expenses" instead of having its own line. If so, check the notes to the accounts or ask the company (many hold investor meetings or answer written questions). A hidden royalty step-up is exactly what quietly eats margin without showing up in a quick ratio scan.

Income tax for Nepali hydropower companies often has a lower rate or a tax holiday for some years after COD, to encourage investment. That too changes — usually upward — partway through a plant's life. Model it explicitly; do not assume it stays the same.

Lesson 44.5 — Promoters, Construction Risk, and the Balance Sheet: What Can Go Wrong Between the Prospectus and the Dividend Cheque

A hydropower share is, in the end, a claim on a stream of dividends. The road from "the plant makes electricity" to "you receive a dividend" is longer and has more toll gates than in most NEPSE sectors. This lesson walks that road.

Start with the promoter

Nepal's hydropower boom has been driven largely by a fairly small number of promoter groups and engineering-construction houses who have built and listed several projects over two decades. A promoter's record on earlier projects is a useful guide:

  • Did their earlier plants finish near budget and near the promised date — or years late and far over budget, as many in this sector have, given landslide-prone hills, seasonal roads and long waits for imported equipment?
  • Did dividends start within a reasonable time after COD, or did the company sit on cash for years, citing DSRA requirements (explained below)?

A promoter who has taken a project from construction to a steady dividend before is a far better signal than the glossy words of a new prospectus.

Pipeline companies: think like a lender

For a company still building, the risks are close to what Chapter 42 described for project lenders, not what a normal share analyst expects:

  • cost overruns from geological surprises — tunnel collapses and unstable slopes are a recurring theme in Himalayan construction;
  • delays that push back COD, and so push back the first dividend by the same number of years;
  • foreign-exchange risk on imported turbines, generators and transmission equipment, if the company has not hedged or matched its loan currency to its revenue currency.

A holder of a pre-COD hydropower share should read construction-progress updates (percentage complete, revised COD) the way a lender reads a drawdown schedule. Treat any COD promise with healthy doubt, given the sector's own history of delays.

WARNING A pipeline hydropower share rising sharply on IPO excitement, well before COD, is pricing in that construction finishes on time and on budget. Given the sector's real record of delays and overruns, that assumption should be actively questioned, not accepted just because the price is going up.

The debt structure

Once a plant runs, the next layer is its debt. Nepali hydropower projects borrow long-term project loans, usually from a group of Nepali commercial banks (because of capital controls and the small domestic bond market). That debt often brings refinancing or restructuring risk at some point — because the loan was shorter than the PPA, or because covenants need renegotiating from time to time.

Check two things in the annual report or notes:

  1. the debt maturity profile — how much falls due in the next one, three and five years;
  2. whether the company has shown it can refinance on reasonable terms, or is at the mercy of whatever bank rates apply when a big payment falls due. In Nepal, bank lending rates have swung widely across credit cycles over the past decade, so this is a real risk.

Why a profitable company may pay a small dividend

This brings us to the DSRA and covenants — the most common reason a profitable-looking hydropower company still pays a disappointing dividend.

  • Lenders almost always require a Debt Service Reserve Account (DSRA): cash set aside, often equal to the next one or two quarters of principal and interest, before any dividend can be paid.
  • Loan covenants often require a minimum Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) — cash available for debt divided by debt due — sometimes with an extra "lock-up" test, before the company may pay dividends at all.

Think of a family that has a good salary on paper, but whose bank insists that three months of loan instalments sit in a locked account before they can spend on a wedding. A company can report solid net profit and still be barred from paying much dividend that year, because its DSRA is being topped up or its DSCR is close to the minimum.

CAUTION Never treat reported net profit as a reliable guide to what a hydropower company can actually pay out. Check the cash flow statement, the DSRA balance (often in the notes), and any covenant language in the directors' report. A company can be profitable on paper and still legally barred from paying the dividend its EPS would suggest.

The checklist

Put together, the promoter-and-balance-sheet checklist for a NEPSE hydropower share is:

  • Has this promoter delivered before?
  • For pipeline names: how much construction is complete, and how has COD guidance drifted?
  • What does the debt maturity ladder look like over the next five years?
  • Is any DSRA or covenant limit currently restricting cash for dividends?

None of these shows up as a single ratio on a stock screener. You have to read the report.

Lesson 44.6 — Valuing a Hydropower Stock: DCF Over the PPA Life, and Avoiding the P/E Trap

Chapters 42 and 43 built the tools for a full discounted cash flow (DCF) model of a hydropower project — valuing a business by estimating its future cash and converting it to today's money. This lesson uses a simpler version as a share investor, and flags the valuation traps this sector produces more reliably than almost any other on NEPSE.

A business with an end date

A hydropower company's main asset has a known, limited life in a way most businesses do not. The PPA has a fixed end date. After it, future cash is truly uncertain: a new PPA might come at a different tariff, or the plant might sell into whatever market exists then.

That makes hydropower one of the cleanest NEPSE sectors for a proper multi-stage DCF:

  1. Project yearly free cash flow to equity (cash left for shareholders) for the remaining PPA term.
  2. Model, year by year, the seasonality (Lesson 44.2), the royalty step-up (Lesson 44.3), the end of the tax holiday, and loan repayment and refinancing.
  3. Discount that cash at a cost of equity based on Nepal's risk-free rate (such as the yield on government bonds) plus a suitable equity risk premium.
  4. Add a deliberately cautious — or even zero — terminal value (the value placed on everything after the forecast) beyond PPA expiry, because what happens after that date cannot be known today.

This is quite different from valuing a bank or a manufacturer, where a growing "forever" terminal value usually does most of the work. In hydropower, the terminal value should do very little of the work; the years up to PPA expiry should do almost all of it.

KEY CONCEPT Because a PPA has a fixed end date, a hydropower DCF should rest mainly on the forecast years up to PPA expiry, with a small or zero terminal value after that — the opposite of most DCFs for ongoing businesses with no natural end date.

The P/E trap, hydropower version

The most dangerous shortcut is a simple trailing price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio — share price divided by last year's earnings per share. This sector produces a particularly sharp version of the "P/E trap" from earlier chapters.

  • Looks cheap, isn't. A company can have an unusually strong year — a very wet monsoon, a full year with no breakdowns, an NEA arrears settlement lifting the realised tariff. It then shows a low P/E on the screener, looking "cheap" only because that one year's EPS was inflated by conditions that will not repeat.
  • Looks expensive, isn't. A company coming off a real drought or a maintenance shutdown can show a high P/E that makes it look expensive — just when the asset is actually at its cheapest point in the cycle.

It is like judging a Terai rice farm's worth from one harvest. A bumper year makes the land look cheap; a flood year makes it look dear. The fix is the same for both: average earnings over at least three to five years, covering both wet and dry years, before working out any P/E-style multiple.

WARNING One year's EPS for a run-of-river hydropower company is a noisy, weather-driven number, not a stable measure of earning power. A trailing P/E built on one such year, without averaging over several, will misprice the share in either direction depending on whether that year was wet or dry.

EV/EBITDA: a fairer comparison

EV/EBITDA is usually a better tool for comparing hydropower companies than P/E. EV (enterprise value) is the value of the whole business — shares plus debt, minus cash. EBITDA is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation.

It removes two distortions:

  • different borrowing levels — some hydropower companies carry far more debt than others, which skews P/E through the interest line;
  • different tax-holiday positions, discussed in Lesson 44.4.

Comparing EV/EBITDA across companies of similar age, similar remaining PPA term and a similar ROR/storage mix is a sensible way to see which ones the market prices more or less generously. But "similar remaining PPA term" matters a great deal. A plant three years from PPA expiry and one twenty-five years away should never trade at the same EV/EBITDA, all else equal, because the first buyer is getting a much shorter stream of cash.

Dividend yield: check it is sustainable

Dividend yield is the measure most retail investors reach for first, since hydropower shares are widely (and often rightly) seen as income shares. But Lesson 44.5 showed why one year's dividend can mislead. DSRA top-ups and covenant lock-ups can hold a dividend down in a year when the underlying cash was fine. And a company can sometimes pay an unsustainably large dividend by running down reserves ahead of a known heavy spending or repayment year.

To check it is sustainable:

  • compare the multi-year average payout with free cash flow to equity — not net profit;
  • watch whether the DSRA balance and DSCR headroom are stable or shrinking;
  • ask whether any expansion project is likely to pull cash away from dividends soon, however strong this year's generation looks.

Chapter recap

  • "Hydropower stock" covers four businesses: the single-asset operator (Upper Tamakoshi), the diversified holding company (Chilime, BPC), the small single-project IPP, and the pipeline company. Identify which one first; each is judged differently.
  • Pure run-of-river output swings hard between monsoon and dry season; peaking plants smooth the day, not the season; true storage is the rare all-weather asset. Never compare quarterly EPS across seasons.
  • Two clocks run inside every company: the PPA term (usually 25–35 years from COD) and the royalty staircase (rates step up after about 15 years). Write both down for each holding.
  • In the annual report, read the generation table: plant problem or river problem, plant load factor, and realised tariff against the PPA. Find where royalty and tax are shown.
  • Between prospectus and dividend: check the promoter's record, construction progress, the debt maturity ladder, and any DSRA or DSCR limit. Profit on paper is not cash for dividends.
  • Value with a DCF to PPA expiry and little or no terminal value. Avoid the one-year P/E trap by averaging 3–5 years; compare with EV/EBITDA among peers of similar PPA term; test dividend yield against free cash flow.
