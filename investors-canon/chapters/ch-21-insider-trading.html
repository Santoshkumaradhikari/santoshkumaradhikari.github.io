WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Insider information — price-sensitive news that the company has not yet published, such as a bonus decision still inside the boardroom. Information asymmetry — one side knowing more than the other, like a trader who hears before the sabzi mandi opens that the tomato trucks are stuck at the border. Disgorgement — being made to hand back the profit from a wrongful trade. Market surveillance — software and staff that watch trading for suspicious patterns. Thin float — few shares actually available to trade, so small orders move the price a lot.

Every stock market rests on a shared belief: that the price on the screen reflects what can be known about a company right now. NEPSE asks you to believe this more than most markets do. Much of what moves prices here — a board's bonus decision, a rights issue not yet filed, a strong unaudited quarter, a merger still being discussed in a boardroom in Kathmandu or Biratnagar — starts as a private fact known to a few people. Only later does it become public. The gap between those two moments, however short, is where insider trading lives.

This chapter treats insider trading not as a scandal to complain about but as a feature of the market to understand and defend against. You cannot remove the information gap as one investor; nobody can. But you can understand how it works in Nepal — in law, in institutions and in behaviour — so that you size positions sensibly, read strange price moves correctly, and resist the "someone knows something" stories behind some of NEPSE's wildest rallies and crashes.

Lesson 21.1 — What Insider Trading Is, and Why Thin Markets Suffer It Most

Insider trading means buying or selling a share using important news that the public does not have yet, obtained through a position of trust — as a director, employee, auditor, banker or anyone close enough to learn things early. The harm is not that someone makes a profit; markets are meant to reward good judgement. The harm is that the profit comes from privileged access, not from skill or risk open to everyone. Money moves quietly from outsiders to insiders, trade by trade, and the outsiders never know.

LEGAL DEFINITION Under Nepal's Securities Act, 2063 (2007), Section 91, insider trading occurs when a person deals in securities — or causes another person to deal — on the basis of insider information that has not been published, or passes such information on in a way likely to affect the price. The Act's explanation defines "insider information or notice" as specific information not yet published by the company that could affect the share price if it were disclosed. Section 92 then deems three groups to have access to such information: (a) the company's directors and employees, and shareholders who can obtain it; (b) people who obtain it as professional service providers to the company — in practice auditors, lawyers and advisers; and (c) anyone who obtains it through direct or indirect contact with either group, which can include relatives, friends and brokers. Section 93 lists when information counts as public, for example once it is published under the stock exchange's rules. Section 101(1) sets the punishment: a fine equal to the amount in controversy, or up to one year in prison, or both.

Why does this hurt more on NEPSE than on a big market such as India's? Three reasons.

  • Mostly small investors. In big markets, funds and professional investors study public news all day, so an insider's edge disappears quickly once news is out. On NEPSE much of the daily trading is small investors reacting to price and rumour. An informed trade moves the price further, faster and for longer.
  • Thin float. Many companies, especially newer banks, insurers, hydropower and microfinance companies, have promoter shares locked for years (commonly three years after the IPO under the Securities Registration and Issue Regulation, 2073). Only a small slice of shares actually trades. A little informed buying or selling moves such a share much more than it would move a large, busy one.
  • News arrives in bursts. Important facts become public through board notices, AGM decisions and exchange circulars — occasional bursts, not a steady stream. The time between a board decision and its public notice is exactly when people in the room, and the people they tell, can act.
WHY IT MATTERS On a thin market, insider trading decides who loses when a rumour-driven move reverses. The small investor who buys because "the price is moving, something must be happening" is very often the last buyer — the one an insider, or someone they tipped, is selling to.

Lesson 21.2 — Nepal's Legal Framework and Its Practical Limits

Nepal does have laws against insider trading. The Securities Act, 2063 is the main one (Sections 91 to 93 define it; Section 101 punishes it). SEBON, set up in 1993 and now working under this Act, investigates breaches, and its regulations add disclosure and conduct duties for companies and brokers.

For years the practical bite was weak. The maximum prison term is one year — small compared with the gain from one well-timed trade in a thinly traded share. Recognising this, the government registered the Securities (First Amendment) Bill, 2081 in Parliament in August 2024. It proposed much heavier penalties: fines reportedly up to Rs 30 million (Rs 3 crore) and prison of up to three years, linked to the size of the trade, plus repayment of the gains (disgorgement — being made to hand back the profit). It also proposed letting SEBON ask NRB for bank records during an investigation. That matters, because the hard part of an insider case is tracing the money and the relationships, not spotting the trade.

REGULATORY STATUS Status at this check (25 November 2026): this book could not confirm that the bill became law. The House of Representatives was dissolved in September 2025, and about 30 bills still before it lapsed; this book has not found a published list showing whether this bill was among them or had passed before then. Until an amended Act appears in the Nepal Gazette, treat the original Securities Act, 2063 penalties (Section 101: a fine equal to the amount in controversy, up to one year in prison, or both) as the law in force. To check, look up the Securities Act on the Nepal Law Commission website and see whether an amendment dated after 2024 is listed.

Even with tougher penalties, enforcement depends on catching people. Four things have held SEBON back.

  • Surveillance is still being built. SEBON plans an AI-based market surveillance system as a priority for fiscal year 2083/84 (2026/27). That it is a plan, not an existing tool, tells you that automatic alerts on suspicious pre-announcement trading have not been a mature part of oversight. Most detection has come from looking back at patterns, complaints, news reports and referrals.
  • Small staff, big job. SEBON oversees hundreds of listed companies, many brokers and millions of demat accounts. Proving one case means rebuilding trading records, broker orders, bank transfers and family links.
  • Conflicts close to the regulator. In 2021 the chairman of SEBON and the chief executive of NEPSE were both alleged to hold shares of Sarbottam Cement obtained through relatives before its IPO; the NEPSE chief executive resigned and the SEBON chairman was suspended (Sharesansar, 2 September 2021). In a small market, the people meant to catch insider trading are not always cleanly separated from those who could commit it. The 2024 bill also proposed keeping conflicted representatives off SEBON's board and a two-year cooling-off period before former SEBON officials could work for listed companies.
  • Cases happen, but one at a time. In March 2023 the Kathmandu District Government Attorney's Office charged Guru Prasad Neupane, chairman of Ridi Power Company, with insider trading on a complaint from SEBON, seeking a fine of Rs 32.3 million (the gain alleged) and jail (Himal Press, 23 March 2023). In April 2023 SEBON fined Gyanendra Lal Pradhan, former executive chairman of Nepal Hydro Developer, Rs 75,000 and barred him from being a public-company director for ten years over sales of shares held in family members' names (Sharesansar, 4 April 2023). In December 2024 SEBON opened a probe into possible insider trading in the shares of the troubled Karnali Development Bank. These show a regulator able to act on clear, high-profile cases — but without the constant surveillance that would catch the many smaller ones.
PRACTICAL TAKEAWAY Do not think, "Insider trading is illegal, so it must be rare." Think, "It is illegal, detection is improving but still developing, and enforcement so far has come case by case." Assume an information gap is always in the background.

Lesson 21.3 — The Mechanics of Information Asymmetry Specific to Nepal

Information asymmetry means one side knows more than the other — like a trader who hears before the sabzi mandi opens that the tomato trucks are stuck at the border. On NEPSE, the extra knowledge travels along three main roads.

Road 1: promoters and the board

Boards of banks, insurers, hydropower and microfinance companies are usually dominated by promoters. They sit in the room where results, dividends, bonus shares, rights plans, mergers and letters from regulators are known long before they are published. The point is not the promoter shares themselves; it is that promoters and the directors they nominate see unaudited numbers, board minutes and regulator letters before anyone outside the room.

Road 2: brokers

Brokers see orders as they come in, including which large accounts are building up or selling a share, often before any news explains why. A broker's desk can notice persistent buying from an account linked to an insider. Section 92's third group — anyone in direct or indirect contact with insiders — can cover people who learn unpublished news this way, so brokers are a possible channel for leaks, not just order-takers.

Road 3: tips and social media

This is the road ordinary investors see, because it runs in public even though the information is not properly public. NEPSE itself has warned investors not to trade on claims spreading through Facebook, TikTok, X, Viber and Telegram. The usual pattern: a rumour of a bonus share, a rights issue or a favourable decision starts in a Facebook group or Viber community, sometimes with a fake or cropped "screenshot" of an internal notice. Small investors pile in, and the price jumps — market commentary has described bonus-rumour moves of 15 to 25 percent within days — before any official word. If the news does not come, or comes smaller, the price falls back fast, often in one panicky session.

Be precise. Not every correct rumour is insider trading. Someone who works out that a bonus is likely from a company's profits and past record is doing honest analysis. But many rumours trace back, one or two steps, to someone with a real inside link — a staff member, a bank employee handling a restructured loan, the printer preparing an AGM notice, a broker's desk. By the time a rumour reaches a Viber group with thousands of members, nobody can trace it and its details are often garbled, but it can still move a thinly traded share a long way.

MARKET REALITY The problem is not only "Is this rumour true?" Even a true rumour, acted on late, puts you as the last buyer at the top of a move that insiders started and social media amplified.

Lesson 21.4 — Recognising the Signs of Informed Trading

You cannot see order books, bank records or board minutes. You can see market patterns that often go with informed trading. No single sign is proof; use them to raise or lower your suspicion, not to switch it on or off.

Warning signWhat it may mean
Sharp volume jump with no public newsNews may be spreading privately before the official notice
Steady, unexplained price drift for several days before a board meeting or AGMPeople who know the likely outcome are positioning early
A sharp move, followed soon by a company notice (sometimes a denial)Strong sign that part of the market knew first
Most buying or selling coming through one or two broker codes, far above their usual sharePossible informed or coordinated trading through one channel
A rumour with oddly exact detail (an exact bonus or rights ratio, a specific approval date) before any noticeMore likely to come from a real inside source
Gains quickly given back when the rumoured news fails to appearThe move was rumour positioning, not a real change in value

Each sign can have an innocent cause: index rebalancing, a chart breakout attracting momentum traders, or plain chance in a share that trades so thinly that a few large honest orders look like a spike. What matters is clustering. When several signs appear in the same share at the same time, the chance that you are watching informed trading rises — and your own behaviour should change.

Lesson 21.5 — Defensive Strategies for the Retail Investor

You cannot out-know an insider. So do not play their game — trading on rumours and expected news. Step out of it and compete on patience, discipline and published facts.

  • Trade only on published information. Board notices, AGM decisions and NEPSE or SEBON circulars are your information. Treat social-media "leaks", however confident, as noise. This one rule removes the most damaging pattern in this chapter: buying into a rumour spike and holding the bag when it falls.
  • Size positions for the information risk. A share showing the warning signs in Lesson 21.4 — thin float, dominant promoter, a pending board decision, unexplained volume — deserves a smaller position than the same expected return would justify in a clearer, busier share. You are not just facing price swings; you may be trading against someone who knows more. Position size is the one lever you fully control.
  • Do not chase moves with no confirmed cause. A price move with no public reason tells you about other people's bets, not about the company. Waiting for confirmation costs you the first part of a real move. It saves you from the much bigger loss of buying the top of a rumour that fails.
  • Use the calendar. Much price-sensitive news arrives on a schedule — AGM season, quarterly results, lock-in expiries. Mark these dates. Around them, reduce positions or simply watch.
  • Avoid big bets on tiny, tightly held shares. Insider trading does the most damage in thin, promoter-dominated shares. Spreading your money away from them cuts this whole risk.
  • Record and report. SEBON does act on complaints. If you see a clear pattern — a provably false rumour followed by a collapse — report it. One complaint may not bring a remedy, but it adds to the record a developing regulator needs.
DISCIPLINE OVER CLEVERNESS Your edge in an unequal market will never be better information. It is discipline: refusing trades that need information you do not have, and sizing positions so that being wrong about a rumour never threatens your savings.

Lesson 21.6 — The Broader Cost to Market Development

Insider trading is not only a private risk. It is a tax on NEPSE's growth. Every undetected case strengthens the belief — among small investors and, more importantly, among the long-term domestic and foreign money Nepal wants to attract — that on NEPSE connections matter more than analysis. That belief has real costs. Patient money stays away, because it needs to trust that prices reflect public information. Small investors drift towards short-term, rumour-driven trading, which makes prices swing more and erodes trust further — a vicious circle. And honestly run companies pay more for capital, because investors demand a discount in case insiders trade ahead of them.

Nepal's direction is towards a fairer market: SEBON's planned AI surveillance, talk of access to bank records for investigations, and proposals on SEBON's own conflicts. But the tougher penalties are not confirmed as law, and constant, high-detection enforcement has not yet arrived. The honest conclusion: invest with today's unequal information fully priced into your behaviour, not in the hope of a level field that has not come yet.

Chapter recap

  • The Securities Act, 2063 defines insider information (Section 91), deems directors, employees, shareholders who can get it, professional advisers and their contacts to have access (Section 92), and punishes insider trading with a fine equal to the amount involved, up to one year in prison, or both (Section 101).
  • A 2024 bill proposed fines up to about Rs 30 million, up to three years in prison and SEBON access to bank records. This book could not confirm it became law, so treat the 2063 penalties as the ones in force.
  • Enforcement has come case by case — Ridi Power, Nepal Hydro Developer, Karnali Development Bank — without constant surveillance.
  • Inside knowledge travels through promoter-dominated boards, brokers who see order flow, and tips amplified on Facebook, Viber and Telegram.
  • Warning signs — volume with no news, drift before board meetings, broker-code concentration, oddly exact rumours, quick give-backs — matter most when several appear together.
  • Your defence is behaviour, not information: trade only on published news, size down in thin promoter-heavy shares, do not chase unexplained moves, and watch the corporate calendar.
