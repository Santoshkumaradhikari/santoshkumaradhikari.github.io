Case Study 2 — A Hydropower Plant

Sabina Thapa opened her notebook to a fresh page and, out of habit, wrote the date at the top before crossing it out and writing today's date instead: August 25, 2026. She almost didn't notice the coincidence until she typed the ticker she had chosen for this case study — CHCL, Chilime Hydropower Company Limited — into her broker's terminal and the company profile line read: commissioned August 25, 2003. Twenty-three years to the day. She sat back in her chair in Kathmandu and allowed herself a small smile. It felt like a sign, though she knew better than to let a coincidence do any analytical work for her. In Chapter 83 she had taken the Canon Score — the book's 100-point, seven-dimension framework for scoring a NEPSE-listed company — and applied it start to finish to a commercial bank. That case had taught her to read a balance sheet full of loans and deposits, to weigh capital adequacy against loan growth, to treat thousands of small, similar transactions as the unit of analysis. Now she was staring at something almost the opposite: one river, one plant, one buyer, and a business whose entire personality was shaped by monsoon clouds and glacial snowmelt rather than by interest rate cycles and credit committees.

That contrast is the entire point of this chapter. A hydropower company is not a smaller, simpler version of a bank. It is a different species of business, and the same Canon Score framework has to be applied with different instincts and different sector adjustments — exactly the kind of sector-specific calibration this book asked you to build in Chapter 65, and exactly the kind of humility about the score's limits this book asked you to practice in Chapter 80. Sabina's task in this chapter is to run Chilime Hydropower Company through the full Canon Score, dimension by dimension, using real, current numbers, and to show her working — including the places where a hydropower company genuinely resists being reduced to a single tidy number.

Lesson 84.1 — Setting Up the Case: One River, One Buyer, One Number

Chilime Hydropower Company Limited, traded on the Nepal Stock Exchange under the symbol CHCL, owns and operates a 22.1 megawatt (MW) run-of-river hydropower plant on the Chilime Khola in Rasuwa district, roughly 133 kilometers north of Kathmandu near the Nepal-Tibet border. A megawatt (MW) is a unit of generating capacity — think of it as the maximum rate at which a plant can produce electricity at full throttle, the way a car's horsepower rating describes its maximum output rather than what it actually uses on an ordinary drive. The Chilime plant uses two Pelton turbines (a type of water turbine well suited to Nepal's steep, fast-flowing rivers) and has been generating power since its commissioning on August 25, 2003. It typically produces around 150 gigawatt-hours (GWh) of electricity a year — a gigawatt-hour is simply a thousand megawatt-hours, a measure of energy actually delivered over time, as opposed to MW, which measures capacity at an instant.

Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA), the state-owned electric utility that is effectively the only legal buyer of wholesale electricity in Nepal, owns 51 percent of Chilime Hydropower Company, with the remaining 49 percent held by the public — including a 10 percent slice specifically reserved for local residents of Rasuwa, the district where the plant sits. Chilime sells essentially all of its output to NEA under a long-term Power Purchase Agreement, or PPA — a contract that locks in the price NEA pays per unit of electricity for a period of years, in exchange for the hydropower company committing to deliver its output reliably. Since its original IPO, Chilime has also become an investor in two much larger plants nearby: Rasuwagadhi Hydropower (111 MW) and Sanjen Hydropower (14.8 MW), both of which began commercial generation in 2024 after years of delay, including damage sustained during the 2015 earthquake. This matters for the case study, because it means the listed company Sabina is scoring is not quite a single, isolated asset — it is a legacy plant plus a growing ownership stake in newer capacity, which changes the concentration-risk conversation this chapter will return to.

Chapter 72 drew a hard line between two fundamentally different hydropower asset classes: pre-COD and operating. COD stands for commercial operation date — the day a plant is certified ready and legally allowed to start selling power under its PPA. A pre-COD hydropower company is still building; its risks are construction risk, financing risk, and the risk that the project never reaches COD at all, or reaches it years late and over budget. An operating company like Chilime, twenty-three years past COD, carries almost none of that risk. Its plant is built, tested, and has a two-decade operating history. This is the single most important qualitative fact Sabina had to establish before she touched a single number on the Canon Score.

KEY CONCEPT Pre-COD and operating hydropower are different asset classes, not different points on the same risk scale. A pre-COD project can be wiped out by a landslide during construction or a financing shortfall before a single unit of power is ever sold. An operating project like Chilime has already survived that phase — its remaining risks are about how much water flows through an existing turbine, not whether the turbine gets built at all.

That single distinction reframes everything else. A bank's revenue comes from thousands of individual loans, deposits, and fee-generating relationships, spread across sectors and borrowers — genuinely diversified, but also genuinely uncertain, since any of those loans could sour and interest rates could move against the bank at any time. Chilime's revenue, by contrast, comes from one contractually fixed tariff schedule paid by one counterparty, NEA, for as long as the PPA runs. In a good monsoon year, the plant generates close to its physical maximum and NEA pays a known price for every unit. In a bad year, the water simply isn't there, but the price per unit doesn't change — the risk is in volume, not price. Sabina wrote in her notebook: "A bank's risk is who owes me money and whether they'll pay it back. A hydropower plant's risk is whether the sky and the glaciers cooperate — the buyer isn't going anywhere, and the price is already agreed." That is a much narrower, more predictable form of revenue risk than a bank carries — but it comes bundled with something a bank almost never has: the entire business sitting inside a single physical structure, on a single river, that cannot be moved, duplicated, or hedged if something goes badly wrong with that one location.

Lesson 84.2 — The Qualitative Dimensions: Thin Liquidity, a Physical Moat, and a Government Landlord

Sabina began, as she had with the bank, on the Canon Score's qualitative dimensions: liquidity and market structure, competitive advantage, and governance.

Liquidity and market structure asks how easily an investor can buy or sell a stock without moving its price — measured loosely by average daily volume (ADV, the typical number of shares that change hands per trading day) and free float (the portion of total shares available for ordinary public trading, excluding large, essentially permanent holdings). On the day Sabina pulled CHCL's data, trading volume for the session was just under 19,000 units, and the 52-week trading range sat between roughly Rs 450 and Rs 525 per share against a current price near Rs 478. With NEA holding a fixed 51 percent stake it has no intention of selling, and 10 percent reserved for local Rasuwa shareholders who tend to hold rather than trade, Chilime's genuinely liquid free float is meaningfully smaller than its total share count of roughly 94.8 million shares would suggest. This is a common pattern across Nepali hydropower stocks and one reason this dimension often scores lower for hydropower names than for large commercial banks, which typically have broader, more actively traded shareholder bases.

CAUTION Thin free float cuts both ways. It can make a stock swing further on modest buying or selling pressure than its underlying business would justify — a useful thing to remember before reading too much into a single day's price move on a plant like Chilime.

Competitive advantage, or moat, is the durability of whatever keeps a company's returns from being competed away. For a bank, a moat is built from brand trust, a branch and agent network, and the friction of customers switching institutions. A hydropower plant's moat is almost entirely physical and regulatory: once a company holds a survey license and a generation license for a specific stretch of river, and has signed a PPA with NEA, no competitor can simply build an identical plant next door and take its customers, because there is no "next door" — the river reach itself is the asset, licensed and spoken for. That is a genuinely durable moat, arguably more durable than most bank moats, since it rests on physical geography and government licensing rather than on customer habits that could shift.

But this moat has an unusual wrinkle worth sitting with. Nepal's PPA tariff structure has evolved over the more than two decades since Chilime signed its original agreement. Newer plants commissioned under more recent tariff templates have, at various points, negotiated different seasonal tariff structures and escalation terms than older plants locked into earlier-era agreements. A company's specific PPA vintage — the tariff structure and terms in force when it originally signed — can matter as much as its river location. Sabina found this genuinely tricky to score: Chilime's plant enjoys an excellent, well-understood river with decades of hydrological data behind it, which reduces uncertainty about how much water it can expect in any given year, but its tariff terms reflect an early-2000s agreement rather than the specific escalation clauses newer entrants might negotiate today. A durable location paired with a fixed but aging tariff structure is a different kind of moat than a bank's brand loyalty, and Sabina scored it as strong but not maximal, noting the tariff-vintage question explicitly in her notes rather than pretending it resolved cleanly.

Governance, the third qualitative dimension, asks about the people running the company and the incentives shaping their decisions. Chilime's majority owner is NEA itself — the same state utility that is also its sole customer under the PPA. That is an unusual structure: the buyer and the majority shareholder of the seller are one and the same entity. On one hand, this arguably reduces payment risk, since NEA has every institutional reason to keep its own subsidiary financially healthy. On the other hand, it raises a related-party question the Canon Score's governance dimension is specifically designed to catch: a wholly independent, privately owned hydropower company would negotiate its PPA tariff at arm's length, while a NEA-majority-owned company negotiating with NEA itself has less obvious separation between buyer and seller. Nepali hydropower more broadly has a well-documented history of related-party concerns around engineering, procurement, and construction (EPC) contractors — cases where a plant's construction contract goes to a firm connected to the same promoter group building the project, with cost overruns and schedule delays that show up years later as diluted shareholder returns. Chilime's own subsidiary projects, Rasuwagadhi and Sanjen, both experienced significant delays, including damage from the 2015 earthquake that pushed their commercial operation dates back by years. Sabina treated this construction-delay history as a governance-adjacent yellow flag worth noting, even though both plants eventually reached commercial operation.

WARNING Related-party EPC arrangements are one of the most common sources of value leakage in Nepali hydropower. A construction contract awarded to a promoter-affiliated firm, without competitive tendering, can quietly transfer value away from minority shareholders years before it ever shows up as a headline problem. Always ask who built the plant and who owns the company that built it.

Lesson 84.3 — The Quantitative Dimensions: Generation, Debt Covenants, and a Bond-Like Valuation Problem

With the qualitative work done, Sabina turned to the three quantitative dimensions: fundamental strength, financial risk, and valuation appeal.

Fundamental strength for a bank had meant net interest margin, loan growth, and asset quality. For Chilime, the equivalent exercise starts with actual generation: roughly 150 GWh a year from the original 22.1 MW plant, now supplemented by Chilime's equity stake in the much larger 111 MW Rasuwagadhi plant and the 14.8 MW Sanjen plant, both of which began commercial generation in 2024. That combination meaningfully increases the company's earnings base beyond what the original plant alone could produce, and it is the clearest evidence of management successfully redeploying two decades of retained cash flow into new generating capacity rather than letting it sit idle — a genuinely positive fundamental signal. The flip side, which Sabina flagged for herself, is that generation figures for a run-of-river plant are never fully predictable in any single year; they depend on rainfall, snowmelt timing, and glacial melt patterns on that specific river system, which is a source of year-to-year variance no amount of good management can fully smooth out.

Financial risk asks how much debt sits on top of the business and how much cushion exists before that debt becomes a problem. In project-financed infrastructure like hydropower, the key covenant lenders impose is typically expressed as a Debt Service Coverage Ratio, or DSCR — a ratio of cash available for debt payments in a period divided by the actual debt payments (principal plus interest) due in that period. A DSCR of 1.0 means the plant generates exactly enough cash to cover its debt payments with nothing left over; project lenders typically require a minimum DSCR — often somewhere in the range of 1.2 to 1.3 times — before they'll consider the project comfortably financed, giving a cushion against a bad hydrology year. Chilime's original plant, now more than two decades old, was largely built with an early mix of equity and debt that has long since been repaid or reduced to a small residual balance, giving the parent company itself a relatively clean balance sheet. The newer capacity added through Rasuwagadhi and Sanjen, however, was built using substantial project-level debt more typical of modern Nepali hydropower financing, and it is that debt — sitting partly at the subsidiary level — that carries the DSCR covenants most relevant to the group's financial risk today. Sabina noted this as a case where "the company" and "the plant" are not quite the same unit of analysis anymore, and a careful investor has to look at consolidated leverage across parent and subsidiaries rather than just the parent's historically clean books.

PRACTICAL TOOL When scoring financial risk for any hydropower holding company, check whether debt sits at the parent level or the subsidiary/project level. A parent with a clean balance sheet can still carry meaningful risk through equity stakes in heavily leveraged subsidiaries — the group's consolidated DSCR position is what actually matters to a shareholder.

Valuation appeal is where the hydropower case diverges most sharply from the bank case. Chilime traded around Rs 478 per share against a 52-week range of roughly Rs 450 to Rs 525, on paid-up capital of Rs 100 per share and total listed shares of about 94.8 million. For fiscal year 2081/82 the company proposed an 8 percent bonus share distribution plus a 4 percent cash dividend; in the prior year it had proposed a 12 percent dividend, and in earlier years dividends as high as 15 percent. Chapter 48 introduced the dividend discount model, or DDM — a valuation approach that says a share is worth the present value of all the dividends it will pay an investor over time, discounted back to today because a rupee received later is worth less than a rupee in hand now. Chapter 44 extended this specifically to hydropower stocks, arguing that a plant with a long-term, contractually fixed PPA tariff behaves more like a bond with an equity wrapper than like a typical growth stock, because so much of its future cash flow is genuinely knowable in advance, at least until the PPA term ends or royalty terms shift. Sabina used a simplified version of this approach: she estimated a rough per-share value using Chilime's demonstrated dividend-paying capacity, discounted at a rate reflecting both Nepal's general cost of equity and a premium for hydrology and single-river concentration risk, and cross-checked that against a simple net asset value (NAV) estimate — a company's total assets minus its liabilities, divided by its share count, representing a rough floor value if the company simply stopped growing and distributed its book equity. Both approaches suggested the current price was neither obviously cheap nor obviously expensive relative to the company's demonstrated cash-generating capacity — a far less dramatic conclusion than either a screaming bargain or an overpriced warning, and one Sabina wrote down exactly that plainly rather than dressing it up as more precise than it was.

Lesson 84.4 — Turning Down the Volume on Sentiment

This is the lesson Sabina had been most curious about, because it is where Chapter 65's sector adjustments and Chapter 80's calibration work stop being abstract and become something she had to actually do with a live stock in front of her.

Market sentiment, in the Canon Score's standard weighting, measures how the broader market currently feels about a stock — momentum, recent price trend, and the tone of news and chatter surrounding it. For most companies this dimension carries real information: sustained buying or selling pressure often does reflect something fundamentals-based investors haven't fully priced in yet. But Chapter 80's backtesting work found a specific, recurring problem with applying that same weight to hydropower stocks: their prices systematically rise into and through the monsoon months (roughly June to September), when generation visibly increases and quarterly results look strong, and systematically soften through the dry season (roughly December to March), when generation drops and the same investors who cheered the wet-season numbers grow anxious about the dry-season ones — even though the entire pattern is a predictable, recurring seasonal cycle rather than new information about the company's prospects. A hydropower company's full-year PPA revenue is, to a first approximation, knowable well in advance from historical average flow data and the fixed tariff schedule; the fact that a third of it arrives in July and a smaller share arrives in January is a timing quirk, not a fundamentals shift.

REGULATORY DETAIL Nepal's power purchase tariff structure has historically built the wet-season and dry-season split directly into the price NEA pays per unit, precisely because regulators recognized that run-of-river output swings seasonally. The tariff itself already prices in the seasonality — a stock's price does not need to re-discover it every six months.

Chapter 65's sector-specific adjustment for hydropower addresses this directly: the market sentiment dimension's normal weight (up to 15 points in the standard framework) should be substantially compressed for operating hydropower names, with the freed-up weight redistributed toward fundamental strength and financial risk, since those dimensions carry more genuine forward-looking information for this sector. In Sabina's worked score above, she capped market sentiment at 3 of a possible 15 points for Chilime specifically because the stock's recent price softening toward the bottom of its 52-week range coincided with the onset of the dry season — a pattern Chapter 80's calibration work had already flagged as noise rather than signal for this sector. Had she scored sentiment at full weight using the raw momentum reading, Chilime's total score would have looked meaningfully weaker than the underlying business justified, precisely the false signal Chapter 80 warned the uncalibrated framework was prone to producing.

CASE IN POINT Sabina compared CHCL's price chart against Nepal's monsoon calendar and found the pattern lined up almost exactly with the seasonal story: softer prices approaching and through the dry months, firmer prices through the wet months, repeating year over year. That is the specific, concrete evidence that justified down-weighting sentiment here rather than doing so on a general sector assumption alone.

This lesson mattered to Sabina precisely because it was the opposite lesson from the bank case study. In Chapter 83, sentiment had been a meaningful, largely trustworthy signal, because bank stock momentum tended to track real shifts in credit conditions and rate expectations. Here, the same dimension, scored the same naive way, would have actively misled her. "The framework doesn't change," she wrote, "but which of its dimensions I should actually trust does — and that's a judgment call the framework itself can't make for me. I have to make it, with my eyes open, and write down why."

Lesson 84.5 — The Full Worked Score, and Where It Genuinely Resists Precision

Adding Sabina's dimension scores from the table above — 6 for liquidity, 15 for competitive advantage, 12 for governance, 15 for fundamental strength, 8 for financial risk, 6 for valuation appeal, and 3 for the down-weighted market sentiment dimension — produced a total of 65 out of a possible 100, against a maximum available weight of 100 points once the sector adjustment redistributed sentiment's unused points partly toward fundamental strength and financial risk in the underlying calculation.

She sat with that number longer than she had with the bank's final score in Chapter 83, because several of the inputs behind it were genuinely harder to pin down than anything in the bank case. Three sources of irreducible uncertainty stood out.

The first was the royalty step-up. Nepal's hydropower royalty structure, set under the Electricity Act, charges operating plants both a fixed capacity royalty (a charge per unit of installed capacity, paid regardless of output) and an energy royalty (a percentage of a plant's royalty-determined revenue), and critically, both rates step up substantially once a plant passes fifteen years of commercial operation. Chilime crossed that fifteen-year threshold back in 2018, which means its current royalty burden already reflects the higher post-year-15 rate — a fact embedded in the financial statements Sabina was scoring, though not always obvious unless you know to look for it. Rasuwagadhi and Sanjen, both commissioned in 2024, will not face their own step-up until roughly the late 2030s, meaning the group's blended royalty burden will itself shift over the coming decade as different assets cross that threshold at different times. Predicting exactly how that blended burden evolves years out is not something the Canon Score, or any scoring framework, can do with real precision.

REGULATORY DETAIL Nepal's hydropower royalty schedule imposes materially higher capacity and energy royalty rates once a plant passes its fifteenth year of commercial operation. This is a known, dated event for every operating plant — not a surprise — but its cash-flow impact still has to be modeled explicitly rather than assumed away, and it lands at a different calendar year for every asset in a multi-plant portfolio like Chilime's.

The second source of uncertainty was tariff escalation and PPA renewal terms. While Chilime's current tariff is fixed under its existing PPA, that agreement will not run forever, and the terms of any eventual renewal — including whether escalation clauses keep pace with inflation, or whether NEA negotiates a lower rate given the plant's now fully depreciated construction cost — are simply not knowable today with any precision.

The third, and in Sabina's view the hardest to score honestly, was climate and hydrology risk. Chilime Khola, like many of Nepal's rivers, draws partly on glacial and snowmelt sources whose long-term reliability under a changing climate is a genuinely open scientific question, not a modeling exercise she could resolve from a spreadsheet. A run of unusually dry monsoons, or a shift in glacial melt timing, could reduce generation in ways that no amount of historical flow data fully protects against, precisely because the future may not resemble the past as closely for river hydrology as it does for, say, a bank's loan book behavior.

CAUTION Where Chapter 83's bank case had messy but ultimately quantifiable uncertainty — credit risk that shows up in provisioning data eventually — this hydropower case has genuine physical and regulatory uncertainty that no amount of additional financial disclosure will fully resolve. A score of 65 here carries a wider honest error band than a score of 65 would for a bank.

Sabina resisted the temptation to either round the score up out of admiration for the moat, or round it down out of anxiety about the hydrology risk. She wrote 65 out of 100, and beside it, in her notebook rather than anywhere a spreadsheet would flatten it, she wrote: "This number is honest, but it is less precise than it looks."

Lesson 84.6 — What the Score Actually Implies, and What Would Move It

A score of 65 sits in the moderate-to-solid range of the Canon Score framework — not a conviction buy, not a stock to avoid, but a reasonably well-run, durable-cash-flow business trading at a price that offers no obvious margin of safety and carries real, hard-to-quantify tail risks alongside its considerable strengths. For an investor already holding CHCL, or considering a modest position as part of a diversified portfolio that also holds banks, microfinance institutions, and other sectors, Sabina's honest read was that Chilime looks like a reasonable long-term holding for its dividend consistency and its now-larger generation base, rather than a name to chase at any price or to avoid outright. She was careful, in her own notes, to frame this as a starting point for further research and personal judgment, not a verdict — exactly the humility this book has asked for since its opening chapters.

WARNING A Canon Score of 65 for a hydropower stock and a Canon Score of 65 for a bank do not carry the same texture of risk, even though the number looks identical. Never compare two scores across sectors without also comparing what kind of uncertainty produced them.

What would move this score in the future? Sabina identified three realistic catalysts worth watching. A genuinely bad drought year — an unusually weak monsoon driven by broader regional climate patterns — would hit generation and near-term cash flow directly, and would be the clearest test of whether the market's sentiment-driven price reaction to a bad dry season is proportionate or overblown; Chapter 80's calibration logic predicts the market will likely overreact to such a year even though a single weak monsoon does not change the plant's decades-long average flow profile. A PPA renegotiation or renewal, whenever Chilime's current agreement approaches its end, would resolve one of the three uncertainty sources Sabina flagged in Lesson 84.5, for better or worse, and deserves close attention when it comes. And the royalty step-up dates for Rasuwagadhi and Sanjen, more than a decade away but already calendared under the Electricity Act's fifteen-year rule, are a known, schedulable event that a patient investor can simply mark down and revisit, rather than something that will arrive as a surprise.

PRACTICAL TOOL Build a simple calendar of known, dated events for any hydropower holding: PPA expiry dates, royalty step-up anniversaries (year 15 from each plant's COD), and major loan covenant review dates if disclosed. These are not predictions — they are known future checkpoints worth revisiting the score against, exactly the drift-awareness discipline Part XV asked you to practice.

Sabina closed her notebook feeling that this case study had taught her something Chapter 83 could not have: that the same seven-dimension framework, applied honestly, does not produce comparable confidence across every sector, even when it produces comparable-looking numbers. A score is only as trustworthy as the analyst's honesty about what it cannot see.

Chapter recap

This chapter applied the Canon Score's full seven-dimension framework to Chilime Hydropower Company Limited, a NEPSE-listed, NEA-majority-owned operating hydropower company running a 22.1 MW plant on the Chilime Khola in Rasuwa district since August 2003, now expanded through equity stakes in the larger Rasuwagadhi and Sanjen plants commissioned in 2024. The case demonstrated, concretely rather than abstractly, why Chapter 72's distinction between pre-COD and operating hydropower matters: an operating plant like Chilime carries a level of revenue predictability few banks can match, anchored by a long-term, price-fixed Power Purchase Agreement with NEA, while simultaneously carrying a concentration risk few banks carry, since its entire business sits on one physical river system rather than across thousands of diversified loans.

Working through the qualitative dimensions showed that liquidity and market structure often score lower for hydropower names than for large banks, given thinner free float once majority state ownership and locally reserved shares are set aside; that competitive advantage rests on a genuinely durable but physically and regulatorily bounded moat, complicated by the specific vintage of a company's PPA tariff terms; and that governance carries a distinctive wrinkle when a company's majority owner and its sole customer are the same state entity, alongside the well-documented related-party EPC risk that has affected Nepali hydropower construction broadly, including Chilime's own subsidiaries. The quantitative dimensions showed how fundamental strength should be read through actual generation and expansion data, how financial risk depends on tracing debt and DSCR covenants to wherever leverage actually sits — parent or subsidiary — and how valuation appeal for a fixed-cash-flow asset is better approached through a dividend-discount or NAV-based lens, per Chapters 48 and 44, than through the growth-oriented multiples an investor might reach for with a typical company.

The chapter's central methodological lesson was the explicit down-weighting of the market sentiment dimension for hydropower stocks, applying Chapter 65's sector adjustment and Chapter 80's backtested calibration finding to a live, current example: Chilime's price pattern tracking the monsoon and dry-season cycle almost exactly, in a way that reflects predictable seasonality rather than new fundamental information, and that would mislead an investor who scored sentiment at full weight without that adjustment.

The resulting score, 65 out of 100, was presented with more explicit humility than Chapter 83's bank score, because three sources of hydropower-specific uncertainty — the royalty step-up that already occurred for Chilime's original plant in 2018 and awaits its subsidiaries in the late 2030s, the unknown terms of eventual PPA renewal, and open-ended climate and hydrology risk to glacier-fed river systems — cannot be resolved by better disclosure the way a bank's credit risk eventually resolves through provisioning data. The chapter closed by naming concrete, watchable catalysts — a drought year, a PPA renegotiation, a scheduled royalty step-up — that would plausibly move the score in the future, encouraging the same drift-awareness discipline Part XV built throughout this book's third volume.

Chapter 85 turns to a third and structurally distinct case study: a microfinance institution, or MFI, a lender specializing in small, often collateral-light loans to low-income and rural borrowers who typically sit outside the reach of commercial banks. Readers will find yet another set of sector-specific wrinkles waiting there — interest rate caps unique to microfinance regulation, portfolio-at-risk metrics standing in for the loan-quality measures a commercial bank case study would use, and governance questions specific to an industry built on trust with borrowers who have few other financial options. Sabina Thapa, having now worked a bank and a hydropower plant end to end, will carry the same framework and the same hard-won humility into that third case, continuing this Part's project of showing that mastery of the Canon Score is built one worked case at a time, not memorized once and applied blindly forever after.
