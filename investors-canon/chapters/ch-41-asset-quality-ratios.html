WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE NPL ratio — bad loans as a share of all loans, measured on one day. Provision coverage ratio — money set aside divided by bad loans. CAR — capital adequacy ratio; NRB's 11% minimum includes a 2.5-point conservation buffer. Conservation buffer — extra capital above the minimum; a bank that falls into it faces limits on dividends, like a spare tyre you must not drive on for long. Interest coverage — operating profit divided by interest cost. Cost-to-income ratio — a bank's operating costs divided by its operating income.

A bank earning 22 percent on equity can still be a value trap if its loan book is quietly rotting. A hydropower company with a generous tariff clause can still default on its loan if its interest cover has slipped below one. An insurer can report growing premiums and still be unable to pay a big claim if its solvency has eroded.

None of this shows in the P/E ratio. It shows in a separate family of ratios — loan quality, capital, solvency, debt servicing and efficiency — that most Nepali investors skip because they look like regulatory paperwork. This chapter closes that gap. By the end, you will have every ratio needed to judge a NEPSE company the way a bank's credit officer would, not the way a headline-chasing trader does.

Lesson 41.1 — Asset Quality: The NPL Ratio and Provisioning Coverage Ratio

What counts as a bad loan

Every rupee a bank lends might not come back. Asset-quality ratios measure how much of the loan book has already gone bad, and how well the bank has cushioned itself.

NRB sorts loans into five classes: Pass, Watchlist, Substandard, Doubtful and Loss. A loan becomes non-performing once it falls into Substandard, Doubtful or Loss — broadly, once principal or interest is more than 90 days overdue.

NPL ratio = non-performing loans ÷ total loans and advances

It tells you what share of the bank's lending has stopped earning reliably.

KEY CONCEPT The NPL ratio is a snapshot on one day, not a flow. A bank can report strong quarterly profit and a rising NPL ratio in the same quarter: profit is earned on good loans while the NPL ratio quietly tracks the growing pile of loans that have stopped paying. Never let a strong profit headline replace checking the NPL trend.

How fast it moved

NRB's Key Financial Indicators put the system-wide NPL ratio at about 4.6 percent at Asar-end 2082 (mid-July 2025). By the third quarter of FY 2025/26, NRB's Financial Stability Report showed 5.60 percent — a sharp worsening in a few quarters, partly because a loan-quality review pushed banks to reclassify restructured and rolled-over loans more strictly. An investor watching only P/E would miss it entirely.

A roughly fourteen-fold gap between the best and worst loan books, under the same regulator in the same economy, shows that "it's a bank share" is not an investment case. Which bank matters enormously.

Provision coverage: the second half of the story

A rising NPL ratio is a red flag. Whether it is survivable depends on the provision coverage ratio (PCR):

PCR = loan-loss provisions ÷ gross non-performing loans

NRB requires rising provisions by class — Watchlist 5 percent, Substandard 25, Doubtful 50, Loss 100. A bank with bad loans that is already well provided (PCR near or above 100 percent) has absorbed the pain. A bank with the same NPL ratio but a PCR of 40–50 percent is still sitting on losses that will hit future profit and, eventually, capital. Think of a farmer who has already stored grain for a failed field, against one who has not yet admitted the field failed.

A worked example

A mid-sized bank has gross NPLs of Rs 8.40 arba on total loans of Rs 190 arba (NPL ratio 4.42 percent) and provisions of Rs 5.30 arba.

PCR = 5.30 ÷ 8.40 = 63.1 percent

  • Rs 3.10 arba of bad loans is not yet provided for.
  • If even half must be written off, the bank takes a one-time hit of about Rs 1.55 arba.
  • For a bank with Rs 24 arba of core capital, that is a manageable 6.5 percent hit — uncomfortable, not fatal.
  • With a PCR of 35 percent instead, the unprovided amount rises by about three-quarters, to roughly Rs 5.5 arba.

Run this stress test in your head before buying any bank on its past P/E.

Rupee provision figures can look reassuring simply because a bank is big. NIC Asia's provisions of about Rs 22.32 arba sound huge, but compare them with its own bad loans and total loans before deciding it is well cushioned. Always turn provisions into a ratio before comparing banks of different sizes.

Watchlist loans: the early warning

Below the NPL line sit Watchlist loans — still technically performing, but flagged for warning signs: repeated restructuring requests, stress in sectors such as tourism, property or hotels, or a big borrower breaking a loan condition. A bank can show a steady or improving NPL ratio for two or three quarters while its Watchlist quietly balloons. Watchlist loans are the pipeline for tomorrow's Substandard and Doubtful loans. If Watchlist moves from, say, 2 to 5 percent of loans in one period, treat it as an early warning that the NPL ratio will rise one or two quarters later.

Lesson 41.2 — Capital Adequacy Ratio Recap and Its Role in Valuation Screening

The formula and the minimums

Lesson 29.5 introduced the capital adequacy ratio (CAR). Here we recap it, then use it as a valuation filter, not just a compliance box.

CAR = (Tier 1 capital + Tier 2 capital) ÷ risk-weighted assets

Under NRB's Basel III-based framework, Class A commercial banks need:

  • core equity (CET1) of at least 4.5 percent;
  • Tier 1 capital of at least 6 percent;
  • total capital of at least 8.5 percent;
  • plus a 2.5 percent capital conservation buffer — making the effective minimums 8.5 percent Tier 1 and 11 percent total.

Fall below 11 percent and NRB's dividend rules bite: no cash dividends until the buffer is rebuilt. The buffer is like a spare tyre — using it is not illegal, but you cannot keep driving normally.

REGULATORY DETAIL NRB requires a minimum CAR of 11 percent (including the 2.5 percent conservation buffer) for Class A commercial banks. A bank below it faces limits on cash dividends — so a CAR shortfall threatens not just safety but the dividend yield you may be buying the share for.

The spread between banks

Himalayan Bank's 0.16-point cushion in mid-2025 should stop a dividend-focused investor cold. One bad quarter of loan losses — a live risk with its 7.68 percent NPL ratio — could push it below the floor and freeze dividends, however the profit and loss otherwise looks.

CAR as a valuation screen

CAR headroom limits how fast a bank can grow, and so the P/B multiple it deserves (Lesson 39.3). A bank cannot grow its risk-weighted loans faster than its capital allows without raising new equity (diluting shareholders) or breaching CAR. Roughly:

Sustainable loan growth ≈ (retained earnings ÷ risk-weighted assets) ÷ minimum CAR

A bank with a high ROE but only 0.16 points of cushion cannot compound book value as its ROE suggests; it will face a rights issue, slower lending or a dividend cut. Standard Chartered's 6.82-point cushion, by contrast, lets it grow a lot before needing new capital — one reason foreign-parent banks with high CAR often trade at a premium despite modest growth. The market is paying for growth capacity funded from capital already in hand.

PRACTICAL TOOL Before buying a bank for its dividend, work out: (current CAR − 11%) × risk-weighted assets = the rupee cushion before a dividend freeze becomes a regulatory risk. A bank with a thin cushion (under 1 point) is a "dividend-at-risk" holding, however attractive its past yield looks in a broker app.

CAR also affects a bank's cost of funds. A bank with a comfortable cushion can issue subordinated (Tier 2) debentures more cheaply, because investors and rating agencies see a going-concern cushion. A bank hugging the minimum pays a higher coupon, or cannot issue at all until it rebuilds capital. So over several years CAR and NIM tend to move together: a bank forced to slow lending to protect CAR also slows the growth of the net interest income that ROE depends on.

Lesson 41.3 — Solvency Ratios for Insurance Companies

The insurer's version of capital

Insurers have no NPL ratio or CAR. Their risk is different: claims could exceed the assets set aside to pay them. Their regulator is the Nepal Insurance Authority (formerly the Insurance Board), and the key ratio is:

Solvency ratio = available solvency margin ÷ required solvency margin

  • The required margin is set from the insurer's liabilities (technical reserves, unearned premium, outstanding claims) and its risks.
  • The available margin is admitted assets minus liabilities — in effect, the insurer's true net-worth cushion.

A ratio of 1.0 means exactly the required cushion — no room for error. Read the ratio as a multiple of the requirement, not as a percentage.

The current target

Under the Risk-Based Capital and Solvency Directive (most recently issued in 2083), the target is 1.30 times for both life and non-life insurers, with warning bands below it that can restrict dividends, new business and investments (Chapter 33). Older reports often cite 1.5 for non-life and 1.3 for life under the earlier framework.

An earlier non-life disclosure, from before the latest figures in Chapter 33, put the industry average at 2.67, up from 2.46, with wide differences:

CASE IN POINT Sagarmatha Lumbini Insurance's solvency ratio fell from 4.07 to 2.54 in one period — a drop of 1.53 points, the sharpest in non-life, even though 2.54 was still comfortably above the floor. This is the pattern to watch: the direction of change often matters more than the level, especially after mergers, when combined companies' ratios can swing sharply as reserving methods are aligned.

Read a merged insurer's solvency ratio in its first one or two periods with extra scepticism. Combining two reserve bases, two claims philosophies and two capital structures can move the ratio a full point or more purely from method changes, not from any real change in claims-paying power. Wait for two clean post-merger quarters.

This matters to shareholders, not just policyholders. An insurer close to the target faces the same limits as a thinly capitalised bank: the regulator can restrict dividends or new business, or demand fresh capital — all of which affect your return, however strong premium growth looks.

Lesson 41.4 — Debt-to-Equity and Interest Coverage: Hydropower and Manufacturing

Can the company carry its debt?

Hydropower and manufacturing companies face no CAR or solvency rule, but the same question applies: can they service their debt through a full cycle? Two ratios answer it:

Debt-to-equity (D/E) = total interest-bearing debt ÷ total shareholders' equity
Interest coverage ratio (ICR) = earnings before interest and tax (EBIT) ÷ interest expense

ICR is like asking how many times over a household's monthly income covers its loan instalment. Once it falls below one, the family is paying from savings.

Hydropower: built on debt by design

Nepali hydropower projects are among NEPSE's most borrowed businesses. Run-of-river projects are usually financed 70:30 or even 80:20 debt to equity during construction: developers put in the minimum equity required by the Department of Electricity Development and the lenders, and fund the rest with shared bank loans. That is not a red flag in itself — it is how the sector works — and it is exactly why interest cover, not just D/E, separates a resilient hydropower share from a fragile one.

A worked example

Take "Kalpataru Hydropower Ltd" — a made-up 35 MW run-of-river plant costing Rs 4.80 arba, financed 75:25:

  • Debt: Rs 3.60 arba at an average 10.5 percent → yearly interest about Rs 37.8 crore.
  • Equity: Rs 1.20 arba.
  • D/E = 3.60 ÷ 1.20 = 3.0
  • Once running at a plant load factor of about 45 percent (typical for run-of-river design energy), yearly sales might give EBIT of about Rs 52 crore.
  • ICR = 52 ÷ 37.8 = 1.38

An ICR of 1.38 is thin. One weak dry season, an unplanned outage or a late payment from the NEA can push it below 1.0, when the company is paying its debt from reserves rather than operations.

Later in its 12–15-year loan, as principal falls while revenue holds or rises, ICR typically climbs towards 2.5–3.5. That is why mature, largely debt-free hydropower companies trade at different multiples from newly opened or still-building ones at peak debt: the market is pricing the path of interest cover, not just this year's EPS.

WARNING Nepal's hydropower sector borrows almost entirely at floating rates tied to each bank's base rate. When NRB tightens and base rates rise — as in parts of FY 2078/79 and 2079/80 — every hydropower company's interest cost rises at once, while its PPA revenue (fixed or only slowly rising) does not. Always test a hydropower company's interest cover against a 1.5–2 percentage point rise in lending rates before trusting this year's figure.

Manufacturing: less debt, but watch the cycle

Listed consumer manufacturers — Bottlers Nepal, Unilever Nepal and Nepal Lube Oil among them — usually carry much less debt than hydropower or banks. Their investment cycles are shorter, working capital rather than construction drives most borrowing, and many are owned by multinationals that prefer equity-funded expansion. A D/E of 0.3–0.8 is common, against 2–4 for a hydropower company mid-construction. Capital-heavy manufacturers — cement, steel rerolling, cables — sit in between, often at 1.0–2.0, reflecting real plant financing.

Never compare a hydropower company's D/E directly with a manufacturer's. Compare it with its own PPA revenue schedule and loan repayment curve. Its "right" D/E in year 3 of a 15-year loan should look nothing like its right D/E in year 13.

The same applies to a manufacturer expanding capacity. A cement or cable maker funding a new line may push D/E from about 1.0 towards 2.0 for the two or three years of building and ramp-up, while interest cover dips because repayments start before the new capacity is fully used. That is not automatically bad. The key question, as with hydropower: does interest cover recover to its earlier level within the time management promised, or does a second or third debt-funded expansion arrive before the first has been digested? A manufacturer that piles expansion on expansion without its cover ever climbing back above 2 shows the same warning sign as a hydropower company whose revenue never catches up with its repayments.

Lesson 41.5 — Efficiency Ratios: Cost-to-Income (Banks) and Asset Turnover (Manufacturers)

Loan quality, capital and solvency ratios tell you whether a company can survive stress. Efficiency ratios tell you how well it turns its resources — staff, branches, machines — into revenue in normal times. They separate a well-run company from a merely solvent one.

Cost-to-income (banks)

Cost-to-income ratio = total operating expenses ÷ total operating income

The lower it is, the less the bank spends to earn each rupee — usually a sign of disciplined branch expansion, digital banking and productive staff.

CASE IN POINT In a widely cited comparison from the third quarter of FY 2074/75 (2018), Prime Commercial Bank's cost-to-income ratio was just 19.85 percent — under Rs 20 spent for every Rs 100 earned — against a sector average of 37.31 percent, while Nepal Credit and Commercial Bank was at 58.66 percent. Years later, cost-to-income is still one of the most reliable single screens for efficient versus bloated banks, because unlike NPL or CAR it depends mainly on management discipline, not the credit cycle.

A bank with a high cost-to-income ratio has less operating profit to absorb a provisioning shock in a downturn: it is less efficient in good years and less resilient in bad ones. Screening on ROE alone can fool you with a bank that earns decent ROE today through fast lending while carrying a heavy cost base that will squeeze margins the moment lending slows.

Asset turnover (manufacturers and traders)

Asset turnover = net sales ÷ total assets

It measures rupees of sales per rupee of assets — the manufacturing counterpart of a bank's cost-to-income, and one of the three parts of DuPont ROE (margin × turnover × equity multiplier) from Chapter 40.

  • A made-up drinks bottler with sales of Rs 6.2 arba and assets of Rs 3.1 arba: 6.2 ÷ 3.1 = 2.0.
  • A made-up cement maker with sales of Rs 8.0 arba but assets of Rs 16.0 arba (kilns, quarry, plant): 8.0 ÷ 16.0 = 0.5.

Neither is "better" on its own; the light-asset bottler naturally turns over faster than a heavy cement plant, just as the D/E table predicted. What matters is whether a company's turnover is improving or worsening against its own history and close peers — and whether falling turnover is offset by rising margins (pricing power) or simply feeding a falling ROE.

Efficiency ratios and capital ratios answer different questions and never replace each other. A bank can have an excellent cost-to-income ratio and a dangerously thin CAR at the same time; efficiency does not protect against a capital shortfall. Always read efficiency alongside — never instead of — the loan-quality and capital ratios.

Lesson 41.6 — Building a Cross-Sector Ratio Scorecard

One sheet for every holding

You now have every ratio family in this book: valuation, profitability and per-share measures, and — from this chapter — loan quality, capital, solvency, debt, coverage and efficiency. The final skill is practical: a spreadsheet you actually update every quarter.

Use one row per company and three blocks of columns, so the same sheet works for a bank, an insurer, a hydropower company or a manufacturer:

  1. Universal columns: sector, latest quarter, market price, EPS (last twelve months), P/E, P/B, ROE.
  2. Stability columns, by sector: for banks, NPL ratio, PCR, CAR and headroom above 11 percent; for insurers, solvency ratio and headroom above the 1.30 target; for hydropower and manufacturers, D/E and interest cover.
  3. Efficiency columns, by sector: cost-to-income for banks, asset turnover for manufacturers, combined ratio for insurers.
PRACTICAL TOOL Add one flag column per company with simple colour rules set once: NPL ratio above 5% → amber; CAR headroom under 1 point → red; solvency ratio within 0.3 of the 1.30 target → red; interest cover under 1.5× → red. One glance at the flag column tells you which holdings need a closer look at the quarterly report before you trust the headline EPS or dividend.

It turns four different regulatory systems into one comparable risk view — the reason to build it yourself rather than rely on a broker's one-size-fits-all screener.

Turning flags into a score

For fifteen or twenty holdings, turn the flags into a simple weighted score so you can rank positions, not just colour them:

  • Banks: 35 percent CAR headroom, 35 percent NPL ratio (lower scores higher), 15 percent PCR, 15 percent cost-to-income — capital and loan quality matter about twice as much to survival as efficiency.
  • Insurers: weight solvency headroom above the target most heavily.
  • Hydropower and manufacturers: weight interest cover above D/E, because cover decides whether the debt can actually be serviced this year, not just how big it is.

The weights need not be precise. Setting them once forces you to decide which risk matters most in each sector, instead of re-arguing it every time results arrive.

Where to get the numbers

  • NRB's "Key Financial Indicators of Commercial Banks" and quarterly Financial Stability Report — bank NPL, PCR and CAR;
  • the Nepal Insurance Authority's quarterly solvency disclosures — insurers;
  • company quarterly reports on NEPSE's disclosure portal and investor pages — D/E, interest cover, cost-to-income and asset turnover.

None of this needs a paid data terminal; every figure in this chapter's tables came from free public disclosures.

A scorecard is only as current as its last update. NPL ratios, CAR cushions and solvency ratios can move a lot within one quarter — as the jump from 4.6 to 5.60 percent sector NPL in this chapter shows. Set a reminder for each quarter-end (Asoj-end, Poush-end, Chaitra-end and Asar-end) and update within two weeks of each.

Chapter recap

  • Loan quality, capital, solvency, debt and efficiency ratios reveal risks the P/E ratio cannot.
  • NPL ratio = bad loans ÷ total loans (a snapshot). Sector NPL rose from about 4.6% (mid-2025) to 5.44–5.57% (mid- to late 2026); banks range from about 0.5% (Everest) to about 15% (Prabhu). Check provision coverage (PCR) and the Watchlist trend.
