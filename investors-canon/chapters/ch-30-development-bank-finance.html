WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE Class B development bank and Class C finance company — NRB licence classes below commercial banks (Class A), with narrower permitted activities; a class is a supervisory category, not just a size. Paid-up capital — the money shareholders have actually put in. Forced merger — a merger pushed by NRB's higher capital floors rather than chosen by the banks. Institutional deposit — a large deposit from a company or fund, which can leave quickly. Interbank borrowing — short-term loans between banks, like borrowing sugar from a neighbour until the market opens.

A new NEPSE investor soon notices a puzzle. Muktinath Bikas Bank and Nabil Bank sit a few rows apart in the same brokerage app, both just labelled "bank". Yet development banks have often traded at much higher price-to-earnings (P/E) multiples than commercial banks. In the first quarter of FY 2081/82, even the cheapest development bank traded above 23 times earnings.

Those gaps can then swing sharply as profits recover. Jyoti Bikas Bank's P/E fell to about 15 times by mid-July 2026 after its profit jumped roughly ninefold. And a finance company's discount to book value can last year after year, even when its return on equity looks respectable.

Most investors have no answer beyond "that's just how they trade". This chapter builds the answer from the rules up. In Nepal, a bank's class is not a marketing label. It is a separate legal and supervisory regime under the Banks and Financial Institutions Act (BAFIA) 2073 and NRB's Unified Directives, shaping everything from the capital promoters must raise to the loans a branch manager may write.

Lesson 30.1 — The Four-Class Architecture and Why "B" and "C" Are Not Small Banks

Four licence classes

BAFIA sorts Nepal's banks and financial institutions into four licence classes, all supervised by NRB but under different rulebooks:

  • Class A — commercial banks;
  • Class B — development banks;
  • Class C — finance companies;
  • Class D — microfinance institutions.

The most common mistake investors make here is treating B and C as smaller, cheaper commercial banks. A development bank is not a discount commercial bank, and a finance company is not a shrunken development bank. Each class was built for a different purpose, and NRB enforces that purpose through capital floors, lists of permitted activities and lending targets that still differ after two decades of mergers.

Think of the difference between a city hospital, a district hospital and a health post. All provide health care and all are regulated, but each is licensed for different work.

KEY CONCEPT In Nepal, licence class is a supervisory category, not a size category. A Class B development bank and a small Class A commercial bank can have similar balance sheets, but the development bank faces tighter limits on cross-border business, access to wholesale funding and (historically) where it may operate. Compare institutions within their class before comparing across classes.

Class D microfinance institutions are licensed narrowly for small group lending to poorer households, and are largely outside the general deposit-taking and corporate lending this chapter covers. They have their own chapter (Chapter 31); nothing here applies to them.

Where they could work, and what for

The class difference was once as much about geography as function:

  • National-level development banks could work across the country; regional and district-level ones were limited to a defined area, often a few neighbouring hill or Tarai districts.
  • Finance companies had an even narrower original purpose: hire-purchase, leasing, and consumer and small-business credit, not full-service deposit-taking and corporate lending.

Over the 2010s, NRB relaxed the geographic limits for development banks that met higher capital levels, letting well-capitalised Class B institutions expand nationally and compete with commercial banks in Kathmandu, Pokhara and other cities. But the underlying idea — that B and C institutions serve a different, more local and riskier part of the credit market — still runs through the directives.

One framework, calibrated by class

NRB does not run one set of directives with footnotes for small players. It runs one integrated framework in which capital rules, lending limits, deprived-sector lending quotas, liquidity rules and permitted businesses are each set separately by class. A finance company's limits on lending against property, its restrictions on foreign currency business and its access to interbank and wholesale funding all differ from a commercial bank's — and all of that shows up in its income statement, and especially in how much that income swings over a credit cycle.

Lesson 30.2 — Capital Floors and the History of Forced Consolidation

The 2015 capital jump

No single fact explains today's B and C tier better than NRB's 2015 capital directive. In its Monetary Policy for FY 2072/73 (2015/16), NRB raised minimum paid-up capital sharply:

  • commercial banks: from Rs 2 arba to Rs 8 arba (four times);
  • national-level development banks: from about Rs 64 crore to Rs 2.5 arba;
  • national-level finance companies: from about Rs 30 crore to Rs 80 crore;
  • regional and district-level institutions: lower but still steep multiples.

Governor Chiranjibi Nepal gave the industry until mid-July 2017 — about two years — to comply.

REGULATORY DETAIL The 2015 capital increase (Monetary Policy FY 2072/73) is the event most responsible for the shape of today's B and C tier on NEPSE. Commercial banks: Rs 2 arba to Rs 8 arba. National-level development banks: about Rs 64 crore to Rs 2.5 arba. National-level finance companies: about Rs 30 crore to Rs 80 crore. Read every B or C merger document from 2016 onwards against this deadline.

Merge or disappear

The result was arithmetic before it was strategy. An institution that could not keep enough profit, or get promoters to put in enough new money, within two years had one practical route: merge with another and combine capital, or be absorbed by one that had already cleared the bar. It was like a rule that every shop must have a bigger floor area within two years — small shops on the same street knock down walls and join together.

NRB's own review, "Optimal Number of Banks and Financial Institutions in Nepal" (NRB Research Department), shows the scale:

  • Around 2012: about 32 commercial banks, 88 development banks and 77 finance companies — 197 institutions in these three classes (about 220 with microfinance), extraordinarily fragmented for an economy of Nepal's size.
  • By mid-March 2022: 27 commercial banks, 17 development banks and 17 finance companies.
  • By then, 239 institutions had gone through a merger or acquisition, and 177 licences had been cancelled.

The consolidation is still going on. Every yearly NRB list of licensed institutions is shorter than the last.

Named examples

  • Jyoti Bikas Bank absorbed Jhimruk Bikas Bank and Raptibheri Bikas Bank, and later Hamro Bikas Bank — three development banks in one share.
  • Lumbini Bikas Bank absorbed Vibor Bikas Bank and Society Development Bank, and took in Lumbini Finance & Leasing (a Class C name) in a cross-class merger — a type that became common once NRB encouraged B and C institutions to combine regardless of class.
  • Mahalaxmi Bikas Bank acquired Yeti Development Bank and Malika Bikas Bank, then Mahalaxmi Finance and Siddhartha Finance.
  • Gandaki Bikas Bank absorbed Fewa Bikas Bank; OM Development Bank merged with Manasalu Development Bank.
  • Shine Resunga and Saptakoshi development banks grew through multi-party mergers, turning district-level institutions into national ones.
CASE IN POINT Mahalaxmi Bikas Bank shows a pattern you can read from a company's own disclosures. A Class B name that has absorbed other development banks (Yeti, Malika) and Class C finance companies (Mahalaxmi Finance, Siddhartha Finance) is not a simple, home-grown business. Its loans, deposits and bad-loan profile mix several institutions' old lending standards, some of which public shareholders could not see at the time. Read the merger-swap documents and post-merger due-diligence disclosures, not just the combined accounts, before assuming one credit culture.

Why this still matters

For a shareholder, this history is live due diligence. Every merger joins two loan books, two credit cultures and often two levels of provisioning discipline. A B-class share today may carry, three or four balance sheets deep, loans made by a district institution that never had a national bank's lending systems.

That is why experienced analysts are more sceptical about loan quality at a recently merged development bank or finance company than at a similar-sized commercial bank that grew on its own. A merger does not create bad loans, but it can hide them for a year or two inside a bigger, less transparent combined balance sheet.

Lesson 30.3 — Permitted Activities: What "B" and "C" Class Cannot Do

The capital floor is only the entry ticket. What a B or C institution may do afterwards differs from a commercial bank, and those differences shape its income statement however well it is run. There are five main differences.

1. Foreign exchange and trade finance

Commercial banks handle most of Nepal's foreign exchange, issue letters of credit for importers and exporters, and keep relationships with foreign banks. Development banks have had much more limited authority here; finance companies, essentially none.

This is not a small gap. Trade finance and foreign exchange earn fees without much capital. Their near-absence from B and C income statements is one reason these institutions rely more on net interest income and earn thinner fee income.

2. What deposits they can attract

This is the bigger divide for funding costs. Finance companies especially have narrower authority to take current (cheque) account deposits and to serve as the settlement bank for institutions, government bodies and large companies. Commercial banks compete hard for that business, because current and call deposits are the cheapest money a bank can get. National-level development banks sit in between.

Government treasury money, big company payroll accounts and remittance-linked current accounts have tended to stay with commercial banks, which offer the full range of trade, treasury and cash-management services those customers need.

KEY CONCEPT The cost of deposits depends not only on the rate offered but on which kind of depositor an institution can realistically attract. Commercial banks draw heavily on cheap current and savings deposits from institutions, remittances and company cash management. B and C institutions draw more on fixed deposits and, historically, on placements from other banks — both more expensive. This funding-cost gap often shows up as a narrower or more volatile margin, which may not survive a liquidity squeeze.

3. Limits on lending to one borrower

For many years NRB capped lending to one borrower or borrower group as a share of each institution's core capital. Because the cap scaled with capital, the same rule bit very differently at a commercial bank with Rs 8 arba or more of capital than at a B or C institution near its Rs 2.5 arba or Rs 80 crore floor. A loan that is a rounding error for a big bank can be a large share of a small institution's book — which is why single defaults have hurt smaller institutions' capital so badly.

In October 2025, NRB removed its fixed Rs 25 crore single-borrower ceilings — on share-backed loans and, as reported, on large single loans that had needed its prior approval — and banks now set borrower limits under their own board-approved credit policies. Whether the separate limit tied to each bank's core capital still binds is not settled in the reports; check NRB's current Unified Directives (Chapters 2 and 22) (Chapter 42). That makes concentration more important to check, not less. When a B or C annual report shows its largest borrowers as a share of core capital, treat a high figure as something to investigate.

4. Property and share-backed lending

NRB periodically tightens how much of a lender's book may be secured on property or on listed shares (margin lending). Smaller institutions with concentrated borrowers and thin capital have been more exposed to property and margin-lending booms and busts than diversified commercial banks. A B or C institution with property or margin lending well above the sector average deserves the same scrutiny as one with a large single-borrower exposure — both have preceded past loan-quality collapses (Lesson 30.4).

5. Directed lending

NRB sets deprived-sector and, more recently, priority-sector and productive-sector lending targets for all classes, calibrated differently by class. Development banks, with their roots outside the Kathmandu Valley, have often carried substantial agriculture, cottage-and-small-industry and small hydropower lending.

A development bank with a hill or Tarai footprint may hold a loan book genuinely tilted to agricultural small businesses and small hydropower — more exposed to the monsoon, remittances and crop prices than the urban trade and property loans that dominate many commercial banks. That is a real difference in credit risk, and it means B-class loan quality can move with Nepal's farm and hydropower cycles rather than its urban property cycle.

Lesson 30.4 — Funding Structure: Institutional Deposits, Interbank Reliance, and Liquidity Fragility

Costlier, less loyal money

The funding side is where this tier's weakness concentrates. It explains stress episodes that investors otherwise experience as sudden, unexplained bad news.

Because development banks and finance companies compete weakly for cheap current and savings deposits, they have relied more on two costlier, less loyal sources:

  • high-rate fixed deposits from savers chasing yield;
  • institutional or wholesale deposits from other banks, cooperatives and company treasuries seeking the higher rates small institutions must pay.

These behave very differently in a squeeze. A salaried person rarely moves a salary account for one extra percentage point. An institutional treasury placement is managed for yield and can be withdrawn — or simply not renewed — the moment a better rate appears or the depositor smells risk. It is the difference between a regular household customer and a wholesale buyer who switches shops for a few rupees.

REGULATORY DETAIL NRB rules limit how much any one institution — including another bank — may place with or lend to a single counterparty. That is why a B or C institution's reliance on wholesale and interbank deposits is a concentration risk, not just a cost issue: a few large institutional depositors can be a big share of total deposits. NRB's periodic tightening of interbank and institutional deposit limits has, at times in the last decade, forced sudden repricing or withdrawal of exactly this money at smaller institutions.

The "problematic institutions" episode

This fragility produced the "problematic institutions" episode of the mid-to-late 2010s — the sector's clearest case of funding stress and loan stress feeding each other. NRB classed a group of development banks and finance companies as problematic after they failed to recover much of their lending and their capital and liquidity worsened together. They included:

  • Nepal Share Markets and Finance, Crystal Finance, Kuber Merchant Finance, Capital Merchant Banking and Finance, World Merchant Banking and Finance, Narayani Development Bank, Nepal Finance, Corporate Development Bank and Lalitpur Finance.

To leave the list, each had to rebuild to 25 percent of the new (post-2015) minimum capital, with a further two years to reach full compliance. Some — Corporate Development Bank, Lalitpur Finance and Kuber Merchant Finance among them — slowly improved. Others did not: NRB sought Crystal Finance's liquidation through the courts, and the Supreme Court stayed an NRB capital order concerning Nepal Share Markets and Finance. Even the regulator's repair path was neither quick nor always successful.

WARNING The 2016–2017 problematic-institutions list is not ancient history. It is a template. Each Nepali credit downturn produces new B and C institutions with the same signature: fast loan growth in the boom, heavy exposure to a few large borrowers or one sector (property, margin lending, a single crop or construction niche), and deposits tilted to costly institutional money that vanishes at the first sign of trouble. Look for this signature actively when you screen a development bank or finance company.

Lesson 30.5 — Reading the Financial Statements: What to Adjust For

Because of all this, B and C statements need adjustments that matter less for a commercial bank.

1. Take the margin apart

Split the net interest margin into what is charged and what is paid. A development bank with a higher margin than a comparable commercial bank is not necessarily more efficient. It may simply charge riskier borrowers more while paying more for deposits — an attractive-looking but fragile spread. Check the cost of funds: if it runs well above the commercial-bank average, the margin is payment for risk, not proof of efficiency.

2. Watch liquidity ratios over time

NRB requires every class to keep a cash reserve ratio against deposits and a statutory liquidity ratio in government securities and similar assets, and it caps the credit-to-deposit (CD) ratio so no institution over-lends against its deposits. These rules are the same for every class on paper.

In practice, a B or C institution near the CD ceiling has far less room to keep lending through a deposit slowdown than a commercial bank with the same ratio, because its deposits are less stable. A bad quarter for deposits can force it to shrink lending or scramble for costly short-term money. Watch the CD ratio's trend over several quarters, especially going into the monsoon or a lull in remittances after festivals. A rising CD ratio into a seasonally weak deposit period is an early liquidity warning in this tier.

3. Read the deposit mix

NRB requires every institution to disclose current, savings, call and fixed deposits. A B or C institution with fixed deposits well above 60–65 percent of the total reprices faster when rates move, because fixed deposits reset to market rates when they mature, while current and savings balances do not.

4. Treat merger history as required reading

Find how many predecessor institutions are inside today's entity, when each merger closed, and whether NRB's post-merger due diligence (usually in the merger scheme or the next annual report) flagged old bad-loan or provisioning gaps. One clean annual report two years after a four-way merger tells you less than the fifth report after it.

5. Judge capital against what can be raised

NRB's capital framework uses broadly similar risk weights across classes. But a B or C institution just above the floor has much less room than a commercial bank with the same ratio, because it cannot easily raise new capital through a rights issue or FPO in a market that prices B and C shares at lower multiples (Lesson 30.6).

PRACTICAL TOOL A four-line screen for any B or C share: (1) fixed deposits as a share of total deposits — above about 60–65 percent needs scrutiny; (2) how many institutions were merged into it, and years since the last merger; (3) loan concentration by sector — agriculture, hydropower, property or margin lending — from the sector-wise loan table; (4) capital buffer above the minimum, in percentage points, not pass or fail. A share that scores badly on two or more deserves a valuation discount before you even open the income statement.

Together the two tables make the chapter's central point. The capital jump for commercial banks was large (four times) but survivable through rights and bonus shares within the existing banks. A similar jump (about 2.7 to 3.9 times) on a much larger, thinner-capitalised group of development banks and finance companies wiped out about 80 percent of them through forced mergers. That is why today's B and C tier is made of merger survivors rather than home-grown businesses — and why merger-integration risk belongs permanently on your checklist.

Lesson 30.6 — Why NEPSE Prices This Tier Differently

Four reasons for the discount

Put the rules, funding and merger history together, and the valuation gap between commercial banks and B and C names stops looking like a market mistake. It looks like a mostly rational — sometimes overdone — price for different risk. Four forces explain most of it:

  1. Float and liquidity. Many B and C names have fewer tradable shares and lower daily volume, which earns a liquidity discount on its own — wider still for smaller, recently merged names with little or no research coverage.
  2. Earnings swings. Their funding mix and sector concentration produce more volatile profits over a credit cycle, and markets discount volatile profits more, even when the average looks similar.
  3. Merger overhang. A recent multi-party merger carries a real risk premium for integration and old loan quality that lasts several years.
  4. Growth ceiling. Commercial banks keep the fee-earning trade finance, foreign exchange and large-corporate business that B and C licences largely exclude, capping the smaller classes' opportunity regardless of management quality.
CAUTION A low P/E or P/B on a B or C share compared with commercial banks is not automatically a bargain. It is often a correct price for the funding and concentration risks in this chapter. Before calling a discount "cheap", check that it is not simply paying the market for a fixed-deposit-heavy funding base, an unintegrated recent merger or a concentrated loan book you have not yet examined line by line.

The coverage gap is your opportunity

Brokerage research and news cover the nineteen listed commercial banks far more than the many smaller B and C names, most of which have little or no formal analysis. So retail money, not research-driven money, sets most prices in this tier. That partly explains the wide spread of multiples and the sharper reactions to rumours, dividend announcements and bonus news.

An investor willing to read merger schemes, deposit-mix disclosures and sector concentration tables — which most retail investors skip — has a genuine information edge here, much harder to find among the heavily covered commercial banks.

Differences within the tier

Development bank valuations spread far wider than commercial bank valuations. Some national-level, well-capitalised, cleanly merged development banks trade at multiples that match or beat commercial banks, reflecting real business quality. Others trade at deep discounts reflecting exactly the risks above.

The class tells you the starting rules. It does not tell you which institution has turned them into a durable, well-funded, well-lent business — and which is still digesting three mergers' worth of unexamined credit risk.

PRACTICAL TOOL When two Class B development banks trade at different multiples, do not stop at the multiple. Line up (a) years since the last merger, (b) fixed deposits as a share of funding, (c) sector concentration in the loan book and (d) capital buffer above the minimum. The discounted share often — not always — scores worse on two or three of the four. If it scores the same or better than the premium peer, you may have found a real mispricing rather than a correctly priced risk.

Chapter recap

  • Nepal has four licence classes — A commercial banks, B development banks, C finance companies, D microfinance — each with its own capital floors, permitted activities and lending targets. Class is a supervisory category, not a size.
  • The 2015 capital jump (Monetary Policy FY 2072/73) — commercial banks Rs 2 to 8 arba, development banks about Rs 64 crore to 2.5 arba, finance companies about Rs 30 to 80 crore — forced a merger wave: development banks fell from 88 to 17 and finance companies from 77 to 17 between 2012 and 2022.
  • Today's B and C names are mostly merger survivors (Jyoti, Lumbini, Mahalaxmi and others); read merger documents, not just combined accounts.
  • B and C institutions have little trade finance or foreign exchange business, weaker access to cheap current deposits, and more concentrated, often agriculture- or hydropower-linked loan books. NRB removed its fixed Rs 25 crore single-borrower ceilings in October 2025, so concentration needs even closer checking.
  • Reliance on costly fixed and institutional deposits makes funding fragile; the mid-2010s problematic-institutions list is a template for future stress.
  • Adjust your analysis: split the margin, watch the CD ratio trend, read the deposit mix, study merger history, and judge capital by how easily it can be raised. Use the four-line screen.
  • The valuation discount reflects liquidity, earnings swings, merger overhang and a growth ceiling. It is often correct — but wide dispersion and thin research coverage create real opportunities for careful investors.
