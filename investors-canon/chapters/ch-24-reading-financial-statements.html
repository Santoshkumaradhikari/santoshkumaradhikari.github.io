WORDS USED IN THIS CHAPTER, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE NFRS — Nepal Financial Reporting Standards, the accounting rulebook for listed companies, based on the international IFRS rules. ICAN — the Institute of Chartered Accountants of Nepal, which licenses auditors; its Accounting Standards Board (ASB) issues NFRS. Carve-out — a part of an international standard that Nepal has chosen to change or delay. Regulatory reserve — profit a bank must set aside, and cannot pay as dividend, when NRB's rules are stricter than NFRS. Audit opinion — the auditor's one-paragraph verdict on the accounts; "unqualified" means clean. Quarterly report — the unaudited results published every three months, fast but less checked, like a shop's daily cash count compared with the year-end stock-take.

After a year or two in the market, most Nepali investors discover something uncomfortable. The accounts published by NEPSE companies are not simply "international accounts with a Nepali flag on them". They come from at least three rulebooks laid on top of each other:

  • the Nepal Financial Reporting Standards (NFRS), issued through the Institute of Chartered Accountants of Nepal;
  • the Companies Act, which sets its own disclosure rules;
  • for banks, development banks, finance companies, microfinance institutions and insurers — about two-thirds of NEPSE's market value — a further layer of rules from Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB) and the Nepal Insurance Authority, which can override the accountants.

On top of these, the Securities Board of Nepal (SEBON) sets disclosure rules for anything traded on NEPSE. So the numbers on the first page of an annual report are the result of a negotiation between global accounting logic and local caution.

This chapter comes first because everything later — ratios, valuation, comparing sectors — is only as good as your understanding of what these statements measure. The most common way Nepali investors misjudge a bank or a hydropower company is to treat its accounts like a foreign data screen, instead of learning to read them in their own language first.

Lesson 24.1 — The Architecture of Nepali Financial Reporting: ICAN, NFRS, and the Standards Hierarchy

Who writes the rules

The Institute of Chartered Accountants of Nepal (ICAN) was set up under the Chartered Accountants Act, 1997 (2053 BS). It licenses chartered accountants and regulates auditors. Through its Accounting Standards Board (ASB), it sets the accounting rules Nepali companies must follow.

Nepal's approach is convergence, not copying. Nepal writes its own standards, numbered to match the International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), but keeps the right to change parts that do not suit Nepal, or to start them later. Think of a tailor who uses a foreign pattern but alters it to fit a Nepali customer.

When the current rules started

The current set, NFRS 2018, came in with different start dates:

  • most standards from the fiscal year starting mid-July 2020 (Shrawan 2077);
  • NFRS 9 (financial instruments) and NFRS 15 (revenue), the two most important for banks, a year later, from Shrawan 2078 (July 2021);
  • NFRS 17 (insurance contracts) was delayed more than once. ASB's current table lists 17 July 2024, but listed insurers first published NFRS 17 accounts in FY 2082/83 (from mid-July 2025).
KEY CONCEPT Nepal's rulebook has three tiers. First, the NFRS proper — about seventeen standards matching IFRS 1 to IFRS 17 (business combinations, financial instruments, revenue, leases, insurance). Second, the Nepal Accounting Standards (NAS) — about twenty-four standards matching the older IAS 1 to IAS 41 (presentation, inventories, property and equipment, income taxes, employee benefits, related parties). Third, Interpretations — rulings that settle unclear points. When people in Kathmandu say "NFRS", they usually mean all three tiers together, and so does this book.

Not every company follows the same rules

ICAN sorts companies by how much the public relies on them. "Publicly accountable" entities — listed companies, banks, insurers and others holding other people's money — must use full NFRS. Smaller private companies may use a simpler version, NFRS for SMEs.

Every NEPSE company uses full NFRS, because listing counts as public accountability. But watch the related-party notes: an unlisted sister company owned by the promoters may report under the simpler, looser rules.

Why Nepal runs behind

Because Nepal converges instead of copying, it always lags. The international board changes its standards every year. Nepal's ASB reviews and catches up, but its cycle often runs two to five years behind.

In one area, the gap is deliberate. For how banks measure possible loan losses, an NRB directive replaces pure NFRS with something more cautious. That matters for the roughly forty percent of NEPSE's tradable shares that sit in commercial banks, development banks and finance companies, so we turn to it next.

Lesson 24.2 — Where NFRS Bends: NRB Directives and the Banking Sector's Parallel Rulebook

NRB's own rulebook

NRB, under the Nepal Rastra Bank Act, 2058 and the Bank and Financial Institutions Act (BAFIA), issues Unified Directives to all licensed banks and financial institutions (BFIs): Class A commercial banks, Class B development banks, Class C finance companies and, through separate directives, Class D microfinance institutions.

These directives cover capital, lending limits, governance, liquidity, interest rates and — most important for reading accounts — how loans are classified and how much must be set aside for losses.

NRB does not simply accept NFRS. Nepal's banking system still has old bad-loan problems, thin markets for selling collateral, and young credit bureaus. So where NRB thinks NFRS would understate risk, it adds a stricter rule — and banks must use whichever result is higher. It is like a school where the board exam sets the pass mark, but the headmaster can demand a higher one.

The clearest example: provisions for bad loans

NFRS 9 asks banks to estimate expected credit losses (ECL) — the losses they expect on loans — by sorting loans into three "stages" by how much their risk has grown. NRB's ECL guideline looks similar but uses fixed rules, leaving little room for each bank's own judgement:

  • Stage 1: payments current, or overdue by up to one month.
  • Stage 2: overdue by more than one month and up to three months.
  • Stage 3 (non-performing): overdue by more than three months. A Stage 3 loan cannot move back down until the borrower has stayed regular for a minimum period after catching up.

NRB also limits how banks estimate what they will recover from a bad loan (the "loss given default"):

  1. use the bank's own past recovery record, if reliable;
  2. if not, use the collateral's value minus a set cut — commonly around 25 percent — and ignore collateral that has gone unsold for many years (commonly five);
  3. if not, use a floor loss rate, commonly around 45 percent, approved by the board.
REGULATORY DETAIL NRB revises its NFRS 9 / ECL guideline from time to time. The day-count cut-offs, the collateral cut, the waiting period before a Stage 3 loan can be upgraded and the floor loss rate are exactly the details that change. Treat the numbers here as examples of NRB's rule-based approach, and check each bank's accounting-policies note, which must state the basis it actually used that year.

The regulatory reserve

The tension between NFRS and NRB shows up in one line every bank investor must learn: the regulatory reserve.

When NRB's required provision is higher than NFRS 9's, the bank must book the higher amount as an expense. That lowers profit, as it should.

The regulatory reserve handles the other direction. Some NFRS items raise profit without bringing in real cash — fair-value gains, actuarial gains on staff pensions, deferred tax assets and similar paper items. NRB does not want these paid out as dividends. So the bank must move an equal amount out of retained earnings into a reserve that cannot be distributed.

The result: a bank's reported profit and its distributable profit are two different numbers. The gap, shown in the statement of changes in equity, tells you how much of the profit is accounting rather than spendable cash.

WATCH FOR Two numbers that look alike but mean different things: "profit for the year" (the NFRS figure after tax) and "distributable profit" or "free profit" (what is left for dividends after the regulatory reserve transfer and the general reserve — commonly about 20 percent of profit each year for BFIs, until the reserve reaches a set multiple of paid-up capital). A bank can show strong profit growth while its distributable profit — and so its ability to keep paying the dividend investors expect — grows slowly or not at all.

Two more Nepali features

Interest suspense. Once a loan is classed as non-performing, interest due on it is not counted as income, even if NFRS logic might allow some. It is parked in an "interest suspense" account until the borrower actually pays — like a shopkeeper who writes a doubtful customer's dues in a separate notebook and counts them only when the cash arrives.

Staff bonus. Under the Bonus Act, 2030, Nepali companies, banks included, must set aside a share of profit for staff bonus — commonly about 10 percent of profit before bonus and tax. It is an expense before profit before tax, which matters when comparing a Nepali bank's costs with a foreign bank's.

NRB sets the format too

NRB fixes not only how banks measure but how they present. Banks must use a standard format set by NRB circular, with fixed line items, order and minimum disclosures — including capital adequacy, bad-loan ratios and liquidity ratios. That is why every Nepali commercial bank's annual report looks the same, while two hydropower companies' reports often do not. NFRS governs the measuring; NRB governs the template.

Lesson 24.3 — SEBON, the Companies Act, and the Disclosure Regime for Listed Issuers

SEBON's job

NRB reaches into how banks measure. SEBON, the market regulator, works on something else: disclosure, timing and investor protection, under the Securities Act, 2063, its regulations and the Corporate Governance Directive for listed companies.

SEBON does not write accounting rules. It makes sure listed companies follow NFRS, use auditors licensed by ICAN, and publish their results through NEPSE on time.

Three kinds of disclosure

  1. Quarterly: unaudited results — balance sheet, profit and loss, and set ratios — within a fixed window after each quarter, commonly cited as thirty days, through NEPSE's disclosure portal.
  2. Annual: the audited annual report, with the auditor's report, notes and the directors' report required by the Companies Act, 2063, and an Annual General Meeting (AGM), normally within six months of the year-end in mid-July. This deadline is often extended with permission, especially for large financial institutions.
  3. Material events: price-sensitive news reported at once — dividend or bonus proposals, mergers, rating changes, large related-party deals, big lawsuits, and changes of chief executive or finance chief.
CASE IN POINT How a bank's dividend reaches the market: the board proposes a dividend (cash, bonus shares or both) from distributable profit, after the regulatory and statutory reserves. This is price-sensitive news and often moves the share at once. But the proposal then needs NRB's approval, because NRB checks whether the bank's capital, bad loans and other measures can support it. Only after NRB approves and shareholders agree at the AGM is it paid. An investor who treats the board's proposal as certain, without allowing for NRB cutting it, misprices the share in the weeks between proposal and approval.

Extra governance rules

SEBON's Corporate Governance Directive adds more to the annual report: promoter and public shareholdings, related-party deals, whether board members are independent, the audit committee's work, risk committee reports (for banks, alongside NRB's own rules) and, increasingly though unevenly, environmental and social practice.

Banks must change their statutory auditor after at most three consecutive appointments. SEBON requires auditors of listed companies to meet ICAN's eligibility rules, and it can order a special audit when it doubts a company's accounts — a power it has used, including on banks where bad loans or related-party lending raised concern.

WARNING SEBON's timetable enforces speed, not depth. A late company is fined; a company that files the bare minimum on time, with thin notes, often escapes notice because the market has moved on to the next quarter. "Filed on time with NEPSE" does not mean "fully meets NFRS". They are different tests, by different bodies, on different timetables.

Lesson 24.4 — Anatomy of the Annual Report: What's Really in a NEPSE Filing

A NEPSE annual report is not one document. It has at least six parts, each written for a different reader. If you read only the financial statements, you throw away about half the useful information.

1. The directors' report

Required by the Companies Act, this is management's story of the year: highlights, the business environment, financial results, the dividend proposal and legally required items such as the number of board meetings and directors' pay.

It contains real information that never appears in the accounts — for a bank, branch expansion and deposit strategy; for a hydropower company, how much it generated compared with its design capacity; for a factory, how much of its capacity it used. But it is also the part most shaped by public relations. Weigh its claims against the hard numbers; never replace them with it.

2. The auditor's report

The auditor must give a clear opinion:

  • unqualified (clean);
  • qualified (fair, except for a named problem);
  • adverse (not fair);
  • disclaimer (the auditor could not form an opinion).

For listed companies, the auditor also lists Key Audit Matters — the areas that needed the most judgement. For a bank these usually include loan-loss estimates, the value of property taken over from defaulters, and interest income that depends on IT systems.

A qualification, an "emphasis of matter" paragraph, or a Key Audit Matter about uncertain loan-loss estimates is not boilerplate. It is the auditor telling you, formally, where the numbers rest on judgement rather than fact.

PRACTICAL TOOL Before reading a single ratio, run this five-point check on any NEPSE annual report. One: read the audit opinion in full — clean, or with a qualification, emphasis of matter or disclaimer? Two: scan the Key Audit Matters for impairment, valuation or related parties. Three: for banks, find the regulatory reserve movement in the statement of changes in equity and work out the gap between reported and distributable profit. Four: read the related-party note in full and check each counterparty against the promoter shareholding list — hidden links between "independent" parties are the most common route for profit manipulation on NEPSE. Five: compare this year's accounting-policies note with last year's, word by word if needed, for any quiet change in method (depreciation, the loan-loss model's economic assumptions, pension assumptions) that the directors' report did not highlight.

3. The financial statements

Four statements follow the auditor's report, each compared with last year: the balance sheet (statement of financial position), the profit and loss with other comprehensive income, the statement of changes in equity, and the cash flow statement. Companies with subsidiaries show both standalone and group (consolidated) figures.

For a bank, this matters: subsidiaries often include a merchant banking arm or an insurance or capital-market business with different risks. Valuing "the bank" on group figures means also taking a view on those subsidiaries.

4. The notes

The notes are where NFRS demands the most, and where careful reading pays most:

  • Accounting policies — the choices the company made within NFRS, such as its loan-loss model or how it recognises revenue from a power purchase agreement under NFRS 15.
  • Segment reporting — results by business line, important for groups with several businesses.
  • Financial risk disclosures (NFRS 7) — credit, liquidity, market and interest-rate risk. For a bank, the maturity-gap and interest-sensitivity tables here really matter.
  • Related parties — loans to, deposits from and deals with directors, key managers and companies under the same promoters.
  • Commitments and contingent liabilities — vital for hydropower and infrastructure companies mid-construction, and for banks in the form of letters of credit and guarantees.
  • Capital adequacy (BFIs) — core (Tier 1) and supplementary (Tier 2) capital against risk-weighted assets, under NRB's Basel-based framework.

5–6. Governance and shareholding

The last substantive section, driven by SEBON's directive, covers board make-up and independence, committees, promoter and public shareholdings and the top shareholders. For many NEPSE companies, the top-shareholder list is the quickest way to identify the few related parties whose deals the notes showed only in total.

Lesson 24.5 — Quarterly Reports: Speed Over Depth, and Where the Gaps Hide

What a quarterly report gives you

Most active NEPSE investors follow companies through quarterly reports, because they come four times a year and, for banks, in the same format across the sector. But a quarterly report is lighter than an annual report, and you need to know what was given up for speed.

For banks and financial institutions, the quarterly template shows at least:

  • a short balance sheet and profit and loss, compared with the same quarter last year and year-to-date;
  • a standard set of ratios: capital adequacy, bad-loan (NPL) ratio, earnings per share (annualised), P/E at the current market price, net worth (book value) per share, liquidity measures, and the interest spread between average lending and deposit rates.

For most retail investors, that ratio panel is the quarterly report. It is useful because it is the same for every bank. But it is unaudited, prepared in a hurry, and has none of the notes, related-party detail or auditor checking of the annual report.

CAUTION A bank's unaudited quarterly figures are management's own classification of loans and provisions — under NRB rules, but without the auditor's testing. It is not unusual, and not by itself a warning, for the year-end figures to show a jump in provisions or bad loans, or a change in profit, once the audit, asset-quality reviews and NRB's own inspection findings are in. Treat quarterly trends as direction, but save final judgement on loan quality for the audited annual figures. Be alert when the audited fourth quarter (the full year minus the first three quarters) differs sharply from what the first three quarters suggested.

Other sectors

Factories, hydropower companies, hotels and traders file quarterly reports in a less fixed format, though SEBON's minimum rules still apply. Their key gaps are related-party deals, contingent liabilities and project-level detail.

A hydropower company's quarterly report shows revenue and profit, but not the notes on its tariff, its "take-or-pay" terms with the buyer, or the status of insurance claims after a flood or landslide. Those appear only in the annual report, if at all.

Lesson 24.6 — The Convergence Gap: Where Nepal Still Diverges from Full IFRS, and Why It Matters to You

Because NFRS looks so much like IFRS, it is tempting to treat Nepali accounts like a foreign company's and apply foreign benchmarks — price-to-book for banks, EV/EBITDA for industrials — without adjusting. That is a mistake. The gap shows up in four places.

1. The time lag

Nepal converges to a fixed version of IFRS, not the constantly updated one. So Nepali accounts always reflect an IFRS that is already several years old. Comparing a Nepali bank's NFRS 9 notes with a regional bank using the latest IFRS 9 amendments, expect differences in structure, not just numbers.

2. The NRB overlay

This is Nepal's biggest departure. Many countries put some prudential filter on IFRS 9 results. But NRB's approach — routing the gap through a named regulatory reserve, and using fixed day-count rules that leave little room for a bank's own models — is a Nepali answer to a Nepali problem: credit-bureau data, collateral markets and loss histories are not yet deep enough for banks' own models to be trusted.

3. Disclosure in practice

What reports actually show still lags what the rules require — a gap Nepal's accounting profession itself acknowledges in ICAN quality reviews and international assessments. Fair-value detail for hard-to-value instruments, sensitivity analysis for pension and market assumptions, and full movement tables for loan-loss stages appear with varying completeness. A note heading does not guarantee a full note.

4. New and difficult standards

Some standards have little Nepali precedent. NFRS 17 was put into practice by insurers only from FY 2082/83, so multi-year comparisons across that change are still being built. Fair values of thinly traded unlisted investments — held by many Nepali companies, including banks — rely on judgement-heavy inputs in a market as thin as Nepal's.

None of this is a reason to distrust NFRS accounts. ICAN's audit and standard-setting system is serious and working, and most NEPSE companies — especially the commercial banks and larger development banks under close NRB and SEBON watch — produce broadly reliable accounts within these limits.

It is a reason to read every Nepali statement with a mental checklist of where local practice differs from "IFRS is IFRS everywhere", and to give as much weight to the qualitative signs — audit wording, Key Audit Matters, regulatory reserve movements and consistent accounting policies — as to the headline ratios. Chapter recap

  • NEPSE accounts come from layered rulebooks: NFRS (through ICAN's Accounting Standards Board), the Companies Act, NRB and Insurance Authority rules for financial companies, and SEBON's disclosure rules.
  • Nepal converges with IFRS rather than copying it, so it runs several years behind and keeps local changes. NFRS 2018 started in July 2020; NFRS 9 and 15 in July 2021; insurers began NFRS 17 reporting in FY 2082/83.
  • For banks, NRB's stricter rules win: they must use the higher loan-loss provision, and paper gains go to a regulatory reserve that cannot be paid out. So reported profit and distributable profit differ.
  • Other Nepali features: interest suspense on bad loans, a statutory staff bonus of about 10% of profit, and an NRB-fixed format for bank accounts.
  • SEBON enforces quarterly, annual and material-event disclosure — speed, not depth. A bank's proposed dividend still needs NRB approval.
  • An annual report has several parts. Read the audit opinion, Key Audit Matters, regulatory reserve, related-party note and accounting-policy changes before any ratio.
  • Quarterly reports are unaudited. Use them for direction, and judge loan quality on audited figures.
  • NFRS accounts are broadly reliable, but read them knowing where Nepal differs from international practice.
